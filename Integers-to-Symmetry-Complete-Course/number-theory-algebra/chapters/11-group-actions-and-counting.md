# Chapter 11 — Group actions, orbit-stabilizer and counting

A square has four corners, but a square necklace with four colored beads does not have a distinguished first bead. Turning the necklace changes the written sequence while preserving the physical arrangement. This creates a counting problem: when should two descriptions represent the same object? It also creates an algebra problem: which symmetries move one description to another? A group action answers both questions with one definition.

This chapter builds on groups, subgroups, cosets, homomorphisms and permutations. We will begin with concrete movements, develop the orbit-stabilizer theorem from a bijection, and then count arrangements without dividing blindly by the number of symmetries. The final sections turn the group on itself. That apparently playful move produces centralizers, conjugacy classes and the class equation, which will drive the existence theorems of Chapter 12.

## 11.1 The difference between a symmetry and the thing it moves

Let the four corners of a square be numbered $0,1,2,3$ clockwise. Let $r$ mean clockwise rotation through one quarter turn. The instruction $r$ is not a corner. It is an operation on corners: $r\cdot0=1$, $r\cdot1=2$, and eventually $r\cdot3=0$. The rotations form a group $C_4=\{e,r,r^2,r^3\}$. The corners form a set $X=\{0,1,2,3\}$. Keeping these two roles separate is essential. A group supplies instructions; a set supplies objects that receive instructions.

We may apply the same group to a different set. For example, let $Y$ be the two diagonals of the square. A quarter turn exchanges the diagonals, while a half turn fixes each diagonal. Thus the group has four elements, but its visible effect on this particular set has only two possibilities. Nothing is wrong: different instructions can produce the same observable action. Later we will describe this using a kernel.

The full symmetry group of the square also contains four reflections. We call it $D_8$, with the subscript indicating its order, eight. Choose a reflection $s$ fixing corners $0$ and $2$. Then $srs=r^{-1}$. This identity has a concrete meaning: reflecting, rotating, and reflecting back reverses the direction of rotation. The same algebraic identity controls the action on corners, diagonals, edges and colorings, although the resulting orbits can differ.

An analogy can help: a group is a collection of reversible commands, and an action is a way to interpret those commands on some data. The analogy stops where reversibility stops. A command such as deleting a bead is not part of a group action on fixed necklaces, because it cannot always be reversed and does not preserve the underlying set of positions.

## 11.2 Defining an action without hiding the consistency conditions

A **left action** of a group $G$ on a set $X$ is a rule assigning an element $g\cdot x\in X$ to every $g\in G$ and $x\in X$, subject to two requirements:

$$
e\cdot x=x,\qquad (gh)\cdot x=g\cdot(h\cdot x).
$$

The first says that doing nothing changes nothing. The second says that the product of two commands must mean their successive application. Because we compose functions from right to left, $h$ acts first in the second formula. An action is not merely a convenient table of movements: the table must respect the multiplication already present in $G$.

Every map $x\mapsto g\cdot x$ is a bijection. To prove this, apply $g^{-1}$ afterward. We obtain $g^{-1}\cdot(g\cdot x)=e\cdot x=x$, and reversing the order gives the other inverse identity. Thus an action determines a homomorphism

$$
\rho:G\longrightarrow\operatorname{Sym}(X),\qquad \rho(g)(x)=g\cdot x.
$$

Here $\operatorname{Sym}(X)$ is the group of bijections from $X$ to itself. Conversely, every such homomorphism defines an action by the same formula. This equivalence explains why actions and permutation representations are two languages for the same idea.

The action is **faithful** if this homomorphism is injective. Equivalently, the only group element fixing every point is $e$. The rotation action on corners is faithful; the rotation action on diagonals is not, because $r^2$ fixes both diagonals. The action is **transitive** if any point can be moved to any other point. Faithfulness and transitivity are different properties. The diagonal action is transitive but not faithful. An action of $C_2$ that swaps two points and fixes a third is faithful but not transitive.

A useful nonexample is the proposed rule $g\cdot x=g+x$ with $G$ a multiplicative group of nonzero real numbers and $X=\mathbb R$. Its identity is $1$, but $1\cdot x=1+x\ne x$. The formula uses the wrong operation. A group action must be checked against the specific group law, not against an informal idea of combining symbols.

## 11.3 Orbits are equivalence classes generated by movement

For $x\in X$, the **orbit** of $x$ is

$$
G\cdot x=\{g\cdot x:g\in G\}.
$$

It is the collection of objects obtainable from $x$ by the allowed symmetries. Define $x\sim y$ when $y=g\cdot x$ for some $g$. This is an equivalence relation. Reflexivity uses $e$. Symmetry uses $g^{-1}$. For transitivity, if $y=g\cdot x$ and $z=h\cdot y$, then $z=(hg)\cdot x$. Its equivalence classes are exactly the orbits. Therefore the orbits partition $X$.

This statement has practical force. Two orbits cannot overlap partially. If one description lies in both, the inverse and composition rules show that every description in either orbit lies in the other. We may therefore count the entire set by adding the sizes of its disjoint orbits.

For the action of square rotations on corners, there is one orbit of size four. For the action on unordered pairs of corners, there are two orbits: the four adjacent pairs and the two opposite pairs. Rotation preserves the distinction between an edge and a diagonal. Listing all six pairs and grouping them makes the abstraction visible before any theorem is applied.

Orbits also appear in arithmetic. The unit group $U(n)=(\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z)^\times$ acts on residue classes by multiplication: $u\cdot[a]=[ua]$. Multiplication by a unit is reversible, so this really is an action. Modulo $12$, the orbit of $[1]$ is $\{[1],[5],[7],[11]\}$, whereas the orbit of $[2]$ is $\{[2],[10]\}$. The zero class is an orbit by itself. The greatest common divisor of a representative with $12$ remains unchanged under multiplication by a unit. This gives an arithmetic invariant that helps separate orbits.

An invariant need not classify orbits completely in an arbitrary action. It gives a necessary condition for two points to lie together. To prove that it is a complete classification, one must also prove that any two points with the same invariant can be connected. Confusing these two tasks is a common source of incomplete arguments.

## 11.4 Stabilizers record the movements that remain invisible

The **stabilizer** of $x$ is

$$
G_x=\{g\in G:g\cdot x=x\}.
$$

It is a subgroup. The identity fixes $x$. If $g$ and $h$ fix $x$, then $(gh)\cdot x=g\cdot(h\cdot x)=x$. If $g$ fixes $x$, applying $g^{-1}$ to $g\cdot x=x$ shows that $g^{-1}$ fixes $x$. These are exactly the subgroup conditions.

Consider a corner of the square under $D_8$. The identity and the reflection through that corner and the opposite corner fix it. No other symmetry does. Its stabilizer therefore has order two. A diagonal, considered as an unordered pair of opposite corners, has a stabilizer of order four. The difference predicts orbit sizes: a diagonal is harder to move into many distinct positions because more symmetries leave it unchanged.

The **kernel of an action** consists of elements fixing every point:

$$
\ker\rho=\bigcap_{x\in X}G_x.
$$

A stabilizer fixes one chosen point. The kernel fixes all points. Stabilizers generally are not normal subgroups, whereas the kernel always is normal because it is the kernel of a homomorphism. For example, the stabilizers of different square corners are different reflection subgroups. Conjugating one by a quarter turn moves it to the stabilizer of the next corner.

More generally, if $y=g\cdot x$, then

$$
G_y=gG_xg^{-1}.
$$

Indeed, $h$ fixes $y$ precisely when $h\cdot(g\cdot x)=g\cdot x$. Apply $g^{-1}$ and use the action law: this is equivalent to $(g^{-1}hg)\cdot x=x$. Hence stabilizers along one orbit are conjugate and have equal sizes. This is a structural statement, not just a counting coincidence.

## 11.5 Orbit-stabilizer: the missing bijection

Suppose $G$ is finite. The **orbit-stabilizer theorem** states

$$
|G\cdot x|=[G:G_x]=\frac{|G|}{|G_x|}.
$$

The central point is a bijection between the orbit and the left cosets of the stabilizer. Define

$$
\Phi:G/G_x\longrightarrow G\cdot x,\qquad gG_x\longmapsto g\cdot x.
$$

Here $G/G_x$ denotes the set of left cosets; no quotient group is being asserted, so $G_x$ need not be normal. We must establish that the rule is independent of the coset representative. If $gG_x=hG_x$, then $h^{-1}g\in G_x$, so $(h^{-1}g)\cdot x=x$. Applying $h$ gives $g\cdot x=h\cdot x$. Thus the rule is well defined.

It is surjective by the definition of an orbit. It is injective because if $g\cdot x=h\cdot x$, the same calculation reversed gives $h^{-1}g\in G_x$, and therefore $gG_x=hG_x$. Lagrange's theorem now counts the cosets, completing the proof.

One can read the proof as a diagnosis of overcounting. The map $G\to G\cdot x$, given by $g\mapsto g\cdot x$, usually identifies several group elements. Those producing the same destination form one coset of $G_x$, and each coset has exactly $|G_x|$ elements. We divide by this precise multiplicity, not by an arbitrary symmetry number.

For square corners, $8/2=4$. For diagonals, $8/4=2$. In the arithmetic action of $U(12)$ on $[2]$, the stabilizer consists of $[1]$ and $[7]$, since $2u\equiv2\pmod{12}$ is equivalent to $u\equiv1\pmod6$. Thus its orbit has size $4/2=2$, agreeing with the direct list.

A consequence worth keeping is that every orbit size divides $|G|$ when the group is finite. This does not mean every divisor occurs as an orbit size in every action. The action determines which stabilizers actually appear. Nor does it mean the whole set $X$ has size dividing $|G|$: several orbits may be added together.

## 11.6 Coset actions turn subgroups into permutations

If $H\le G$, let $X$ be the set of left cosets $G/H$. Define $g\cdot(aH)=(ga)H$. If $aH=bH$, then $a=bh$ for some $h\in H$, and $(ga)H=(gbh)H=(gb)H$, so the action is well defined. The action is transitive because $ba^{-1}$ moves $aH$ to $bH$.

The stabilizer of the coset $H$ is precisely $H$. The stabilizer of $aH$ is $aHa^{-1}$. The kernel is therefore

$$
\bigcap_{a\in G}aHa^{-1},
$$

the largest normal subgroup of $G$ contained in $H$. To justify the word largest, any normal subgroup $N\subseteq H$ satisfies $N=aNa^{-1}\subseteq aHa^{-1}$ for every $a$. Hence $N$ lies in the intersection.

When $[G:H]=m$ is finite, this action gives a homomorphism $G\to S_m$. The first isomorphism theorem then implies that $G$ modulo the kernel is isomorphic to a subgroup of $S_m$. Thus a subgroup of small index forces a large portion of $G$ to have a small permutation description.

For instance, a subgroup of index two is normal. The action gives a homomorphism into $S_2$, and transitivity ensures its image has order two. Its kernel also has index two and lies inside $H$. Since both have the same finite index and the kernel is contained in $H$, they coincide. There is also a direct coset proof from Chapter 8; the action proof reveals why two cosets are especially restrictive.

There is an action using the group itself with no subgroup chosen: left multiplication on $G$. It is faithful, because an element fixing $e$ must be $e$, and transitive. This gives **Cayley's theorem**: every group is isomorphic to a group of permutations of a set. For finite $G$ of order $n$, the containing permutation group can be $S_n$. The theorem says that the abstract axioms never describe objects more mysterious than reversible rearrangements, although the resulting permutation representation may be much larger than necessary.

## 11.7 Counting objects up to symmetry

Let $G$ act on a finite set $X$. We want the number of orbits, written $|X/G|$. If every stabilizer is trivial, every orbit has size $|G|$, so the answer is $|X|/|G|$. Such an action is called **free**. But necklace actions are usually not free: a solid-colored necklace remains unchanged under every rotation, while an irregular coloring may remain unchanged only under the identity.

For $g\in G$, define its fixed-point set by

$$
\operatorname{Fix}(g)=\{x\in X:g\cdot x=x\}.
$$

**Burnside's counting lemma** states

$$
|X/G|=\frac1{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}|\operatorname{Fix}(g)|.
$$

The right side is the average number of objects fixed by a symmetry. This average is always an integer, but that is a conclusion of the theorem, not something transparent from the expression alone.

To prove it, count the set $T=\{(g,x):g\cdot x=x\}$ in two ways. Fixing $g$ first gives $|T|=\sum_g|\operatorname{Fix}(g)|$. Fixing $x$ first gives $|T|=\sum_x|G_x|$. Now group the latter sum by orbits. Along one orbit $O$, all stabilizers have size $|G|/|O|$, by orbit-stabilizer. Consequently

$$
\sum_{x\in O}|G_x|=|O|\frac{|G|}{|O|}=|G|.
$$

Every orbit contributes exactly $|G|$, so $|T|=|G||X/G|$. Dividing proves the formula. The key idea is not an unexplained averaging trick. It is that every orbit contributes the same total number of fixed pairs, even though individual stabilizers vary.

## 11.8 A complete necklace calculation

Color four numbered positions using two colors, black and white, and regard rotations as equivalent. There are $2^4=16$ labeled colorings. The group is $C_4$. We count fixed colorings for each rotation.

The identity fixes all $16$. A quarter turn forces every position to have the same color: position $0$ must match $1$, which must match $2$, and so on. It therefore fixes exactly two colorings. A three-quarter turn has the same condition and also fixes two. A half turn pairs positions $0,2$ and positions $1,3$. Each pair can independently be black or white, so it fixes $2^2=4$. Burnside gives

$$
\frac{16+2+4+2}{4}=6.
$$

Dividing $16$ by $4$ would give four, which is wrong because the orbits have different sizes. We can inspect the six types: zero black beads, one black bead, two adjacent black beads, two opposite black beads, three black beads, and four black beads. The orbit sizes are respectively $1,4,4,2,4,1$, which sum to $16$. This independent check explains the count rather than merely repeating it.

With $k$ available colors, the same cycle argument gives

$$
\frac{k^4+2k+k^2}{4}.
$$

Each cycle of a permutation must receive a constant color for the coloring to remain fixed. If a permutation of positions has $c$ disjoint cycles, it fixes $k^c$ colorings. The colors themselves are labeled; exchanging black and white is not an allowed symmetry unless we explicitly enlarge the acting group.

If reflections are also allowed, we use $D_8$. For four positions and $k$ colors, two reflections through opposite corners have three position cycles, and two reflections through opposite edge midpoints have two. The count becomes

$$
\frac{k^4+2k+k^2+2k^3+2k^2}{8}
=\frac{k^4+2k^3+3k^2+2k}{8}.
$$

For $k=2$ this is again six. Equality of the two answers at two colors is a special feature of this small example, not permission to ignore reflections in general. With three colors, rotation gives $24$ classes and the full symmetry group gives $21$. Before applying any counting theorem, decide exactly which physical moves identify objects.

## 11.9 Conjugation is an action on the group itself

Let $G$ act on its underlying set by

$$
g\cdot x=gxg^{-1}.
$$

The identity condition holds. Also $(gh)x(gh)^{-1}=g(hxh^{-1})g^{-1}$, so the action law holds. Its orbits are **conjugacy classes**. Its stabilizer at $x$ is

$$
C_G(x)=\{g\in G:gx=xg\},
$$

the **centralizer** of $x$. Orbit-stabilizer therefore gives

$$
|\operatorname{Cl}(x)|=[G:C_G(x)].
$$

The centralizer contains the cyclic subgroup generated by $x$, but it can contain much more. It consists of all group elements compatible with commuting with this particular element. The **center** is $Z(G)=\{z:zg=gz\text{ for every }g\in G\}$. It is the intersection of all centralizers and is a normal subgroup. A central element has a conjugacy class of size one; conversely, if the conjugacy class of $x$ has size one, every conjugate equals $x$, so $x$ commutes with every group element.

In $S_3$, the identity forms a class of size one. The three transpositions form one class, because relabeling the two exchanged symbols produces any transposition. The two three-cycles form one class. Thus $6=1+3+2$. The centralizer of a transposition has order two, giving class size three. The centralizer of a three-cycle has order three, giving class size two. The center is trivial, since only the identity has a singleton class.

Conjugation is best understood as changing labels while retaining structural behavior. If a permutation has cycles of certain lengths, conjugation relabels the entries in those cycles and preserves their lengths. In a general group, conjugation preserves element order, but equal order need not imply conjugacy. In an abelian group every conjugacy class is a singleton, even when many distinct elements have the same order.

## 11.10 The class equation and why divisibility enters

Choose one representative $x_i$ from each noncentral conjugacy class of a finite group. Because conjugacy classes partition the group,

$$
|G|=|Z(G)|+\sum_i[G:C_G(x_i)].
$$

This is the **class equation**. It converts a structural decomposition into a sum of subgroup indices. Those indices divide $|G|$, so prime divisibility can constrain the center.

Suppose $|G|=p^a$ for a prime $p$ and $a\ge1$. For a noncentral $x_i$, its centralizer is a proper subgroup, so its index is a power of $p$ greater than one. Every term in the sum is therefore divisible by $p$. Reducing the class equation modulo $p$ gives $|Z(G)|\equiv0\pmod p$. Since the center contains the identity and hence is nonempty, its order is at least $p$. Thus every nontrivial finite group of prime-power order has a nontrivial center.

The argument depends on the prime-power hypothesis. In $S_3$, whose order is $6$, the noncentral class sizes are $3$ and $2$. No single prime divides both, and their sum does not force a nontrivial center. This counterexample shows exactly why the proof cannot be reused indiscriminately.

We can already prove that every group of order $p^2$ is abelian. Its center has order $p$ or $p^2$. In the second case there is nothing to prove. In the first case, $G/Z(G)$ has order $p$, so it is cyclic. A useful lemma says that if $G/Z(G)$ is cyclic, then $G$ is abelian. Indeed, write every element as $g^az$ with $z\in Z(G)$, using a generator $gZ(G)$ of the quotient. Then $(g^az)(g^bw)=g^{a+b}zw=g^{b+a}wz=(g^bw)(g^az)$. This makes $G$ abelian, which would actually force $Z(G)=G$. Hence the first possibility collapses into the second.

Notice the distinction between this lemma and the false statement that every group with a cyclic quotient is abelian. The central subgroup in the quotient is crucial. For example, $S_3/A_3$ is cyclic, but $S_3$ is not abelian.

## 11.11 From arithmetic data to structural data

The action viewpoint explains several familiar number-theoretic arguments in a unified way. Fermat's little theorem can be obtained from the group of nonzero residues modulo a prime, but the elementary proof using multiplication by a nonzero residue is already an action argument. Multiplication permutes the nonzero residue classes. A product taken over the whole set is unchanged, and cancellation becomes legitimate because every factor is nonzero in a field.

Another useful action is translation on residue classes. The additive group $\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z$ acts on itself by $[a]\cdot[x]=[a+x]$. The action is free and transitive. Its orbit-stabilizer equation is $n=n\cdot1$. Although elementary, this shows why the regular action has no invisible movements: a translation fixing even one residue must be the zero translation.

For an action of a group of order $p^a$, every orbit size is a power of $p$. Consequently, for any finite acted-on set $X$, the number of points fixed by every group element is congruent to $|X|$ modulo $p$. To see this, separate the orbits of size one from all larger orbits. Larger orbits contribute multiples of $p$; size-one orbits are precisely the common fixed points. This elementary congruence is a powerful existence tool: if $p$ does not divide $|X|$, at least one common fixed point exists.

The phrase fixed by every group element matters. A point fixed by one particular nonidentity element need not be a singleton orbit. A rotation may preserve a coloring that a reflection changes, or vice versa. In Chapter 12 we will deliberately choose an acting prime-power subgroup and a coset set whose size is not divisible by $p$. The forced fixed point will convert into containment of one subgroup inside a conjugate of another. That is the mechanism behind Sylow conjugacy.

## 11.12 Working with an action in practice

When a new problem mentions symmetry, begin by naming three things: the group, the set and the rule. Then check that the rule respects identities and multiplication. Next ask what information is preserved, which points are reachable from one another, and which group elements leave a chosen point unchanged. Only then select a counting theorem.

For instance, in a necklace problem the set is not the collection of necklaces up to symmetry; it is first the collection of labeled colorings. The group acts on that larger set, and the unlabeled necklaces are its orbits. Starting with already-identified objects and then quotienting again can silently double-count equivalences. Similarly, when acting on cosets, the points are cosets, not elements chosen from cosets. The representative-independence calculation is part of the construction.

There are three frequent confusions worth separating. First, an action can be transitive without being faithful, as the diagonal example showed. Second, a stabilizer is generally not the kernel, because it fixes one point rather than all points. Third, $G/H$ can mean a coset set even when $H$ is not normal; it becomes a quotient group only when multiplication of cosets is well defined. Context determines which meaning is in use, so write the relevant explanation whenever ambiguity could affect a proof.

The [permutations lab](../companion/index.html) lets you inspect small permutation calculations and the cycle behavior behind these examples. Its finite outputs are evidence for specific cases, not proofs of the orbit-stabilizer theorem or Sylow's theorems. To connect experimentation with proof, predict a class or orbit size using a stabilizer, compute the small example, and compare the two descriptions. A mismatch usually signals a mistaken group action, an incorrect stabilizer, or an unspoken equivalence convention.


## 11.13 A second counting problem: prescribing the number of black beads

Often a counting question specifies a composition rather than merely a palette. Suppose four positions must contain exactly two black beads and two white beads. There are six labeled colorings, because choosing the two black positions determines the coloring. The rotation action preserves this condition, so it restricts to a smaller acted-on set. This restriction is legitimate because every rotation sends a coloring with two black positions to another coloring with two black positions.

The identity fixes all six. A quarter turn or three-quarter turn would force all four beads to have the same color, incompatible with having exactly two black beads. Those rotations fix zero. A half turn has two position cycles, each of length two. A fixed coloring with two black beads must color one complete cycle black and the other white, so there are exactly two. Burnside now gives $(6+0+2+0)/4=2$. The two types are adjacent black beads and opposite black beads.

This example reveals why the shortcut “a permutation with $c$ cycles fixes $k^c$ colorings” has hypotheses. That formula permits every cycle to receive any color independently. A prescribed number of black beads couples those choices. The cycle lengths, not only their number, then matter. To find fixed colorings under a permutation with cycle lengths $\ell_1,\ldots,\ell_c$, choose a collection of complete cycles whose lengths sum to the required number of black beads. The other cycles become white.

For a larger concrete calculation, consider six positions with exactly two black beads, up to rotations. The identity fixes $\binom62=15$ colorings. Rotations through one or five steps have one cycle of length six and fix none. Rotations through two or four steps have two cycles of length three and also fix none. A half turn has three cycles of length two, so exactly three fixed colorings arise by choosing one cycle to be black. The orbit count is $(15+3)/6=3$. We can interpret the three types by circular distance between the black beads: distance one, two or three. This geometric classification agrees with the fixed-point calculation and gives a useful independent check.

There is a small number-theory connection inside this calculation. Rotation by $j$ steps on $n$ positions has $\gcd(n,j)$ cycles, each of length $n/\gcd(n,j)$. Starting at a position, the first return occurs when $kj\equiv0\pmod n$. Dividing by the greatest common divisor shows that the least positive such $k$ is $n/\gcd(n,j)$. Thus modular arithmetic computes the permutation's cycle structure, which then controls counting up to symmetry. The two subjects are interacting through an explicit mechanism, not merely sharing vocabulary.

## 11.14 Normalizers and why conjugating a subgroup differs from conjugating an element

Conjugation also acts on the set of subgroups of $G$: the element $g$ sends a subgroup $H$ to $gHg^{-1}$. This image is a subgroup because conjugation preserves products and inverses. The action laws follow from the same calculation used for individual elements. The stabilizer of the point $H$ is called its **normalizer**:

$$
N_G(H)=\{g\in G:gHg^{-1}=H\}.
$$

The equality here is equality of sets. It does not demand $ghg^{-1}=h$ for every $h\in H$. The latter, stronger demand defines the centralizer of the subgroup. Therefore centralizing a subgroup implies normalizing it, while the converse can fail.

For example, in $S_3$, the subgroup $A_3=\{e,(123),(132)\}$ is normal, so every element of $S_3$ normalizes it. A transposition conjugates $(123)$ to $(132)$, however, so it does not centralize this subgroup. The normalizer is all of $S_3$, while the centralizer of $A_3$ is $A_3$ itself. The distinction is about preserving the whole collection versus fixing each member individually.

Every subgroup $H$ is contained in its own normalizer. Indeed, conjugating $H$ by one of its elements produces $H$, because subgroup products and inverses remain inside. Moreover, $H$ is normal in $N_G(H)$ by the defining equality. In fact the normalizer is the largest subgroup of $G$ in which $H$ is normal. Any subgroup with that property consists entirely of elements satisfying the normalizer condition.

Orbit-stabilizer now says that the number of conjugate subgroups of the form $gHg^{-1}$ is $[G:N_G(H)]$. This resembles the conjugacy-class formula for an element, but the stabilizers are different: an element uses a centralizer, and a subgroup uses a normalizer. In $S_3$, take $H=\{e,(12)\}$. Its conjugates are the three subgroups generated by transpositions, so the normalizer has order $6/3=2$. Since $H$ is already contained in the normalizer and has order two, the normalizer equals $H$.

These formulas prepare an important counting question. If some family of subgroups forms a single conjugation orbit, then the size of the family divides $|G|$. If every subgroup in that family has order $p^a$, then its normalizer contains at least $p^a$ elements, which further constrains that family size. Chapter 12 will apply exactly this reasoning to Sylow subgroups. Introducing the normalizer now prevents that later proof from looking like a new, unexplained counting trick.

## 11.15 A proof-writing clinic for action arguments

A polished action proof usually contains a short construction followed by a short translation. The construction identifies an action for which counting is easy. The translation explains what the resulting fixed point, orbit or stabilizer means in the original problem. Skipping the translation is a common reason an argument feels mysterious even when every displayed equation is correct.

Suppose a subgroup $P$ acts by left multiplication on $G/H$ and the coset $gH$ is fixed by every element of $P$. Expanding the definition gives $pgH=gH$ for every $p\in P$. Equality of left cosets is equivalent to $g^{-1}pg\in H$. Therefore the fixed-point statement is equivalent to $g^{-1}Pg\subseteq H$. Every symbol has now been translated: the point is a coset, its fixedness is an equality of cosets, and that equality becomes subgroup containment. The fixed point is useful because the containment is useful.

To audit such a proof, keep quantifiers visible. “There is a coset fixed by every element” means one common coset works for all elements. “Every element fixes some coset” allows different cosets and is much weaker. An action can satisfy the second statement without satisfying the first. Likewise, “all stabilizers have equal order” is automatic within one orbit, but not across different orbits. These distinctions are small in language and substantial in mathematics.

## 11.16 A final distinction about finite evidence

A computer can list every point in a small orbit, check each stabilizer membership, and verify a numerical identity exactly. Such a calculation establishes the identity for that finite input. A theorem about all finite groups needs a uniform argument, such as the coset bijection, that does not depend on completing an unbounded collection of searches. The two kinds of evidence serve different purposes. Computation is excellent for finding a wrong conjecture or an incorrect numerical example. Proof explains why a statement survives every allowed input. When an experiment suggests a general pattern, identify the invariant or correspondence that could replace the list. In this chapter the recurring replacement was a bijection: descriptions became cosets, and fixed pairs could be counted in two directions.

## 11.17 Optional exercises

Hints and complete reference solutions are in [Chapter 11 solutions](../solutions/11-solutions.md). These problems are optional opportunities to reconstruct the ideas rather than gates to further reading.

1. Let $C_6$ act on the six residues modulo $6$ by addition. Find the orbit and stabilizer of $[2]$, and decide whether the action is faithful and free.
2. Let $U(12)$ act on residues modulo $12$ by multiplication. Find the orbit and stabilizer of $[3]$. Explain why the orbit size is compatible with orbit-stabilizer.
3. Prove directly that if $y=g\cdot x$, then $G_y=gG_xg^{-1}$. Identify the step using an inverse action.
4. Count colorings of the vertices of a triangle with three labeled colors up to rotations. Then count them up to all triangle symmetries. Explain the difference.
5. Determine the conjugacy classes of $S_3$, and use them to show that a normal subgroup cannot have order two.
6. Prove that a finite group acting on a set with trivial stabilizers has a set size divisible by the group order. Give an example showing why faithfulness alone is insufficient.
7. Suppose a group of order $25$ acts on a set of $26$ points. Prove that at least one point is fixed by every group element. Can the same conclusion be forced for a set of $25$ points?
8. Let $G$ act on its left cosets of $H$. Prove that the action kernel is the largest normal subgroup contained in $H$, and compute it when $G=S_3$ and $H=\{e,(12)\}$.

The progression in this chapter has been from movement to equivalence, from equivalence to cosets, and from cosets to divisibility. The next chapter uses those same steps to answer a surprisingly strong question: how much subgroup structure is already forced by the prime factorization of a finite group's order?
