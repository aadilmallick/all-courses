# Chapter 07 — Cyclic groups, cosets and Lagrange

Suppose a machine has a reversible move $a$. Starting from its initial state, we apply the same move repeatedly. Does the machine eventually return to its starting state? If it does, how many distinct positions occur before the first return? If several reversible moves are available, can a repeating pattern generated by one move help us understand all possible moves?

These questions lead to two central constructions. A **cyclic subgroup** records repeated application of one element. A **coset** records a translated copy of a subgroup inside a larger group. Cosets turn group structure into a partition, and counting the pieces yields Lagrange's theorem. The result is a bridge from algebra to number theory: the size of a subgroup must divide the size of a finite group.

Our prerequisites are the group axioms, integer division, divisibility, gcds, and Bezout's identity. No geometry is required, although the square group from Chapter 6 will remain a useful example. Treat this chapter in three stages: repetitions and cyclic groups; cosets and counting; arithmetic consequences and classification.

## 1. Repetition and the order of an element

For $a$ in a group $G$, its integer powers form the subgroup

$$
\langle a\rangle=\{a^k:k\in\mathbb Z\}.
$$

It contains the identity $a^0=e$, products satisfy $a^ra^s=a^{r+s}$, and inverses satisfy $(a^r)^{-1}=a^{-r}$. These observations prove that it is a subgroup. A group generated by one element is called **cyclic**. The word suggests a loop, but an infinite cyclic group does not loop back: the integers under addition are cyclic, generated by $1$.

The **order of an element** $a$, written $\operatorname{ord}(a)$, is the smallest positive integer $n$ for which $a^n=e$, if such an integer exists. If no positive exponent returns to $e$, we say $a$ has infinite order. The word order also describes the size $|G|$ of a finite group. These usages are related, but one concerns an element and the other a set. We will show precisely how they fit together.

In the additive group $\mathbb Z/6\mathbb Z$, the class $[1]$ has order $6$: the successive multiples are $[0],[1],[2],[3],[4],[5]$, and the next is $[0]$ again. The class $[2]$ has order $3$, because $2[2]=[4]$ and $3[2]=[0]$. The class $[3]$ has order $2$, and $[0]$ has order $1$. The identity is the only element of order one.

In $(\mathbb Z,+)$, every nonzero integer has infinite order. If $a\ne0$ and $n>0$, then $na\ne0$. This example prevents a common misunderstanding: belonging to a cyclic group does not mean having finite order. The adjective cyclic describes generation by one element, not the finiteness of the group.

## 2. What a first return tells us about every return

Assume $a$ has finite order $n$. Then

$$
a^k=e\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad n\mid k
$$

for every integer $k$. One direction is immediate: if $k=qn$, then $a^k=(a^n)^q=e$. For the other direction, use division with remainder: write $k=qn+r$ where $0\le r<n$. If $a^k=e$, then $e=(a^n)^qa^r=a^r$. Minimality of $n$ rules out $0<r<n$, so $r=0$.

This proof works for negative $k$ too, because integer division with a nonnegative remainder remains valid. For instance, if $n=5$ and $k=-7$, we write $-7=(-2)5+3$. Powers of $a$ are therefore controlled by exponent classes modulo $n$, not only by positive exponents.

It follows that $a^i=a^j$ exactly when $n\mid(i-j)$. Indeed, multiplying by $a^{-j}$ reduces the equality to $a^{i-j}=e$. Thus the elements $e,a,\ldots,a^{n-1}$ are distinct and comprise all of $\langle a\rangle$. We have proved

$$
|\langle a\rangle|=\operatorname{ord}(a).
$$

For an infinite-order element, no two distinct integer powers agree, since equality would give a nonzero exponent returning to the identity. Therefore $\langle a\rangle$ is infinite, with each integer describing exactly one element. A cyclic group is consequently either a finite repeating sequence or an infinite sequence indexed without repetitions by all integers.

As a concrete calculation, let $r$ be a quarter-turn of a square. Its order is $4$, so $r^{2026}=r^2$ because $2026=506\cdot4+2$. Also $r^{-3}=r$ because $-3\equiv1\pmod4$. Exponents can be reduced modulo the order only after that order has been established. A guessed cycle length is not a substitute for checking the first return.

## 3. Why cyclic groups are abelian

If $G=\langle a\rangle$, any two elements are powers $a^r,a^s$. Their products satisfy

$$
a^ra^s=a^{r+s}=a^{s+r}=a^sa^r.
$$

Thus every cyclic group is abelian. The commutativity comes from addition of integer exponents. This argument also explains why powers of one element commute inside a group that is not itself abelian.

The converse fails. The group $\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z\times\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z$ is abelian under coordinatewise addition, but every nonidentity element has order $2$. A cyclic group of order $4$ would have a generator of order $4$, so this four-element group cannot be cyclic. Commutativity says that two independently chosen operations can be exchanged; cyclicity says every operation comes from repetition of a single one. The second condition is stronger.

It is often useful to compare two groups through a property that all generators would have to satisfy. To test whether a finite group is cyclic, search for an element whose order equals the group size. If one exists, its cyclic subgroup already contains every element. If every element has a smaller order, no single element generates the group. We will later develop efficient ways to find possible orders without listing every power.

## 4. Orders of powers and a gcd formula

Let $a$ have finite order $n$. What is the order of $a^k$? A power $(a^k)^m$ equals the identity precisely when $n\mid km$. Set $d=\gcd(n,k)$, write $n=dn_0$ and $k=dk_0$, and note that $\gcd(n_0,k_0)=1$. The condition becomes $n_0\mid k_0m$. Euclid's lemma then gives $n_0\mid m$. The least positive such $m$ is $n_0$, so

$$
\operatorname{ord}(a^k)=\frac{n}{\gcd(n,k)}.
$$

This includes $k=0$: the gcd is $n$, and the identity has order one. For negative $k$, the gcd is understood to be nonnegative, so the same formula applies. If the original element has infinite order and $k\ne0$, then $a^k$ still has infinite order, since a return would imply $a^{km}=e$ with $km\ne0$.

For $G=\langle a\rangle$ of order $12$, the element $a^8$ has order $12/\gcd(12,8)=3$. Its subgroup is $\{e,a^8,a^{16}\}=\{e,a^8,a^4\}$. The element $a^5$ has order $12$ and generates all of $G$, because $\gcd(12,5)=1$.

The generators of a cyclic group of order $n$ are exactly the powers $a^k$ with $\gcd(n,k)=1$. The number of such residue classes is Euler's totient $\varphi(n)$, a function we will examine in Chapter 9. Here its structural meaning is already visible: it counts the ways to choose one generator for the same finite cyclic group.

## 5. Every subgroup of a cyclic group is cyclic

Let $G=\langle a\rangle$ and $H\le G$. If $H=\{e\}$, it is cyclic, generated by $e$. Otherwise consider positive exponents $k$ for which $a^k\in H$. There is at least one: any nonidentity element of $H$ is some integer power; taking an inverse makes its exponent positive if necessary. Let $d$ be the least such positive exponent.

We claim $H=\langle a^d\rangle$. Since $a^d\in H$, every power of $a^d$ belongs to $H$, giving one inclusion. For the other, choose $a^k\in H$ and write $k=qd+r$ with $0\le r<d$. The element $a^r=a^k(a^d)^{-q}$ belongs to $H$. Minimality of $d$ forces $r=0$, and therefore $a^k\in\langle a^d\rangle$.

For finite $G$ of order $n$, the least positive exponent for the trivial subgroup is $n$, so a uniform version of the argument includes that case. Since $a^n=e\in H$, dividing $n$ by the least exponent $d$ shows $d\mid n$. Thus subgroups are generated by powers whose exponents divide $n$.

There is exactly one subgroup of each order $m$ dividing $n$, namely

$$
\left\langle a^{n/m}\right\rangle.
$$

Its order is $m$ by the formula just proved. Any subgroup of order $m$ has the form $\langle a^d\rangle$ with $d\mid n$, and its order $n/d=m$ forces $d=n/m$. Existence and uniqueness are separate claims, and both have now been justified.

For a cyclic group of order $12$, the subgroup orders are $1,2,3,4,6,12$. Their generators can be chosen as $a^{12},a^6,a^4,a^3,a^2,a$. The subgroup of order $4$ contains $e,a^3,a^6,a^9$, while the subgroup of order $3$ contains $e,a^4,a^8$. Their intersection is trivial because a common element would have exponent divisible both by $3$ and by $4$, hence by $12$.

This classification resembles the earlier classification of additive subgroups of the integers as sets of multiples. In both proofs, choose a smallest positive exponent or integer, then divide with remainder. Recognizing a proof pattern across contexts is one of the most useful habits in algebra.

## 6. Translating a subgroup creates a coset

Let $H\le G$ and $g\in G$. The **left coset** $gH$ is the subset

$$
gH=\{gh:h\in H\}.
$$

The **right coset** $Hg$ is $\{hg:h\in H\}$. These are subsets of $G$, not products of two numbers and not automatically subgroups. Multiplication by $g$ translates every element of $H$. In an additive group the notation becomes $g+H$ and $H+g$, which are equal because addition commutes.

For $G=\mathbb Z$ and $H=4\mathbb Z$, the coset $1+H$ is the set of integers congruent to $1$ modulo $4$. The four distinct cosets are $4\mathbb Z$, $1+4\mathbb Z$, $2+4\mathbb Z$, and $3+4\mathbb Z$. Thus the familiar residue classes are already cosets of an additive subgroup. Quotient language is extending something we have used, rather than inventing an unrelated object.

A coset usually lacks the identity. The set $1+4\mathbb Z$ does not contain $0$, so it is not an additive subgroup. In general, $gH$ contains $e$ exactly when $g\in H$, in which case $gH=H$. If $gh=e$ for some $h\in H$, then $g=h^{-1}\in H$. Conversely, if $g\in H$, closure and inverse closure show $gH=H$.

Different representatives can describe the same coset. In the integer example, $1+4\mathbb Z=5+4\mathbb Z$. The representatives differ by an element of the subgroup. The general criterion is

$$
aH=bH\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad b^{-1}a\in H.
$$

If the cosets agree, $a=be$ belongs to $bH$, so $a=bh$ for some $h\in H$ and $b^{-1}a=h$. Conversely, if $a=bh_0$ with $h_0\in H$, then $aH=bh_0H=bH$, because multiplying a subgroup by one of its own elements does not change the subgroup.

## 7. Cosets partition the group

Every element $g\in G$ belongs to $gH$, because $g=ge$. So left cosets cover $G$. The key fact is that two left cosets are either disjoint or equal. Suppose $x\in aH\cap bH$. Then $x=ah_1=bh_2$ for some $h_1,h_2\in H$. Rearranging gives $b^{-1}a=h_2h_1^{-1}\in H$, so the criterion above yields $aH=bH$.

This proof is more informative than drawing several nonoverlapping sets. It explains why a single shared element forces the entire translates to agree. The same argument works for infinite groups, and it works for right cosets with the factors arranged appropriately.

The map $H\to gH$ given by $h\mapsto gh$ is a bijection. It is surjective by the definition of $gH$, and injective by cancellation. Therefore every left coset has the same number of elements as $H$, whether that number is finite or infinite. The inverse bijection sends $x\in gH$ to $g^{-1}x$.

The **index** $[G:H]$ is the number of distinct left cosets of $H$ in $G$. It can be finite even when the group and subgroup are infinite. For example, $[\mathbb Z:4\mathbb Z]=4$, although both sets contain infinitely many integers. Index counts pieces, not individual elements in a piece.

For a finite group, the coset partition gives

$$
|G|=[G:H]\,|H|.
$$

This is **Lagrange's theorem**. In particular, $|H|$ divides $|G|$. The proof consists of a partition into equally sized subsets. All group-theoretic work went into establishing the partition and the equal sizes; the final multiplication is ordinary counting.

Be careful about the direction of the theorem. It says that an existing subgroup has order dividing the group order. It does not say that every divisor necessarily occurs as a subgroup order. That stronger conclusion holds in cyclic groups by the explicit construction above, but it fails for some other groups. We will later have enough permutation theory to inspect such failures concretely.

## 8. An explicit noncommutative coset calculation

Use the square group $D_8=\{e,r,r^2,r^3,s,rs,r^2s,r^3s\}$ with $r^4=s^2=e$ and $sr=r^{-1}s$. Let $H=\{e,s\}$. Its left cosets are

$$
H=\{e,s\},\quad rH=\{r,rs\},\quad r^2H=\{r^2,r^2s\},\quad r^3H=\{r^3,r^3s\}.
$$

They are disjoint pairs covering all eight elements. Hence $[D_8:H]=4$ and $8=4\cdot2$, exactly as Lagrange predicts.

The right coset $Hr$ is $\{r,sr\}=\{r,r^3s\}$. It differs from $rH=\{r,rs\}$. Thus left and right cosets can be different subsets. This does not threaten Lagrange's theorem, whose proof used one consistent choice of side. It does matter when we try to make the cosets themselves into a group, a construction developed in Chapter 8.

For comparison, let $R=\langle r\rangle=\{e,r,r^2,r^3\}$. Its two left cosets are $R$ and $sR$, while its two right cosets are $R$ and $Rs$. The reflection relation shows $sR=Rs$, the set of four reflections. This subgroup has matching left and right cosets, unlike the two-element reflection subgroup.

Performing a few calculations by hand is valuable here. A symbol such as $rH$ denotes a set, so to prove two such symbols unequal, identify an element belonging to one and not the other. To prove equality, either list both sets or use the membership criterion. Treating coset notation as if it were ordinary multiplication of cancellable numerical factors can hide the actual set-theoretic question.

## 9. Orders divide the group size

Apply Lagrange's theorem to the cyclic subgroup $\langle a\rangle$ of a finite group $G$. Since its size is the order of $a$, we obtain

$$
\operatorname{ord}(a)\mid |G|.
$$

Consequently $a^{|G|}=e$. The latter statement follows because every multiple of the order gives a return to the identity. The group need not be abelian. One element's powers form a cyclic subgroup regardless of how the other elements interact.

If $|G|$ is a prime $p$, choose any $a\ne e$. Its order divides $p$ and is not $1$, so its order is $p$. Therefore $G=\langle a\rangle$ is cyclic. Every group of prime order is thus abelian, although groups with composite order need not be.

This proof establishes more than the existence of some generator: every nonidentity element generates a group of prime order. Compare the additive classes modulo $p$, where every nonzero class has additive order $p$. Multiplicative classes modulo $p$ form a different group with $p-1$ elements, and their possible orders depend on divisors of $p-1$ instead. Distinguishing additive from multiplicative orders is crucial.

If a finite group has order $15$, each element order must lie among $1,3,5,15$. Lagrange alone does not prove that each of these orders occurs, nor that the group is cyclic. It eliminates impossible orders. Later theorems will add existence information. A useful reading habit is to separate a restriction from an existence theorem, even when the same integers appear in both.

## 10. Counting element orders in a cyclic group

Let $G$ be cyclic of order $n$. For every divisor $d$ of $n$, the unique subgroup of order $d$ has $\varphi(d)$ generators. These are exactly the elements of $G$ having order $d$. Every element has one definite order, so partitioning the elements by their orders gives

$$
\sum_{d\mid n}\varphi(d)=n.
$$

For $n=12$, the counts are $\varphi(1)=1$, $\varphi(2)=1$, $\varphi(3)=2$, $\varphi(4)=2$, $\varphi(6)=2$, and $\varphi(12)=4$. Their sum is $12$. The identity accounts for the divisor $1$, so we set $\varphi(1)=1$ in this arithmetic identity even though the main modular examples use moduli at least $2$.

We can also obtain this identity purely by classifying integers $1\le k\le n$ according to $\gcd(k,n)$. The group proof makes the same arithmetic partition visible through element orders. Such double interpretations are a recurring theme: a sum over divisors often reflects a decomposition of an algebraic object into natural pieces.

The formula does not hold with the same interpretation for every group of order $n$, because noncyclic groups can have different numbers of elements of each order. The four-element product group has three elements of order $2$ and none of order $4$, whereas a cyclic group of order $4$ has one element of order $2$ and two of order $4$. The total size agrees, but the distribution reveals the structure.

## 11. Commuting elements of coprime orders

Suppose $a$ and $b$ commute and have finite orders $m$ and $n$, with $\gcd(m,n)=1$. Then the order of $ab$ is $mn$. The commutativity assumption is essential because it permits $(ab)^k=a^kb^k$.

First, $(ab)^{mn}=a^{mn}b^{mn}=e$, so the order divides $mn$. To prove that no smaller positive exponent works, assume $(ab)^k=e$. Then $a^k=b^{-k}$. This common element belongs both to $\langle a\rangle$ and to $\langle b\rangle$. Any element in that intersection has order dividing both $m$ and $n$, by Lagrange applied inside the two cyclic groups. Coprimality forces the common element to have order one, so $a^k=b^k=e$.

Therefore $m\mid k$ and $n\mid k$. Since the two are coprime, $mn\mid k$. The least positive return exponent is $mn$, proving the claim. Notice how three tools cooperate: commuting factors, subgroup intersection, and elementary divisibility.

A related calculation in a direct product needs no coprimality assumption. The order of $(a,b)\in G\times H$, when the coordinate orders are finite, is $\operatorname{lcm}(m,n)$, the least positive integer divisible by both. Indeed, $(a,b)^k=(e,e)$ exactly when both $a^k=e$ and $b^k=e$. This gives a direct criterion for a product of two finite cyclic groups to be cyclic.

If $C_m=\langle a\rangle$ and $C_n=\langle b\rangle$, the product has $mn$ elements, and $(a,b)$ has order $\operatorname{lcm}(m,n)$. When the orders are coprime this equals $mn$, so the product is cyclic. If they share a divisor greater than one, every coordinate pair has order at most $\operatorname{lcm}(m,n)<mn$, so no generator exists. Hence $C_m\times C_n$ is cyclic exactly when $\gcd(m,n)=1$.

This statement is the group-theoretic version of the Chinese remainder theorem. The arithmetic map $[x]_{mn}\mapsto([x]_m,[x]_n)$ preserves addition and becomes a bijection when the moduli are coprime. Chapter 8 supplies language for saying precisely that the two additive groups have the same structure.

## 12. Index calculations without listing every element

For subgroups $K\le H\le G$ of a finite group, Lagrange's formula twice gives

$$
[G:K]=[G:H][H:K].
$$

Indeed, $|G|=[G:H]|H|$ and $|H|=[H:K]|K|$; substitute the second equation into the first and divide by the positive integer $|K|$. There is also a direct coset-partition proof, useful for infinite groups with finite indices, but the finite calculation already explains the relationship we need here.

An index-two subgroup has a special property: its left and right cosets match. If $g\in H$, both are $H$. If $g\notin H$, the left coset $gH$ is the complement $G\setminus H$, because there are exactly two left cosets. There are also exactly two right cosets, since right multiplication gives equally sized pieces of the same finite group; thus $Hg$ is the same complement. Chapter 8 will call this property normality.

For finite $G$, the number of right cosets equals the number of left cosets even if the cosets themselves differ. Both numbers equal $|G|/|H|$. More generally, taking inverses maps the left coset $gH$ to the right coset $Hg^{-1}$ and gives a bijection between the two families. The equality of counts does not require commutativity.

Suppose a group of order $24$ has subgroups $K\le H$ with $|K|=3$ and $|H|=12$. Then $[G:H]=2$, $[H:K]=4$, and $[G:K]=8$. The middle group divides the coset partition into two levels. Such index arithmetic will later help us bound possible subgroup configurations without constructing every element explicitly.

## 13. A structural worked example modulo twelve

Take the additive group $G=\mathbb Z/12\mathbb Z$ and the subgroup $H=\langle[4]\rangle=\{[0],[4],[8]\}$. Its order is $3$, and its index is $4$. The cosets are

$$
H,\quad [1]+H,\quad [2]+H,\quad [3]+H.
$$

For example, $[1]+H=\{[1],[5],[9]\}$ and $[3]+H=\{[3],[7],[11]\}$. The representative $[5]$ describes the same coset as $[1]$, because $[5]-[1]=[4]\in H$. There are twelve group elements but only four cosets, and no coset should be mistaken for one of its representatives.

The element $[8]$ generates $H$ too, since its order is $12/\gcd(12,8)=3$. The element $[6]$ does not belong to $H$, even though $6$ is a divisor of $12$. Divisibility of a representative by a selected integer is a different question from divisibility of an element's order. Write the actual subgroup list when the two notions begin to blur.

Now compare $K=\langle[3]\rangle=\{[0],[3],[6],[9]\}$. Its order is $4$. The intersection $H\cap K$ is $\{[0]\}$, while combining elements of the two subgroups generates all of $G$, because $[4]-[3]=[1]$. This is a small instance of the coprime-order phenomenon: the subgroup sizes $3$ and $4$ are coprime, and together the two independent cycles recover a cycle of length twelve.

The experiment offers evidence in selected finite cases; the order and coset proofs explain why the observed divisibility holds in every finite group.

## Reading the subgroup pattern through divisibility

For a cyclic group $G=\langle a\rangle$ of finite order $n$, write $H_d=\langle a^d\rangle$ when $d\mid n$. The subscript records a step size in the exponent, not the subgroup's size. In fact, $|H_d|=n/d$. Larger step sizes produce smaller subgroups. This reversal is easy to overlook if one tries to remember a subgroup diagram without remembering what its labels mean.

For divisors $d,t$ of $n$, the containment $H_d\subseteq H_t$ holds exactly when $t\mid d$. If $t\mid d$, then $a^d$ is a power of $a^t$, so every power of $a^d$ lies in $H_t$. Conversely, if $a^d\in H_t$, write $a^d=a^{tk}$ for an integer $k$. Then $n\mid(d-tk)$. Since $t\mid n$, it follows that $t\mid d$. The proof uses the fact that both step sizes divide $n$; without that restriction, different exponents can describe the same subgroup.

The intersection of $H_d$ and $H_t$ is $H_{\operatorname{lcm}(d,t)}$. An exponent represents an element of $H_d$ exactly when it is divisible by $d$, and membership in both subgroups therefore requires divisibility by the least common multiple. Because both $d$ and $t$ divide $n$, their least common multiple also divides $n$, so the notation fits the established classification. The intersection consequently has order $n/\operatorname{lcm}(d,t)$.

The subgroup generated by $H_d$ and $H_t$ is $H_{\gcd(d,t)}$. Products of their powers have exponents of the form $du+tv$. Every such exponent is a multiple of the gcd. Bezout's identity gives the reverse direction: the gcd itself is an integer combination of $d$ and $t$, so the power $a^{\gcd(d,t)}$ belongs to the generated subgroup. Again the subgroup statement is the integer-combination theorem in a different setting.

For a concrete case, take $n=60$, $d=6$, and $t=10$. The subgroup $H_6$ has order $10$ and $H_{10}$ has order $6$. Their intersection is $H_{30}$, of order $2$, while together they generate $H_2$, of order $30$. These numbers distinguish intersection from generation: a common restriction makes the subgroup smaller, whereas allowing both kinds of steps makes it larger. Computing the exponents first and subgroup orders second keeps the reversal under control.

The same ideas work in the infinite cyclic group, with the identity element corresponding to exponent $0$. Its subgroups are the multiples $d\mathbb Z$. There is no finite ambient order $n$ by which to divide, but divisibility still controls containment. For example, $6\mathbb Z\cap10\mathbb Z=30\mathbb Z$, and the subgroup generated by the two sets is $2\mathbb Z$. This comparison shows which parts of cyclic structure are consequences of integer arithmetic and which parts specifically require finiteness.

## Finding an element order with fewer calculations

Suppose $G$ is finite and we know $|G|=N$. Lagrange gives $a^N=e$, so we can search among divisors of $N$ rather than among all positive integers. One method is to test the positive divisors in increasing order. The first divisor $d$ satisfying $a^d=e$ is the order. The proof is immediate from the divisibility theorem for return exponents: the true order belongs to the candidate list, and no smaller candidate can be a return.

A more efficient method repeatedly removes prime factors from a known return exponent. Begin with $M=N$. For a prime $q$ dividing $M$, compute $a^{M/q}$. If the result is $e$, replace $M$ by $M/q$ and continue. If the result is not $e$, retain that factor at the current stage. Repeating until no prime factor can be removed produces the exact order.

Why does the final value work? Throughout the process the true order $d$ divides $M$, because $a^M=e$. If $M>d$, the integer $M/d$ has a prime divisor $q$. Then $d$ divides $M/q$, implying $a^{M/q}=e$, so a factor could still have been removed. Therefore the process cannot stop at a value larger than $d$. Since it never loses the divisibility $d\mid M$, it stops precisely at $d$.

For illustration, suppose an element in a group of order $24$ is known through computation to satisfy $a^{12}=e$ and $a^4=e$, while $a^2\ne e$. Starting from $24$, remove a factor $2$ to reach $12$, remove a factor $3$ to reach $4$, and then the failed test at $2$ shows no further factor can be removed. The order is $4$. The arithmetic of exponents organizes the computation, but the power evaluations themselves must still be performed in the actual group.

Repeated squaring makes those evaluations shorter. To compute $a^{13}$, write $13=8+4+1$ and form $a^2$, then $a^4=(a^2)^2$, then $a^8=(a^4)^2$. Multiply $a^8a^4a$. These particular factors commute because they are powers of the same element, so no assumption that the entire group is abelian is needed. Chapter 9 applies this procedure to modular exponentiation, where it becomes an essential practical tool.

## What finiteness contributes and what it does not

The coset partition and the bijection from a subgroup to each coset work for arbitrary groups. The finite numerical formula $|G|=[G:H]|H|$ is used here as an equality of ordinary nonnegative integers. When both sets are infinite, one should not divide infinity by infinity to calculate an index. For example, $\mathbb Z$ and $4\mathbb Z$ are both infinite, yet the index is $4$, while the subgroup $\{0\}$ has infinitely many cosets.

The difference matters when adapting a proof. The statement that a finite subgroup size divides a finite group size has ordinary arithmetic content. A statement involving infinite cardinalities requires a separate framework, and it is unnecessary for this course. We can still reason directly about cosets and their representatives in an infinite group without pretending their sizes are finite numbers.

Similarly, the existence of a smallest positive return exponent requires at least one positive return. The well-ordering principle then finds a smallest one. In a finite group, repetition among powers guarantees a return by cancellation. In an infinite group, no such repetition is guaranteed. These are distinct uses of finiteness, and identifying them makes it easier to decide which results survive when we move between finite and infinite examples.

## 14. Frequent errors and how the proofs repair them

An equation $a^k=e$ gives a multiple of the order, not necessarily the order itself. If $a^6=e$, the order might be $1,2,3$, or $6$. To claim order $6$, show that no smaller positive return occurs. The gcd formula for powers is safe only after the original order has been established.

A group of size $n$ does not automatically have an element of order $n$. The four-element product group already disproves that claim. Likewise, a subgroup size dividing $n$ does not automatically produce a subgroup. The coset argument derives a necessary condition from an existing subgroup; it does not reverse itself.

A coset representative is not unique, but the coset is a definite subset. If $aH=bH$, it is usually wrong to conclude $a=b$. What follows is $b^{-1}a\in H$. This is the same distinction already encountered when congruent integers were different representatives of one residue class.

Finally, do not replace a left coset by a right coset without justification. In an abelian group they coincide, but the square example shows an explicit failure in general. The next chapter will identify exactly the subgroup condition that makes the two sides agree and allows us to multiply cosets consistently.

## A labeling that remembers the operation

A finite cyclic group of order $n$ can be labeled by exponent classes modulo $n$. The label $[k]$ corresponds to $a^k$. This correspondence is well-defined because replacing $k$ by $k+qn$ does not change the power. It is injective because equal powers have exponents differing by a multiple of $n$, and it is surjective because every element is a power. More than a matching of elements, it remembers the operation: adding labels $[r]+[s]$ corresponds exactly to multiplying $a^ra^s=a^{r+s}$.

For an infinite cyclic group, the same correspondence uses integers themselves rather than residue classes. The map $k\mapsto a^k$ is bijective because no nonzero exponent gives the identity. Again addition of labels corresponds to multiplication. Thus all infinite cyclic groups have the same multiplication pattern, and all finite cyclic groups with the same order have the same pattern. The names or physical appearance of their elements are irrelevant to this statement.

This is a useful intermediate idea before the formal language of the next chapter. Two sets of the same size can always be paired somehow, but an arbitrary pairing may forget the operation. Here the exponent construction supplies a pairing that preserves it. A four-cycle of rotations therefore has the same operation pattern as addition modulo four, whereas the four-element product group does not. Its nonidentity elements all have order two, a feature that any operation-preserving pairing would have to preserve. We can already distinguish structural sameness from mere equality of cardinalities, even before assigning a name to that distinction.

## 15. Optional exercises

Hints and full solutions are in [Chapter 07 solutions](../solutions/07-solutions.md).

1. In the additive group $\mathbb Z/18\mathbb Z$, find the orders of $[4],[6],[9]$, and list all generators of the whole group.
2. Let $a$ have order $20$. Find the orders of $a^6$ and $a^{15}$, their generated subgroups, and the order of the intersection of those subgroups.
3. Prove that a subgroup of a finite cyclic group is determined uniquely by its order. Then list all subgroups of a cyclic group of order $18$.
4. For $H=\{e,s\}\le D_8$, compute $rH,Hr,r^2H,Hr^2$. Which displayed left and right cosets agree?
5. Prove that a finite group of prime order has no proper nontrivial subgroup. State why the same proof does not apply to a group of order $p^2$.
6. Determine whether $C_4\times C_6$ and $C_4\times C_9$ are cyclic, and find the largest possible element order in each.
7. Prove $\sum_{d\mid n}\varphi(d)=n$ by sorting the integers $1,\ldots,n$ according to their gcd with $n$.
8. Let $a,b$ be commuting elements of finite orders $m,n$. Prove that the order of $ab$ divides $\operatorname{lcm}(m,n)$, and give an example where equality fails.

## 16. The bridge to quotients

Repeated operations have led to orders, gcds, and generators. Translating a subgroup has led to equal-sized cosets and divisibility of group sizes. Residue classes are now recognizable as cosets of an additive subgroup, making modular arithmetic one instance of a general construction.

There is an unfinished question. Can the cosets themselves be multiplied by the rule $(aH)(bH)=abH$? The rule looks natural, but different choices of representatives may give different answers. Chapter 8 resolves that question through homomorphisms, kernels, and normal subgroups. The reward will be a precise explanation of how a complicated group can be compressed while preserving its multiplication.
