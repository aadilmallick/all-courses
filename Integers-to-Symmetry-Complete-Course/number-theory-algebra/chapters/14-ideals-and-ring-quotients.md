# Chapter 14 — Ideals, quotient rings and ring CRT

Congruence modulo an integer works because the difference between equivalent integers is a multiple of the modulus. Addition preserves that condition, and multiplication preserves it because a multiple of the modulus remains a multiple after multiplication by any integer. This second fact is the bridge from additive subgroups to ideals. An ideal records a collection of elements that can consistently be treated as zero while retaining both ring operations.

We will derive the definition from the requirement that quotient multiplication be well defined. Then we will use kernels to recognize ideals, prove the ring isomorphism theorem, and characterize fields and domains through maximal and prime ideals. The Chinese remainder theorem will reappear as a statement about splitting a ring into independent quotient components. The arithmetic version from Chapter 5 is one concrete instance of this general mechanism.

## 14.1 Why an additive subgroup is not enough

Suppose $R$ is a ring and $I$ is an additive subgroup. Its additive cosets $a+I$ can be added by $(a+I)+(b+I)=(a+b)+I$, because additive quotient groups were already constructed in Chapter 8. We would like to multiply by $(a+I)(b+I)=ab+I$. But changing representatives changes the product:

$$
(a+i)(b+j)-ab=aj+bi+ij,\qquad i,j\in I.
$$

To guarantee that the difference lies in $I$, we need products of ambient ring elements with elements of $I$ to stay in $I$. Closure of $I$ under multiplying its own elements is not enough: the coefficients $a,b$ need not belong to $I$.

For a concrete failure, consider the additive subgroup $\Delta=\{(n,n):n\in\mathbb Z\}$ inside $\mathbb Z\times\mathbb Z$. It is even a subring with the ambient identity. But $(1,1)\in\Delta$ and $(1,0)(1,1)=(1,0)\notin\Delta$. In the proposed quotient, $(1,1)+\Delta=0+\Delta$. Multiplying by $(1,0)+\Delta$ would give both $(1,0)+\Delta$ and $0+\Delta$, distinct cosets. The operation would depend on the chosen representative and hence would not be a function.

This example makes the needed strengthening unavoidable. We are not inventing a condition arbitrarily; we are reading it from the representative-independence calculation. The correct objects for ring quotients are more restrictive than additive subgroups or subrings.

## 14.2 The ideal definition and its first consequences

An **ideal** $I$ of a commutative ring $R$ is an additive subgroup satisfying

$$
r i\in I\quad\text{for all }r\in R,\ i\in I.
$$

The extra condition is called absorption. We write $I\trianglelefteq R$ when useful, though this notation differs in meaning from normality in a group. The additive group of a ring is abelian, so additive normality is automatic; absorption is the additional multiplicative requirement.

Every ideal contains zero, is closed under differences, and is closed under multiplication of its own elements. It need not contain one. In fact, an ideal contains one exactly when it equals the entire ring. If $1\in I$, then every $r=r1$ lies in $I$. Conversely, the whole ring certainly contains one. An ideal other than $R$ is called **proper**.

The zero ideal $\{0\}$ and whole-ring ideal $R$ always exist. In $\mathbb Z$, the multiples $n\mathbb Z$ form an ideal for every nonnegative integer $n$. In a field, there are only two ideals. If an ideal contains a nonzero element $a$, it contains $a^{-1}a=1$, so it is the whole field. Thus a field's lack of nontrivial ideals is another form of its universal division property.

An ideal is not generally a subring under our shared-identity convention. The ideal $2\mathbb Z$ lacks the integer one. Conversely, the diagonal example shows that a subring need not be an ideal. The two definitions preserve different information: subrings describe smaller arithmetic systems; ideals describe which errors may be ignored in a quotient.

## 14.3 Principal ideals and generated ideals

For $a\in R$, the **principal ideal** generated by $a$ is

$$
(a)=\{ra:r\in R\}.
$$

It is an ideal: differences of multiples remain multiples, and multiplying a multiple by any ring element produces another multiple. It contains $a=1a$, and every ideal containing $a$ must contain all its multiples. Therefore $(a)$ is the smallest ideal containing $a$.

For several elements $a_1,\ldots,a_k$, the generated ideal is

$$
(a_1,\ldots,a_k)=\{r_1a_1+\cdots+r_ka_k:r_i\in R\}.
$$

The same closure checks prove it is an ideal, and the minimality follows from absorption and addition. A generated ideal consists of linear combinations with coefficients in the ambient ring. The coefficient ring matters. Integer combinations of two integers behave differently from rational combinations, since any nonzero rational generator already generates the whole field $\mathbb Q$.

In $\mathbb Z$, $(a,b)=(d)$ where $d=\gcd(a,b)$. Every combination $ax+by$ is divisible by $d$, so $(a,b)\subseteq(d)$. Bezout gives $d=ax_0+by_0$, so $d\in(a,b)$ and every multiple of $d$ belongs to $(a,b)$. This proves the reverse inclusion.

For example, $(18,30)=(6)$. The statement does not merely say six is a common divisor. It says the entire set of integer combinations of eighteen and thirty is exactly the set of multiples of six. This set-level formulation is the one that generalizes naturally to other rings.

## 14.4 Every ideal of the integers is principal

Let $I$ be an ideal of $\mathbb Z$. If $I=\{0\}$, then $I=(0)$. Otherwise it contains a nonzero integer and hence a positive integer, because it is closed under negatives. By well-ordering, let $d$ be its least positive element.

For any $a\in I$, integer division gives $a=qd+r$ with $0\le r<d$. Since $a$ and $qd$ lie in $I$, their difference $r$ lies in $I$. Minimality of $d$ forces $r=0$. Therefore every $a\in I$ is divisible by $d$, so $I\subseteq(d)$. The reverse inclusion follows from $d\in I$ and absorption. Thus $I=(d)$.

The proof deliberately echoes Euclid's algorithm: divide by a smallest positive object and force the remainder to vanish. Chapter 16 will abstract this reasoning into Euclidean domains and principal ideal domains. For now, it tells us that integer moduli are not merely examples of ideals; they account for all ideals of the integer ring.

Containment reverses ordinary divisibility: $(a)\subseteq(b)$ exactly when $b\mid a$. For instance, $(12)\subseteq(4)$ because every multiple of twelve is a multiple of four. A larger generating integer can produce a smaller ideal. When interpreting ideal diagrams, check an element rather than relying on the apparent numerical size of the generator.

## 14.5 Constructing the quotient ring

Given an ideal $I$, define $a\sim b$ when $a-b\in I$. The additive subgroup conditions make this an equivalence relation. Its classes are $a+I$. Set

$$
(a+I)+(b+I)=(a+b)+I,\qquad
(a+I)(b+I)=ab+I.
$$

Addition is well defined by the subgroup calculation. For multiplication, replace $a,b$ by $a+i,b+j$. The difference $aj+bi+ij$ lies in $I$ by absorption and addition, so the product class is unchanged. This finishes the representative-independence check.

All ring identities descend from $R$. For example, distributivity in the quotient follows because $a(b+c)=ab+ac$ already holds before taking classes. The zero is $I=0+I$ and the identity is $1+I$. If $I$ is proper, these are distinct: equality would mean $1\in I$. Thus $R/I$ is a ring under our nonzero-ring convention whenever $I$ is proper.

If $I=R$, the construction has a single class, so zero equals one. This is the **zero ring**. We allow it only as this explicitly identified degenerate quotient; it is excluded from the standing convention $1\ne0$ and is neither a field nor an integral domain. Keeping this exception visible avoids accidentally calling the quotient by the whole ring a field.

The quotient map $\pi:R\to R/I$, $a\mapsto a+I$, preserves addition, multiplication and identity. Its kernel is exactly $I$. The phrase “declare every element of $I$ to be zero” is therefore precise: exactly those elements map to the quotient's zero class.

## 14.6 A small quotient calculated completely

Take $R=\mathbb Z$ and $I=(4)$. There are four classes represented by $0,1,2,3$. Addition and multiplication are the familiar operations modulo four. In this quotient, $[2]\ne[0]$ but $[2]^2=[0]$. The quotient of a domain can therefore have zero divisors.

Now take $R=\mathbb Z\times\mathbb Z$ and $I=\mathbb Z\times\{0\}$. This is an ideal: multiplying $(a,0)$ by any $(r,s)$ gives $(ra,0)$. Two pairs belong to the same coset precisely when their second coordinates agree. The quotient therefore retains the second coordinate and forgets the first. The map $(a,b)+I\mapsto b$ is a ring isomorphism onto $\mathbb Z$.

This example prevents an overly narrow view of quotients as merely finite modular systems. A quotient can remain infinite. Its purpose is to identify elements according to an ideal, not necessarily to reduce the cardinality to a small number. The same idea can remove one component, impose an equation, or identify expressions that differ by a controlled algebraic error.

A quotient also does not mean that the original elements of $I$ have literally become equal inside $R$. They remain distinct in $R$; their images coincide in a newly constructed ring. Carefully distinguishing an element from its image is especially important when a quotient calculation is later interpreted back in the original ring.

## 14.7 Kernels and the first isomorphism theorem

Let $f:R\to S$ be a ring homomorphism. Its kernel $K=\{r:f(r)=0\}$ is an ideal. It is an additive subgroup by the group-homomorphism argument. If $k\in K$ and $r\in R$, then $f(rk)=f(r)f(k)=f(r)0=0$, proving absorption. Since $f(1)=1\ne0$, the kernel is proper when the target is a nonzero ring.

The **first ring isomorphism theorem** says

$$
R/K\cong f(R).
$$

Define $\overline f(r+K)=f(r)$. If $r+K=r'+K$, then $r-r'\in K$, so $f(r)=f(r')$. The map is therefore well defined. It preserves operations because $f$ does. It is surjective onto the image by definition. If $\overline f(r+K)=0$, then $r\in K$, so its kernel contains only the zero class. Thus it is injective and hence an isomorphism.

The theorem identifies the image's arithmetic with the source's arithmetic after precisely the lost information has been removed. For reduction modulo $n$, the kernel is $n\mathbb Z$, giving $\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z$ as expected. For the projection $R\times S\to S$, the kernel is $R\times\{0\}$, explaining the preceding quotient without writing operation tables.

A common misuse is to write $R/\ker f\cong S$ without verifying surjectivity. The theorem gives the image $f(R)$, which can be a proper subring. Inclusion $\mathbb Z\to\mathbb Q$ has zero kernel, but it does not imply $\mathbb Z\cong\mathbb Q$. Its image is the embedded integer subring, not all rational numbers.

## 14.8 Ideals upstairs and downstairs

Let $\pi:R\to R/I$ be the quotient map. Ideals of $R/I$ correspond exactly to ideals $J$ of $R$ containing $I$. Given such a $J$, its image $J/I=\{j+I:j\in J\}$ is an ideal in the quotient. Given an ideal $L$ downstairs, its inverse image $\pi^{-1}(L)$ is an ideal upstairs containing $I$.

Why are these procedures inverse? If $J\supseteq I$, an element $r$ belongs to $\pi^{-1}(J/I)$ exactly when $r+I=j+I$ for some $j\in J$. Then $r-j\in I\subseteq J$, so $r\in J$. Conversely, $r\in J$ clearly maps into $J/I$. Starting with $L$, surjectivity of $\pi$ ensures every element of $L$ is an image of some element of its inverse image, so one recovers $L$.

The containing condition is indispensable. An ideal not containing $I$ cannot be recovered faithfully after every element of $I$ has been collapsed to zero. This correspondence will turn maximality into a field condition and will classify ideals in residue rings.

For instance, ideals of $\mathbb Z/12\mathbb Z$ correspond to integer ideals $(d)$ containing $(12)$. The containment means $d\mid12$, so the possibilities are $d=1,2,3,4,6,12$. Their images are the multiples of $[d]$ modulo twelve. The zero ideal corresponds to $d=12$, and the whole ring corresponds to $d=1$. This reverses the numerical order just as before.

## 14.9 Prime ideals and domain quotients

A proper ideal $P$ is **prime** if $ab\in P$ implies $a\in P$ or $b\in P$. The condition says that a product cannot become zero modulo $P$ without one factor becoming zero modulo $P$.

Precisely, $P$ is prime if and only if $R/P$ is an integral domain. A product $(a+P)(b+P)$ is the zero class exactly when $ab\in P$. Its factors are zero exactly when $a\in P$ or $b\in P$. Thus the defining condition for primality is exactly the absence of nonzero zero divisors in the quotient. Properness guarantees that the quotient is nonzero.

In $\mathbb Z$, the ideal $(p)$ is prime for a positive prime integer $p$. The zero ideal is also prime, because the integers form a domain. Thus prime ideal does not always mean “generated by a nonzero prime element.” In a general ring, prime ideals need not be principal at all; that broader phenomenon will be discussed when polynomial rings in several variables appear in a later course.

The ideal $(6)$ is not prime: $2\cdot3\in(6)$ while neither factor lies in $(6)$. Its quotient has the corresponding zero-divisor equation $[2][3]=[0]$. This is a direct dictionary, with no extra calculation hidden between the ideal statement and the quotient statement.

## 14.10 Maximal ideals and field quotients

A proper ideal $M$ is **maximal** if there is no ideal strictly between $M$ and $R$. Equivalently, any ideal containing $M$ is either $M$ or $R$. The correspondence theorem says this happens exactly when $R/M$ has only its zero and whole-ring ideals.

A nonzero commutative ring has only those two ideals exactly when it is a field. One direction was proved in Chapter 13. For the other, choose $a\ne0$. Its principal ideal $(a)$ is nonzero, so it must be the whole ring. Therefore $1=ra$ for some $r$, making $a$ a unit. Since every nonzero element is a unit, the ring is a field. Combining these statements gives

$$
M\text{ maximal}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad R/M\text{ is a field}.
$$

One can also see the inverse directly. If $a\notin M$ and $M$ is maximal, the ideal $M+(a)$ properly contains $M$ and hence equals $R$. Thus $1=m+ra$ for some $m\in M$. Reducing modulo $M$ gives $(r+M)(a+M)=1+M$. This explicitly builds the inverse of every nonzero quotient class.

Every maximal ideal is prime, because every field is a domain. The converse fails: $(0)$ is prime in $\mathbb Z$ but not maximal, since $(0)\subsetneq(2)\subsetneq\mathbb Z$. For nonzero integer ideals, $(n)$ is maximal exactly when $n$ is prime, because its quotient is a field exactly then. The exceptional zero ideal is why one must distinguish the general concepts even in the integer example.

## 14.11 Adding and multiplying ideals

For ideals $I,J$, their intersection is an ideal. Their set-theoretic union generally is not: $(2)\cup(3)$ contains two and three but not their sum five. Their **sum** is instead

$$
I+J=\{i+j:i\in I,j\in J\}.
$$

This is the smallest ideal containing both. Difference closure and absorption follow term by term. Their **product** $IJ$ is the set of finite sums of products $ij$ with $i\in I,j\in J$. We need finite sums, not merely single products, to guarantee additive closure in arbitrary rings. In the integers, $(a)(b)=(ab)$.

Always $IJ\subseteq I\cap J$, because each product lies in both ideals by absorption. Equality need not hold: $(2)(2)=(4)$, while $(2)\cap(2)=(2)$. Ideals are called **comaximal** when $I+J=R$. Equivalently, there are $i\in I,j\in J$ with $i+j=1$.

For comaximal ideals, $IJ=I\cap J$. We already know one inclusion. If $x\in I\cap J$, write $1=i+j$. Then $x=xi+xj$, and both terms lie in $IJ$: in the first, $x\in J$ and $i\in I$; in the second, $x\in I$ and $j\in J$. Thus $x\in IJ$.

In the integers, $(a)+(b)=(\gcd(a,b))$. Hence the ideals are comaximal exactly when $a,b$ are coprime. The ideal language generalizes the Bezout certificate $ax+by=1$ rather than replacing it with an unrelated definition.

## 14.12 Ring Chinese remainder theorem

Let $I,J$ be proper comaximal ideals. Consider the homomorphism

$$
\Phi:R\longrightarrow R/I\times R/J,\qquad r\longmapsto(r+I,r+J).
$$

Its kernel is $I\cap J$. It is surjective because comaximality supplies $i+j=1$ with $i\in I,j\in J$. Given desired classes $a+I,b+J$, set $x=aj+bi$. Modulo $I$, the term $bi$ vanishes and $j=1-i$ becomes one, so $x+I=a+I$. Modulo $J$, the other term vanishes and $i$ becomes one, so $x+J=b+J$.

The first isomorphism theorem therefore gives

$$
R/(I\cap J)\cong R/I\times R/J.
$$

Since $I\cap J=IJ$ in this situation, the left side can also be written $R/IJ$. The theorem asserts an isomorphism of rings, so it preserves addition and multiplication simultaneously. Its unit-group consequence is the multiplicative CRT used earlier.

For $R=\mathbb Z$, $I=(4)$ and $J=(9)$, the theorem gives $\mathbb Z/36\mathbb Z\cong\mathbb Z/4\mathbb Z\times\mathbb Z/9\mathbb Z$. A certificate is $1=(-8)+9$, with $-8\in(4)$ and $9\in(9)$. To solve $x\equiv3\pmod4$ and $x\equiv5\pmod9$, use $x=3\cdot9+5\cdot(-8)=-13\equiv23\pmod{36}$. Checking gives remainders three and five. This is the same arithmetic algorithm as before, now explained by a ring map and its kernel.

## 14.13 When independence fails

Take moduli four and six. The map $\mathbb Z\to\mathbb Z/4\mathbb Z\times\mathbb Z/6\mathbb Z$ still exists, and its kernel is $(12)$. But it is not surjective. The pair $([0]_4,[1]_6)$ cannot occur, because an integer cannot be simultaneously even and odd. The ideals are not comaximal: $(4)+(6)=(2)$.

A pair of classes occurs exactly when its representatives agree modulo two. Necessity follows because both congruences impose the same parity. For sufficiency, solving $x=a+4t$ and requiring $x\equiv b\pmod6$ leads to $4t\equiv b-a\pmod6$. Chapter 5 says this is solvable exactly when $\gcd(4,6)=2$ divides $b-a$. Thus there are twelve compatible pairs rather than all twenty-four pairs.

The kernel alone does not guarantee the full product as an image. The first isomorphism theorem still gives an isomorphism from $\mathbb Z/12\mathbb Z$ onto the image, but the image is a proper subring of the product. Comaximality is exactly the extra hypothesis guaranteeing independent choices of the two quotient coordinates.

This is a recurring algebraic pattern: a map is easy to define, its kernel is easy to identify, and the substantive existence problem is surjectivity. Keeping those steps separate makes it much harder to prove an accidentally stronger theorem than the hypotheses support.


## 14.14 The quotient's mapping property

There is another way to understand why a quotient is the correct place to forget an ideal. Suppose $f:R\to S$ is a ring homomorphism and $I\subseteq\ker f$. Then there is a unique homomorphism $\widetilde f:R/I\to S$ satisfying $f=\widetilde f\circ\pi$. Its formula must be $\widetilde f(r+I)=f(r)$. It is well defined because $r-r'\in I$ implies $f(r-r')=0$, so representatives of the same class have the same image.

Uniqueness follows from surjectivity of $\pi$: every quotient element is some $r+I$, so the required factorization determines its image. This statement is called a universal mapping property. The terminology is less important than the mechanism. Any arithmetic-respecting map that already treats $I$ as zero can be computed through the quotient, and it can be computed through it in only one compatible way.

For example, reduction modulo three can be computed after reduction modulo six, because $(6)\subseteq(3)$. The induced map $\mathbb Z/6\mathbb Z\to\mathbb Z/3\mathbb Z$ sends $[a]_6$ to $[a]_3$. In the opposite direction, the same-looking rule $[a]_3\mapsto[a]_6$ is not well defined: $[0]_3=[3]_3$, but $[0]_6\ne[3]_6$. The ideal containment provides an efficient test for the direction in which a residue map is allowed.

More generally, if $I\subseteq J$, there is a surjective homomorphism $R/I\to R/J$. Its kernel is $J/I$, and the first isomorphism theorem gives $(R/I)/(J/I)\cong R/J$. This is the third isomorphism theorem in the ring setting. It says that forgetting $I$ and then forgetting the remaining part of $J$ has the same final effect as forgetting $J$ immediately. The containment hypothesis ensures that the second stage makes sense.

## 14.15 Classifying ideals in a product ring

Let $R,S$ be nonzero commutative rings. Every ideal $K$ of $R\times S$ has the form $I\times J$ for ideals $I\le R$ and $J\le S$. We will prove this rather than assume that an arbitrary subset of a product splits into independent coordinates.

Define $I=\{r:(r,0)\in K\}$ and $J=\{s:(0,s)\in K\}$. Difference closure and absorption show these are ideals in their respective rings. If $(r,s)\in K$, multiply by the ambient elements $(1,0)$ and $(0,1)$ to obtain $(r,0)\in K$ and $(0,s)\in K$. Hence $(r,s)\in I\times J$, proving one inclusion. Conversely, if $r\in I$ and $s\in J$, both coordinate pieces belong to $K$, and adding them gives $(r,s)\in K$. Thus equality holds.

This proof uses precisely the absorption that failed for the diagonal subring. Ideals cannot correlate coordinates in an arbitrary way, because multiplying by coordinate selectors separates those coordinates. In $\mathbb F_2\times\mathbb F_2$, each factor field has only its zero and whole ideals, so the product has exactly four ideals: zero, the two coordinate axes, and the whole ring.

Each axis is maximal. Quotienting by the first axis leaves the second field, and quotienting by the second leaves the first. The zero ideal is not prime, since the product ring has zero divisors. This gives a complete small example where there are two distinct maximal ideals and no smaller prime ideal. It contrasts with the integers, where the zero ideal is prime and there are infinitely many maximal ideals indexed by prime numbers.

## 14.16 CRT selectors and explicit reconstruction

The elements $i,j$ with $i+j=1$ in the CRT proof become coordinate selectors after passing to $R/(I\cap J)$. The class of $j$ maps to $(1,0)$ in $R/I\times R/J$, and the class of $i$ maps to $(0,1)$. Consequently their quotient classes satisfy $\overline i^2=\overline i$, $\overline j^2=\overline j$, $\overline i\,\overline j=0$ and $\overline i+\overline j=1$.

To verify the idempotence directly, $j^2-j=j(j-1)=-ij$, which belongs to $IJ=I\cap J$. Similarly for $i$. Their product belongs to the same ideal. Thus the nontrivial idempotents observed modulo six were not accidents; they are the algebraic record of a product splitting.

For moduli four and nine, the selector for the first coordinate is $[9]_{36}$ and the selector for the second is $[-8]_{36}=[28]_{36}$. Their sum is one modulo thirty-six, their product is zero, and each squares to itself. Given any residue $[x]_{36}$, the decomposition $[x]=[9x]+[28x]$ isolates its two CRT components. The first term has its modulo-nine component zero; the second has its modulo-four component zero.

This is useful for calculations. Instead of raising a large residue to a large power modulo thirty-six, compute the two components modulo four and nine, then reconstruct. The ring isomorphism guarantees that addition, multiplication and exponentiation all agree with componentwise calculation. The gain is conceptual as well as computational: a composite modulus can hide independent pieces of arithmetic.

If a ring is a domain, it has no nontrivial idempotents, so it cannot be isomorphic to a product of two nonzero rings. This is another quick consequence of the identity $e(e-1)=0$. The ring CRT does not contradict it, because quotienting a domain by a composite modulus can introduce zero divisors and hence enable a splitting unavailable in the original domain.

## 14.17 More than two coprime conditions

The CRT extends to finitely many pairwise comaximal proper ideals $I_1,\ldots,I_k$:

$$
R/\bigcap_{j=1}^k I_j\cong\prod_{j=1}^k R/I_j.
$$

Here pairwise comaximal means $I_i+I_j=R$ whenever $i\ne j$. To see why the two-ideal proof can be iterated, one needs a small lemma: if $I+J=R$ and $I+K=R$, then $I+JK=R$. Choose $i+j=1$ and $i'+k=1$ with $i,i'\in I$, $j\in J$, $k\in K$. Multiplying gives $1=ii'+ik+ji'+jk$. The first three terms lie in $I$, and the last lies in $JK$. This proves the lemma.

Repeated application shows that $I_1$ is comaximal with $I_2\cdots I_k$. Induction then proves that the product of all the ideals equals their intersection and that independent quotient coordinates can be reconstructed. This provides a full proof route rather than merely asserting that the two-ideal theorem obviously generalizes.

In integer arithmetic, factoring $n=p_1^{a_1}\cdots p_k^{a_k}$ gives

$$
\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z\cong\prod_j\mathbb Z/p_j^{a_j}\mathbb Z.
$$

Different prime-power factors are coprime, so their ideals are pairwise comaximal. This is the ring-level reason that many modular problems reduce to prime-power moduli. It does not turn a prime-power residue ring into a field when the exponent exceeds one; it isolates the remaining local difficulty. Hensel lifting in Chapter 29 will exploit exactly that remaining prime-power structure.

## 14.18 Nilpotents, prime quotients and what an ideal detects

An element $a+I$ in a quotient is nilpotent precisely when $a^k\in I$ for some positive integer $k$. For example, $[2]$ modulo eight is nonzero but its third power is zero. Every prime ideal contains any element whose positive power lies in it. To prove this, if $a^k\in P$, primality applied to $a\cdot a^{k-1}$ gives $a\in P$ or $a^{k-1}\in P$. Repeating the argument eventually forces $a\in P$.

Consequently a quotient by a prime ideal has no nonzero nilpotents, in agreement with its being a domain. The converse is false: a ring can have zero divisors but no nonzero nilpotents. The product $\mathbb F_2\times\mathbb F_2$ is an example. Its two coordinate selectors are zero divisors, but any positive power of a nonzero selector remains that selector, never zero.

For an integer modulus $n$, the quotient has a nonzero nilpotent when a prime square divides $n$. If $n=p^a m$ with $a\ge2$ and $p\nmid m$, the integer $n/p$ is nonzero modulo $n$, and its square is divisible by $n$: its exponent of $p$ is $2a-2\ge a$, while its other prime exponents are doubled. Thus its residue is a nonzero nilpotent. If $n$ is squarefree, $n\mid x^k$ forces every prime dividing $n$ to divide $x$, and therefore $n\mid x$. So no nonzero nilpotent occurs. Unique factorization in the integers provides the exact criterion.

This optional observation distinguishes two types of failure of the domain property. A squarefree composite modulus splits into field components and has idempotent zero divisors. Repeated prime factors permit nilpotent behavior. Both are visible through ideal quotients, but they have different structural consequences. Later chapters will revisit this distinction through polynomial factorization and lifting roots.

## 14.19 How to turn a quotient problem into a kernel problem

When asked to identify $R/I$, look for a familiar target ring and a homomorphism from $R$ whose kernel is exactly $I$. Verify three separate claims: the map preserves the operations and identity; every desired target element is reached; and precisely the elements of $I$ map to zero. The first isomorphism theorem then identifies the quotient. Guessing a matching cardinality alone is insufficient, because rings of the same size can have different characteristics and different zero-divisor behavior.

For example, $\mathbb Z/4\mathbb Z$ and $\mathbb F_2\times\mathbb F_2$ each have four elements, but their characteristics are four and two. They cannot be isomorphic because an identity-preserving homomorphism preserves repeated sums of one. The former also has a nonzero nilpotent, whereas the latter does not. These invariants are quick ways to reject a proposed isomorphism before constructing any map.

Conversely, a well-chosen map can make an apparently complicated quotient transparent. Projection removes a product component, reduction changes an integer modulus, and CRT separates compatible arithmetic coordinates. In each case the quotient is explained by exactly what information the homomorphism forgets. That interpretation will be particularly valuable for polynomials, where imposing one polynomial relation can create a new field.


## 14.20 Solving a linear equation through an ideal

The equation $(a+I)(x+I)=b+I$ asks for an element $x\in R$ such that $ax-b\in I$. Equivalently, it asks whether $b$ belongs to the ideal $I+(a)$. Indeed, if $ax-b=i\in I$, then $b=ax-i$ is in that sum. Conversely, an expression $b=i+ax$ produces the required quotient equation after reduction. This gives a general solvability criterion without assuming the quotient is a field.

For $R=\mathbb Z$ and $I=(n)$, the criterion becomes $b\in(n,a)=(\gcd(n,a))$. We recover the familiar statement that $ax\equiv b\pmod n$ is solvable exactly when $\gcd(a,n)$ divides $b$. The ideal formulation has not changed the arithmetic answer; it has identified the structural reason behind it.

For example, in $\mathbb Z/18\mathbb Z$, the equation $[6]x=[12]$ is solvable because twelve lies in $(18,6)=(6)$. Dividing the corresponding integer divisibility condition by six gives $x\equiv2\pmod3$. The six residue solutions modulo eighteen are $[2],[5],[8],[11],[14],[17]$. In contrast, $[6]x=[9]$ is impossible because nine is not a multiple of six. The obstruction is membership in a particular ideal, rather than a failure to guess the right inverse.

Uniqueness has a second structural description. If $x_0$ is one solution, then $x$ is another exactly when $a(x-x_0)\in I$. Thus all differences between solutions form the kernel of the additive map on $R/I$ given by multiplication by $a+I$. This map is not generally a ring homomorphism, since it need not preserve one or multiplication, but it is an additive-group homomorphism. Its kernel is the annihilator of $a+I$ in the quotient. The terminology merely names the already visible collection of factors that multiply it to zero.

In the modulo-eighteen example, the differences are multiples of three, producing a kernel with six elements. Every nonempty fiber of an additive homomorphism is a coset of its kernel, so every solvable right-hand side has the same number of solutions. This reconnects the quotient-ring calculation to group theory: existence is ideal membership, while multiplicity is a coset count.

The distinction between additive and ring homomorphisms in this example is deliberate. Multiplication by six preserves sums, but it sends one to six, so it is not an identity-preserving ring homomorphism. A map may be useful without preserving every piece of structure. The task is to state exactly which structure it preserves and apply the corresponding theorem, rather than labeling every arithmetic-looking map a ring homomorphism.

## 14.21 Why quotient notation needs an ambient ring

The symbol $(a)$ means all multiples of $a$ with coefficients in the current ring. Thus $(2)$ is a proper ideal in the integers but is the whole ring in the rationals, because $1=2(1/2)$. A quotient expression without an ambient ring can therefore hide a fundamental ambiguity. Similarly, a class represented by an integer may be nonzero modulo one ideal and zero modulo a larger ideal. Keep the ring and ideal visible until the context is secure. Most quotient mistakes can be traced to forgetting either the allowed coefficient set, the representative-independence check, or the distinction between the zero element upstairs and the zero class downstairs. Each of these has a direct test in the definitions above.
