# Chapter 10 — Permutations, parity and alternating groups

A rearrangement of a finite set is a reversible operation. We can perform two rearrangements in succession, undo one, or ask which rearrangements are generated by a few basic moves. This makes permutations a rich source of concrete groups. More surprisingly, every finite group can be represented by permutations, so this chapter supplies a universal language for finite algebra.

We will learn two descriptions of a permutation: a table that records every output, and cycles that reveal the repeated motion. From cycles we will compute orders. From transpositions we will construct the sign homomorphism and the alternating group. Finally, the alternating group on four symbols will show why the converse of Lagrange's theorem fails.

The prerequisites are the group, order, and quotient ideas developed in Chapters 6–9. We need no linear algebra. A permutation is just a bijective function, and its multiplication is function composition. Throughout, $\sigma\tau$ means first apply $\tau$, then apply $\sigma$. Keeping that convention visible is more important than calculating quickly.

## 1. The symmetric group

A **permutation** of a set $X$ is a bijection $X\to X$. The permutations form a group under composition: composites of bijections are bijections, composition is associative, the identity function is an identity, and inverse functions supply inverses. Chapter 6 checked these statements for arbitrary sets. For $X=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}$, the group is written $S_n$ and called the **symmetric group**.

There are $n!$ permutations, where $n!=n(n-1)\cdots2\cdot1$. To count, choose the image of $1$ in $n$ ways, then the image of $2$ in $n-1$ remaining ways, and continue. Every bijection arises exactly once from such a sequence of choices. We take $0!=1$, although our main examples use positive $n$.

A two-row display records all values:

$$
\sigma=\begin{pmatrix}1&2&3&4&5\\3&5&4&1&2\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Thus $\sigma(1)=3$, $\sigma(2)=5$, and so on. Each symbol occurs exactly once in the bottom row, expressing bijectivity. A function table with a repeated output is not a permutation.

To compose permutations, follow one input through the rightmost map and then the leftmost. For $\alpha=(1\ 2\ 3)$ and $\beta=(1\ 2)$, whose cycle notation will be explained shortly, $\alpha\beta$ sends $1$ to $2$ under $\beta$ and then to $3$ under $\alpha$. It sends $3$ to $3$ and then to $1$, while $2$ returns to itself. Hence $\alpha\beta=(1\ 3)$. Reversing the factors gives $(2\ 3)$ instead. The group $S_3$ is therefore nonabelian.

The labels themselves carry no numerical arithmetic in this operation. The expression $\sigma(3)=4$ says where the symbol three is sent; it does not mean that we add one to every label. A permutation is determined by its specified assignments, not by a guessed numerical formula.

## 2. Cycles record repeated motion

A cycle $(a_1\ a_2\ \cdots\ a_k)$ sends $a_1$ to $a_2$, each following listed symbol to the next, and $a_k$ back to $a_1$. It fixes every symbol not listed. For example, $(1\ 4\ 3)$ sends $1\mapsto4\mapsto3\mapsto1$, while fixing $2$ and $5$ when regarded as an element of $S_5$.

The same cycle can be written starting at a different point: $(1\ 4\ 3)=(4\ 3\ 1)=(3\ 1\ 4)$. Reversing the order generally changes the permutation: $(1\ 3\ 4)$ is the inverse of $(1\ 4\ 3)$. A one-symbol cycle fixes its symbol, so it is often omitted from notation. When computing cycle type or counting all moved and fixed symbols, keep track of those omitted fixed points.

A **transposition** is a two-cycle $(a\ b)$. It exchanges two distinct symbols and fixes all others. A transposition is its own inverse and has order two. The empty product of cycles denotes the identity permutation.

To find the disjoint cycles of a permutation, start with one unused symbol and follow its repeated images until it returns. Because the set is finite and the function is bijective, it must return to its starting symbol rather than entering a different already traversed point. Then start with another unused symbol and repeat.

For the two-row example above, following $1$ gives $1\mapsto3\mapsto4\mapsto1$, while following $2$ gives $2\mapsto5\mapsto2$. Therefore

$$
\sigma=(1\ 3\ 4)(2\ 5).
$$

The cycles are disjoint: they involve no common symbols. Their order in this product can be exchanged, because each acts only on symbols fixed by the other.

## 3. Why disjoint cycle decomposition is unique

Starting from a symbol $x$, its repeated images under a fixed permutation are determined. The set of symbols visited before returning to $x$ cannot be altered by choosing a different notation. These sets either agree or are disjoint: if the repeated paths meet, bijectivity lets us trace backward and see that they belong to the same cycle.

Thus the underlying cycle sets and their cyclic directions are fixed by the permutation. The remaining choices are the starting point within each cycle and the order in which disjoint cycles are displayed. This is the precise meaning of uniqueness of disjoint cycle decomposition. It does not say that a cycle has only one written string.

Disjoint cycles commute, but overlapping cycles usually do not. For example, $(1\ 2)$ and $(3\ 4)$ commute because each fixes the two symbols moved by the other. The transpositions $(1\ 2)$ and $(2\ 3)$ overlap, and their two composition orders give different three-cycles. When simplifying a product, first identify whether the cycles are disjoint before exchanging them.

A product already written in cycles need not be a disjoint cycle decomposition. The expression $(1\ 2\ 3)(2\ 4)$ is valid notation for a composition, but the cycles overlap. To compute powers or orders efficiently, convert the total permutation into disjoint cycles by tracing inputs through the entire product. Applying a formula to the listed factor lengths before this conversion would be unjustified.

## 4. Powers, inverses, and orders

A cycle of length $k$ has order $k$. After $m$ applications, a symbol moves $m$ positions around the cycle and returns exactly when $k\mid m$. The inverse cycle traverses the same symbols backward:

$$
(a_1\ a_2\ \cdots\ a_k)^{-1}=(a_k\ a_{k-1}\ \cdots\ a_1).
$$

For a product of disjoint cycles, a power is the product of the corresponding powers, because the cycles commute. The permutation returns to the identity exactly when every cycle returns. Hence its order is the least common multiple of its disjoint cycle lengths.

For $\sigma=(1\ 3\ 4)(2\ 5)$, the order is $\operatorname{lcm}(3,2)=6$. Its square is $(1\ 4\ 3)$, because the transposition squares to the identity. Its cube is $(2\ 5)$, because the three-cycle cubes to the identity. Its fifth power is its inverse because five is congruent to minus one modulo six.

A power of a single cycle can split into several cycles. Let $c=(1\ 2\ 3\ 4\ 5\ 6)$. The square advances by two positions, producing $c^2=(1\ 3\ 5)(2\ 4\ 6)$. The cube gives $c^3=(1\ 4)(2\ 5)(3\ 6)$. In general, $c^m$ has $\gcd(k,m)$ cycles, each of length $k/\gcd(k,m)$.

To justify the general statement, label positions by classes modulo $k$. Applying $c^m$ adds $m$. The additive subgroup generated by $[m]$ has size $k/\gcd(k,m)$, and its cosets partition the positions into $\gcd(k,m)$ cycles. Thus an earlier theorem about cyclic groups directly explains the permutation calculation.

An element of $S_n$ can have order larger than $n$. In $S_7$, a disjoint three-cycle and four-cycle have combined order twelve. The order measures the common return time, not the number of available symbols. On the other hand, every element order divides $n!$ by Lagrange, consistent with the least-common-multiple formula.

## 5. Building every permutation from swaps

A cycle can be decomposed into transpositions by

$$
(a_1\ a_2\ \cdots\ a_k)
=(a_1\ a_k)(a_1\ a_{k-1})\cdots(a_1\ a_2).
$$

The rightmost transposition sends $a_1$ to $a_2$, which the remaining factors fix. A middle symbol $a_j$ is first sent to $a_1$ by its transposition and then to $a_{j+1}$ by the next relevant factor. The last symbol $a_k$ is sent to $a_1$. Symbols outside the cycle remain fixed, proving the formula by checking all possible inputs.

For example, $(1\ 2\ 3\ 4)=(1\ 4)(1\ 3)(1\ 2)$. Every permutation is a product of disjoint cycles, and every cycle is a product of transpositions, so transpositions generate $S_n$.

The decomposition is far from unique. We can insert $(a\ b)(a\ b)=e$ anywhere in a product, changing the number of transpositions by two without changing the permutation. More complicated rewritings are possible too. It is therefore not automatic that the parity of the number of transpositions is meaningful. We must prove that no permutation can be written both as an even number and as an odd number of transpositions.

This is a representative-independence problem of a familiar kind. The same permutation has many expressions, and we want to assign a value using any expression. Before defining that value, we need a theorem that different expressions produce the same result.

## 6. Inversions give a well-defined parity

For $\sigma\in S_n$, an **inversion** is a pair of positions $i<j$ with $\sigma(i)>\sigma(j)$. Let $I(\sigma)$ be the number of inversions in the bottom row of its two-row display. The quantity depends only on the permutation, since it is computed directly from its values.

For the row $3,1,4,2$, the inversions are the pairs of entries $(3,1)$, $(3,2)$, and $(4,2)$, so there are three. Define the **sign** by $\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)=(-1)^{I(\sigma)}$, either $1$ or $-1$. A permutation is **even** if its sign is $1$, and **odd** if its sign is $-1$.

Swapping entries in two adjacent positions changes the inversion count by exactly one in absolute value. Their comparison with each other reverses. For every outside entry, the total number of comparisons it contributes with those two entries stays the same, since the same two values remain on the same side of that outside position. Therefore the sign flips.

Swapping entries in arbitrary positions also flips the sign. Such a swap can be implemented by an odd number of adjacent swaps: move the left entry to the right position using $d$ adjacent swaps, then move the original right entry back to the left position using $d-1$, where $d$ is the distance between positions. The total is $2d-1$, an odd number. Each adjacent swap flips sign, so the net effect is a sign change.

Right multiplication by a transposition swaps the two input positions in the value row of a permutation. Hence multiplying by any transposition changes the sign. Starting from the identity, which has no inversions and sign $1$, a product of $r$ transpositions has sign $(-1)^r$. Consequently every transposition decomposition of the same permutation has the same parity. This proves the desired independence.

If $\tau$ is written as a product of $r$ transpositions, applying those factors one at a time on the right of $\sigma$ gives

$$
\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma\tau)=(-1)^r\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)
=\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\operatorname{sgn}(\tau).
$$

Thus sign is a homomorphism from $S_n$ to the multiplicative group $\{1,-1\}$. The proof has established both the invariant and its compatibility with composition.

## 7. Reading sign from cycles

A $k$-cycle is a product of $k-1$ transpositions, so its sign is $(-1)^{k-1}$. In particular, an odd-length cycle is even, and an even-length cycle is odd. The adjectives refer to different things: odd length means the cycle contains an odd number of symbols, while even permutation means its transposition count has even parity.

For disjoint cycle lengths $k_1,\ldots,k_r$, the sign is $(-1)^{\sum_i(k_i-1)}$. Including one-cycles, their lengths sum to $n$, giving the equivalent formula $(-1)^{n-r}$. Fixed points contribute zero to the exponent, so omitting them from the written decomposition does not alter the sign calculation if the remaining formula is used consistently.

For $\sigma=(1\ 3\ 4)(2\ 5)$, the three-cycle is even and the transposition is odd, so $\sigma$ is odd. Its order is six, but order and sign are different invariants. A product of two disjoint transpositions is even and has order two. A three-cycle is even and has order three. Knowing sign alone does not determine order.

The inverse of a permutation has the same sign, because $1=\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma\sigma^{-1})$ and each sign is its own inverse. Conjugate permutations also have the same sign, since the signs of the conjugating element and its inverse cancel. These facts follow from the homomorphism law without counting inversions again.

## 8. The alternating group

The **alternating group** $A_n$ is the subgroup of even permutations in $S_n$. It is the kernel of the sign homomorphism, so it is normal. For $n\ge2$, a transposition has sign minus one, making the sign map surjective. The first isomorphism theorem gives

$$
S_n/A_n\cong\{1,-1\},\qquad |A_n|=\frac{n!}{2}.
$$

For $n=1$, both $S_1$ and $A_1$ are trivial, so the half-factorial count is not used. The condition $n\ge2$ is where a transposition first exists.

The group $A_3$ consists of $e,(1\ 2\ 3),(1\ 3\ 2)$ and is cyclic of order three. The group $A_4$ has twelve elements: the identity; eight three-cycles, obtained by choosing three of four symbols and one of two cyclic directions; and three products of two disjoint transpositions. These cycle types exhaust the even possibilities on four symbols.

The subgroup $A_n$ is generated by three-cycles for $n\ge3$. Every even permutation is a product of an even number of transpositions. Group the transpositions into pairs. If the pair is identical it cancels. If the two share one symbol, their product is a three-cycle. If they are disjoint, use

$$
(a\ b)(c\ d)=(a\ c\ b)(a\ c\ d).
$$

Checking the four symbols verifies the identity. Thus each pair can be replaced by a product of three-cycles. Conversely, every three-cycle is even, so the subgroup they generate stays inside $A_n$. Together these inclusions prove the generation statement.

Generation by three-cycles is a structural fact, not a claim that every even permutation is a single three-cycle. A double transposition, for instance, needs the two three-cycles in the displayed formula. This distinction will be useful when studying normal subgroups and solvability later in the course.

## 9. Conjugation relabels cycles

For any $\sigma\in S_n$,

$$
\sigma(a_1\ a_2\ \cdots\ a_k)\sigma^{-1}
=(\sigma(a_1)\ \sigma(a_2)\ \cdots\ \sigma(a_k)).
$$

To prove it, start with the input $\sigma(a_i)$. Applying $\sigma^{-1}$ gives $a_i$, the cycle gives $a_{i+1}$, and applying $\sigma$ gives $\sigma(a_{i+1})$. Inputs outside the displayed cycle remain fixed. The formula therefore follows by evaluating all input types.

Conjugation changes the labels of a cycle but not its length. The multiset of disjoint cycle lengths, including fixed points, is called the **cycle type**. Conjugate permutations in $S_n$ have the same cycle type. Conversely, if two permutations have the same cycle type, pair their cycles of equal lengths and choose a relabeling sending each symbol in the first cycle to the corresponding symbol in its partner. The conjugation formula then sends the first permutation to the second. Hence cycle type classifies conjugacy in $S_n$.

For example, $(1\ 2\ 3)(4\ 5)$ and $(2\ 4\ 5)(1\ 3)$ have the same cycle type. Map $1,2,3$ to $2,4,5$ and $4,5$ to $1,3$, respectively. The resulting relabeling is a bijection and provides an explicit conjugating permutation.

The statement concerns conjugacy in the full symmetric group. Two permutations of the same type need not be conjugate inside a smaller subgroup. In the abelian group $A_3$, conjugation fixes every element, so its two distinct three-cycles are not conjugate inside $A_3$, even though they are conjugate in $S_3$. Always specify the ambient group when discussing conjugation.

## 10. The square group inside a symmetric group

Label square vertices $1,2,3,4$ clockwise. The quarter-turn is $r=(1\ 2\ 3\ 4)$, and reflection through vertices one and three is $s=(2\ 4)$. These permutations satisfy $r^4=s^2=e$ and $sr=r^{-1}s$, giving the same group $D_8$ studied earlier as a subgroup of $S_4$.

The word subgroup is justified because composing or reversing square symmetries again gives a square symmetry. There are eight such permutations, not all twenty-four permutations in $S_4$. A general permutation of vertices need not preserve adjacency, so it need not arise from a rigid motion of the square.

Sign does not coincide with geometric orientation for this vertex representation. The quarter-turn $r$ is an odd permutation because it is a four-cycle, even though it preserves the square's orientation in the geometric sense. The reflection $rs=(1\ 2)(3\ 4)$ is even as a vertex permutation. The two notions answer different questions: parity counts swaps of labels, whereas orientation describes the action on the plane. An analogy between them must not be mistaken for an identity.

The restriction of sign to $D_8$ is still a homomorphism. Its kernel has four elements, since $r$ and $s$ both have negative sign and the image contains both signs. One can list the kernel as $e,r^2,rs,r^3s$. This gives another normal subgroup of the square group and another example of a structural map producing a kernel automatically.

## 11. A small classification: subgroups of S_3

The group $S_3$ has six elements: the identity, three transpositions, and two three-cycles. By Lagrange, subgroup orders can only be $1,2,3,6$. A subgroup of order two is generated by its unique nonidentity element, which must have order two. Thus the three transpositions give exactly three such subgroups.

A subgroup of order three is cyclic and contains a three-cycle together with its inverse. The two three-cycles generate the same subgroup $A_3$, so there is exactly one subgroup of order three. Together with the identity subgroup and the whole group, this lists all six subgroups of $S_3$.

The order-two subgroups are not normal. Conjugating $(1\ 2)$ by $(1\ 2\ 3)$ yields $(2\ 3)$, outside the subgroup generated by $(1\ 2)$. The subgroup $A_3$ is normal because it is a sign kernel, or because it has index two. This example shows how counting, element orders, conjugation, and kernels cooperate to give a complete description.

A subgroup list need not be a list of distinct generators. Two different generators can produce the same cyclic subgroup, as the two three-cycles do here. To avoid overcounting, compare the generated sets or use the cyclic subgroup classification.

## 12. A_4 and the failed converse of Lagrange

Inside $A_4$, consider

$$
V=\{e,(1\ 2)(3\ 4),(1\ 3)(2\ 4),(1\ 4)(2\ 3)\}.
$$

Multiplying any two distinct nonidentity elements gives the third, and each squares to $e$. Thus $V$ is a subgroup isomorphic to $C_2\times C_2$. Conjugation relabels double transpositions to double transpositions, so $V\trianglelefteq A_4$. The quotient has order three and is therefore cyclic.

Although six divides twelve, $A_4$ has no subgroup of order six. Suppose such a subgroup $H$ existed. Its index would be two, so it would be normal, and the quotient $A_4/H$ would have order two. Under the quotient map, every three-cycle must map to the identity: its image has order dividing three, but also dividing two by Lagrange in the quotient. The only common possibility is order one.

Since three-cycles generate $A_4$, the quotient map would send every element to the identity. That would make the quotient trivial, contradicting its two elements. Therefore no such subgroup exists. This is a complete counterexample to the converse of Lagrange, with every required ingredient already proved.

The argument reveals why existence questions need more than divisibility. A proposed subgroup can conflict with how the whole group is generated and with the orders possible in the resulting quotient. Later the Sylow theorems will guarantee some subgroup orders—specifically suitable prime powers—without claiming every divisor occurs.

## 13. Every finite group is a permutation group

Let $G$ be a finite group with $m$ elements. For each $g\in G$, define left translation $L_g:G\to G$ by $L_g(x)=gx$. It is a permutation, with inverse $L_{g^{-1}}$. Define $\rho:G\to S_G$ by $\rho(g)=L_g$, where $S_G$ denotes all permutations of the underlying set $G$.

For all $g,h,x$, we have $(L_gL_h)(x)=g(hx)=(gh)x=L_{gh}(x)$. Hence $\rho$ is a homomorphism. It is injective because $L_g(e)=g$, so equal translations force equal elements. The first isomorphism theorem identifies $G$ with the subgroup $\rho(G)$ of $S_G$. After labeling the $m$ elements by $1,\ldots,m$, this is a subgroup of $S_m$.

This is **Cayley's theorem**. It says every finite group can be represented by permutations in a way that preserves its multiplication exactly. It does not say every group is equal to a symmetric group, nor that the smallest useful representation needs $m$ symbols. The square group has eight elements but already acts faithfully on four vertices, a smaller representation than the general construction provides.

For $G=C_3=\{e,a,a^2\}$, left translation by $a$ cyclically permutes the three elements: $e\mapsto a\mapsto a^2\mapsto e$. The image is the three-element subgroup of $S_3$ generated by that cycle. For a more complicated group, each left translation can still be read directly from one row of its multiplication table.

The theorem explains why studying permutations is not a detour from abstract groups. It gives a universal concrete model. Chapter 11 generalizes this idea to actions on other sets, allowing representations that reveal more information with fewer points.

## 14. Counting permutations by cycle type

To count $k$-cycles in $S_n$, first choose the $k$ symbols, then arrange them in a cyclic order. There are $\binom nk$ choices of symbols. For a chosen set, there are $k!$ linear lists, but each cycle is represented by exactly $k$ rotations of its list. Thus there are $(k-1)!$ cycles on that set, giving

$$
\binom nk(k-1)!=\frac{n!}{k(n-k)!}.
$$

For instance, there are $\binom43\cdot2=8$ three-cycles in $S_4$, matching the earlier list count in $A_4$. There are $\binom42=6$ transpositions in $S_4$, and $3!=6$ four-cycles.

More generally, suppose a cycle type has $m_j$ cycles of length $j$, with $\sum_j jm_j=n$. The number of permutations of that type is

$$
\frac{n!}{\prod_j j^{m_j}m_j!}.
$$

Start with an ordered list of all $n$ symbols and cut it into blocks of the required lengths. Each length-$j$ cycle can be rotated in $j$ ways without changing it, giving the factor $j^{m_j}$. The $m_j$ cycles of the same length can be exchanged among themselves in $m_j!$ ways, giving the second factor. Dividing removes exactly these repeated descriptions.

For type consisting of two two-cycles in $S_4$, the count is $4!/(2^2\cdot2!)=3$. For a three-cycle and two fixed points in $S_5$, the count is $5!/(3\cdot2!)=20$. Including fixed points through $m_1$ ensures the formula accounts for every symbol consistently.

## Sorting measures the number of adjacent swaps

Inversion count is more than a parity device. It is exactly the smallest number of adjacent-position swaps needed to sort a permutation's value row into increasing order. Every adjacent swap changes the inversion count by one in absolute value. Starting from $I$ inversions and ending at zero therefore requires at least $I$ swaps.

That lower bound can be attained. If a value row is not increasing, some adjacent pair is out of order; otherwise every successive entry would be smaller than the next, making the entire row increasing. Swap such an adjacent inverted pair. Its contribution changes from one inversion to zero, and all other inversion contributions remain unchanged in total. The count decreases by exactly one. Repeating eventually reaches zero after exactly $I$ steps, because a nonnegative integer cannot decrease forever. A row with zero inversions is increasing, so it is the identity row.

For example, begin with $3,1,4,2$, whose inversion count is three. Swap the first two entries to get $1,3,4,2$, then swap the last two to get $1,3,2,4$, then swap the middle two to get $1,2,3,4$. Three swaps attain the lower bound. Reversing the sequence writes the original permutation as a product of three adjacent transpositions, confirming its odd sign.

This is the mathematical principle behind a basic sorting procedure. We have not assumed a programming language or an algorithmic library; the proof explains why a local improvement must terminate and why its count is optimal for the permitted move. If arbitrary swaps are allowed instead, a different minimum results, so the move set is part of the problem.

For arbitrary transpositions, the minimum number needed to build a permutation is $n-c$, where $c$ is its number of disjoint cycles including fixed points. The cycle decomposition gives a construction using exactly that many swaps, because a cycle of length $k$ uses $k-1$. For the lower bound, multiplying by a transposition either joins two cycles into one or splits one cycle into two. To see this, follow the two arrows leaving the swapped input positions: exchanging their destinations splices two separate loops together if the positions were in different loops, and cuts one loop into two if they were in the same loop. All other arrows remain unchanged.

Thus each transposition changes the cycle count by exactly one. The identity has $n$ cycles, so reaching a permutation with $c$ cycles needs at least $n-c$ transpositions. The construction meets the bound. In particular, every transposition decomposition has the same parity as $n-c$, giving another route to the sign formula after the cycle-count change has been justified.

Notice the distinction in the four-entry example: the permutation with row $3,1,4,2$ has disjoint cycle $(1\ 3\ 4\ 2)$, so it needs only three arbitrary swaps too. Other examples separate the two counts. The transposition $(1\ 4)$ requires one arbitrary swap but five adjacent swaps, since the row $4,2,3,1$ has five inversions. Parity agrees, while the exact number depends on which operations are allowed.

## Two different binary measurements of square symmetry

There are two natural homomorphisms from $D_8$ to a two-element group. One records whether a symmetry is a rotation or a reflection. Write this map as $o$, with value one on rotations and minus one on reflections. The multiplication relations show that the product of two reflections is a rotation, a rotation times a reflection is a reflection, and two rotations give a rotation. Therefore $o$ is a homomorphism, with kernel $\langle r\rangle$.

The other is the sign of the induced permutation of four vertices. It sends both $r$ and $s$ to minus one, so it is different from $o$. Its kernel was listed above as $\{e,r^2,rs,r^3s\}$. The product of these two numerical homomorphisms is a third homomorphism, since their outputs commute in $\{1,-1\}$. It sends $r$ to minus one and $s$ to one, and its kernel is $\{e,r^2,s,r^2s\}$.

We have thereby found three distinct index-two normal subgroups of the same group. Each corresponds to forgetting a different aspect of the symmetry. This example makes a general point about quotient maps: a group can admit several different compressions of the same size, and the size alone does not determine which information is lost. Explicit kernels distinguish the maps.

It also explains why calling a permutation even is not an informal statement that its motion looks balanced or orientation-preserving. The word even has a precise definition from transposition parity. The geometric orientation map is another legitimate invariant, but its value need not match permutation sign for a selected action. Definitions settle which quantity a particular argument uses.

## A complete cycle-type count on four symbols

The possible cycle types in $S_4$ are four fixed points; one transposition with two fixed points; two disjoint transpositions; one three-cycle with a fixed point; and one four-cycle. Their counts are respectively one, six, three, eight, and six. The sum is twenty-four, so the types exhaust the group without overlap.

Their signs are respectively positive, negative, positive, positive, and negative. Adding the positive counts gives twelve elements in $A_4$, consistent with the kernel calculation. Their orders are respectively one, two, two, three, and four. Thus no permutation in $S_4$ has order six, even though six divides the group size twenty-four. Divisibility is a necessary restriction on element orders, just as it is on subgroup orders; it need not be sufficient.

These three parallel columns—count, sign, and order—answer different questions about the same objects. Count measures how many elements have a type; sign detects the parity homomorphism; order measures return time. Working through the full small example is a way to keep those concepts distinct before the groups grow too large to list by hand.

The **support** of a permutation is the set of symbols it moves. For $(1\ 2\ 3)$ considered as an element of $S_5$, the support has three symbols, the permutation has order three, the ambient group has order one hundred twenty, and the action uses five symbols. These four quantities happen to share some numbers in small examples, but they are conceptually different. Adding extra fixed symbols changes the ambient symmetric group without changing the permutation's nontrivial cycles or its order.

This flexibility is useful when comparing groups. A permutation on the first three symbols can be regarded as an element of $S_4$ or $S_5$ by fixing every new symbol. The resulting inclusion preserves composition and is injective, so it is a group embedding. Its image is the subgroup fixing all the added symbols. Such an embedding does not mean the original symmetric group becomes equal to the larger one; it identifies a particular subgroup inside it. This is the same distinction between a source, its image, and a full target that appeared in Chapter 8.

## 16. What permutations make visible

A disjoint cycle decomposition turns repeated motion into arithmetic of lengths and least common multiples. A transposition decomposition turns rearrangement into parity, and the sign homomorphism makes parity independent of the chosen decomposition. Normal subgroups and quotients then become concrete in $S_3$, $D_8$, and $A_4$.

Use the `permutations` experiment in the [companion](../companion/index.html) to enter one permutation by its image list and inspect its cycles, parity, and order. Compose two permutations by hand first, then enter the resulting image list to check its cycle behavior. Trace a few symbols carefully when the factors do not commute. The experiment checks a finite case, while the proofs establish the general formulas.

In the next chapter, groups will act on sets without requiring the group itself to be all permutations of that set. Orbits and stabilizers will explain how symmetries organize objects, and counting those organized pieces will lead to applications ranging from conjugation to counting colorings.
