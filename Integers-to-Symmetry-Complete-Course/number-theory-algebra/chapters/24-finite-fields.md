# Chapter 24 — Finite fields, Frobenius and polynomial factorization

There is no field $\mathbb Z/4\mathbb Z$: its nonzero class two squares to zero and has no inverse. Nevertheless, a field with four elements does exist. The mistake is to assume that a field of a given size must use ordinary integer multiplication modulo that size. A new field can instead be built from a polynomial quotient.

Our task is to determine exactly which finite sizes are possible, construct the corresponding fields, and understand their arithmetic. The answer is that finite fields have prime-power sizes, one field for each such size up to isomorphism. Their multiplicative groups are cyclic, while their additive groups are vector spaces. The Frobenius map will connect these two descriptions to polynomial factorization.

The prerequisites are polynomial quotients, finite group orders, vector-space bases, and finite field extensions. We prove the existence and uniqueness statements rather than treating a finite-field notation as evidence that the object exists. Our computations use small examples so every reduction can be checked by hand.

## 1. Why the size must be a prime power

The characteristic of a field is the least positive integer $m$ such that $m\cdot1=0$, if such an integer exists; otherwise its characteristic is zero. A finite field cannot have characteristic zero, because the elements $0,1,1+1,\ldots$ would all be distinct.

The positive characteristic must be prime. If $m=ab$ with $1<a,b<m$, then $(a\cdot1)(b\cdot1)=m\cdot1=0$, while neither factor is zero by minimality of $m$. This contradicts the absence of zero divisors. Write the characteristic as $p$.

The map $\mathbb Z\to F$ sending an integer $n$ to $n\cdot1$ has kernel $p\mathbb Z$. Its image is a copy of $\mathbb F_p=\mathbb Z/p\mathbb Z$, called the prime subfield. Thus every finite field is a vector space over $\mathbb F_p$.

Because the field has finitely many elements, choose a basis by adding an element outside the span until all elements are included. If the basis has $n$ members, each coordinate has $p$ choices, so the field has $p^n$ elements. In particular there are no finite fields of sizes six, ten, twelve, or eighteen. This is an impossibility statement, not a failure to find a good multiplication table.

The additive group of a field with $p^n$ elements is $C_p^n$. Every nonzero element has additive order $p$, because adding it $p$ times is multiplication by $p\cdot1=0$. Its multiplicative group excludes zero and has $p^n-1$ elements. The two groups therefore have different sizes and generally different structures, even though they come from the same field operations.

## 2. Constructing and understanding four elements

Over $\mathbb F_2$, the polynomial $f=X^2+X+1$ has no root: its values at zero and one are both one. A reducible quadratic over a field has a linear factor and hence a root, so $f$ is irreducible. Form

$$
\mathbb F_4=\mathbb F_2[X]/(X^2+X+1).
$$

Let $\alpha$ be the class of $X$. Every class has a unique representative $a+bX$ with $a,b\in\mathbb F_2$, so the elements are $0,1,\alpha,1+\alpha$. The defining equation gives $\alpha^2=\alpha+1$, because subtraction and addition coincide in characteristic two.

Addition is coordinatewise modulo two. Thus $\alpha+\alpha=0$ and $(1+\alpha)+\alpha=1$. Multiplication uses the relation. For example,

$$
\alpha(1+\alpha)=\alpha+\alpha^2=\alpha+(\alpha+1)=1.
$$

Therefore the inverse of $\alpha$ is $1+\alpha$. Also $(1+\alpha)^2=1+\alpha^2=\alpha$, since the middle term $2\alpha$ vanishes. The three nonzero elements form a cyclic group generated by $\alpha$, with powers $1,\alpha,1+\alpha,1$.

This field is not the ring of integers modulo four. Its characteristic is two, so $1+1=0$. In $\mathbb Z/4\mathbb Z$, the characteristic is four. An isomorphism must preserve one and addition, so these rings cannot be isomorphic. Counting four elements does not specify the operations.

The construction also distinguishes symbols from representatives. The symbol $\alpha$ is a residue class of polynomials, not an ordinary integer. The expression $\alpha^2=\alpha+1$ is an exact identity in the quotient. We are not assigning a decimal approximation to a complex root, and there is no ordering compatible with the field operations.

## 3. Eight and nine elements: two useful contrasts

Over $\mathbb F_2$, the cubic $X^3+X+1$ has neither zero nor one as a root. A reducible cubic has a linear factor, so the polynomial is irreducible. Let

$$
E=\mathbb F_2[X]/(X^3+X+1),\qquad \alpha=[X].
$$

There are eight elements, represented uniquely by $a+b\alpha+c\alpha^2$. The relation is $\alpha^3=\alpha+1$. Successive powers are

| Exponent | Reduced power |
|---|---|
| $0$ | $1$ |
| $1$ | $\alpha$ |
| $2$ | $\alpha^2$ |
| $3$ | $\alpha+1$ |
| $4$ | $\alpha^2+\alpha$ |
| $5$ | $\alpha^2+\alpha+1$ |
| $6$ | $\alpha^2+1$ |
| $7$ | $1$ |

For instance, multiply the exponent-five expression by $\alpha$: the result is $\alpha^3+\alpha^2+\alpha=(\alpha+1)+\alpha^2+\alpha=\alpha^2+1$. The table lists seven distinct nonzero elements, so $\alpha$ generates the multiplicative group. Inverses can be read by completing exponents to seven: $(\alpha^2)^{-1}=\alpha^5$.

For nine elements, use $\mathbb F_3[X]/(X^2+1)$. The squares in $\mathbb F_3$ are zero and one, so $-1=2$ is not a square and the polynomial is irreducible. With $\beta=[X]$, we have $\beta^2=-1=2$ and every element has form $a+b\beta$ with coefficients modulo three.

The element $\beta$ generates the field over $\mathbb F_3$, but its multiplicative order is four, since $\beta^2=-1$ and $\beta^4=1$. The multiplicative group has eight elements, so $\beta$ does not generate that group. In contrast, $1+\beta$ has square $2\beta$, fourth power two, and eighth power one. Its fourth power is not one, so its order is eight.

An element generating a finite field as a field need not generate its multiplicative group. A primitive element of the multiplicative group means an element of order $q-1$ in a field of size $q$. A polynomial whose root is such an element is called primitive; irreducibility alone does not guarantee primitiveness. The quadratic $X^2+1$ over $\mathbb F_3$ is a concrete counterexample.

## 4. Creating splitting fields without assuming an algebraic closure

A splitting field of a polynomial over $F$ is an extension generated by its roots in which the polynomial factors completely into linear factors. Such an extension can be constructed by finitely many adjunctions. If a polynomial has an irreducible factor $g$ of degree greater than one, the quotient $F[X]/(g)$ is a field containing a root of $g$, hence a root of the original polynomial.

In that larger field, divide out a linear factor and repeat for the remaining polynomial. Each step removes at least one required linear factor. After finitely many steps, all roots are present. The extension generated by the roots is finite because each adjunction is algebraic of finite degree and the tower law multiplies finitely many finite numbers.

This construction establishes existence without presupposing that all roots already lie in some mysterious larger universe. We may perform our argument inside the resulting splitting field. Later, if we need roots of two polynomials together, adjoin roots of their product and work in a common splitting field.

We also need to recognize repeated roots. The formal derivative of $f(X)=\sum a_iX^i$ is $f'(X)=\sum_{i\geq1}i a_iX^{i-1}$. This is an algebraic operation on coefficients; no limits or calculus are involved. Expanding products of monomials proves the product rule $(fg)'=f'g+fg'$.

If $f=(X-a)^rh$ with $h(a)\ne0$, then a repeated root with $r\geq2$ makes both $f(a)$ and $f'(a)$ zero. Conversely, if $f(a)=0$, write $f=(X-a)h$; then $f'(a)=h(a)$. Thus $f'(a)=0$ implies another factor $X-a$ divides $h$. A polynomial has no repeated roots in a splitting field precisely when it is coprime to its derivative.

This criterion works in positive characteristic too, but derivatives can look unfamiliar there. The derivative of $X^p$ is zero in characteristic $p$, because its coefficient $p$ is zero. Consequently the derivative of $X^{p^n}-X$ is $-1$. That constant never vanishes, so this polynomial has only simple roots.

## 5. Existence for every prime-power size

Fix a prime $p$ and positive integer $n$, and set $q=p^n$. Take a splitting field $L$ of $P(X)=X^q-X$ over $\mathbb F_p$. The derivative calculation shows that its $q$ roots are distinct. Let $S$ be their set. We will prove that $S$ itself is a field.

First observe the characteristic-$p$ identity

$$
(x+y)^p=x^p+y^p.
$$

The binomial coefficients $\binom pk$ for $0<k<p$ are divisible by $p$: the numerator contains the prime $p$, while the factorial denominators contain no factor $p$. Thus all middle terms vanish. Iterating the identity gives $(x+y)^q=x^q+y^q$. The corresponding product identity $(xy)^q=x^qy^q$ follows from commutativity.

If $x,y\in S$, then $x^q=x$ and $y^q=y$. Hence $(x+y)^q=x+y$ and $(xy)^q=xy$, so sums and products stay in $S$. Zero and one are roots. Negatives stay in $S$, either by the same power identity or because the map $x\mapsto x^q$ is additive and fixes zero. If $x\ne0$, then $(x^{-1})^q=(x^q)^{-1}=x^{-1}$, so inverses stay in $S$.

Therefore $S$ is a subfield of $L$ with exactly $q$ elements. Because a splitting field is generated by the roots and $S$ already is a field containing all of them, $L=S$. We have constructed a field of every size $p^n$.

The proof is not circular. We did not assume an irreducible polynomial of every degree existed in order to construct the field. We constructed a splitting field using whatever irreducible factors appear along the way, then identified its root set as the desired field. The existence of irreducible polynomials of every positive degree will follow afterward.

For $p=2,n=2$, this gives the roots of $X^4-X$. In characteristic two the polynomial factors as $X(X+1)(X^2+X+1)$. Adjoining a root of the irreducible quadratic supplies exactly the four-element field already built explicitly. The abstract root-set construction and the concrete quotient construction agree.

## 6. Why the field is unique up to isomorphism

Let $E$ be any finite field of size $q$. Its nonzero multiplicative group has $q-1$ elements, so Lagrange gives $a^{q-1}=1$ for every nonzero $a$. Thus every element satisfies $a^q=a$, including zero. Since $X^q-X$ has degree $q$, the elements of $E$ are exactly all its roots. Therefore every field of size $q$ is a splitting field of that polynomial over its prime subfield.

We give the relevant uniqueness argument explicitly. Suppose $E$ and $E'$ both have size $q$, and begin with the natural isomorphism between their copies of $\mathbb F_p$. Suppose an embedding of an intermediate field $K\subseteq E$ into $E'$ has already been defined. Choose a root $\alpha$ of $X^q-X$ not yet included in $K$.

Let $m$ be its minimal polynomial over $K$. It divides $X^q-X$ in $K[X]$. Transport its coefficients through the existing embedding, obtaining a polynomial $m^*$ over the image field. It still divides $X^q-X$, because the latter has coefficients in $\mathbb F_p$ that are fixed. This polynomial splits in $E'$, so $m^*$ has a root $\beta\in E'$.

The formula $g(\alpha)\mapsto g^*(\beta)$ extends the embedding to $K(\alpha)$. It is well-defined because two expressions differ by a multiple of $m$, whose transported polynomial vanishes at $\beta$. Equivalently, both simple fields are polynomial quotients by corresponding irreducible polynomials. A field homomorphism preserving one is injective, since its kernel is a proper ideal in a field and therefore zero.

Repeat until all roots generating $E$ have been included. Only finitely many roots are needed, so this gives an embedding $E\to E'$. Both fields have $q$ elements, so the injective map is surjective. Hence they are isomorphic. Uniqueness here means uniqueness of isomorphism type, not a preferred labeling of every element or a unique isomorphism between two displayed constructions.

We may now write $\mathbb F_q$ without ambiguity about the abstract field. Different irreducible-polynomial representations may require a nontrivial coordinate change to identify their elements. The notation does not say that their chosen residue classes of $X$ must correspond.

## 7. The nonzero elements form a cyclic group

The multiplicative group $H=\mathbb F_q^\times$ is finite and abelian. If $q=2$, then $H=\{1\}$ is the trivial cyclic group, generated by $1$; handle this case separately. For $q>2$, by Chapter 21, it has invariant factors $C_{d_1}\times\cdots\times C_{d_r}$ with $d_1\mid\cdots\mid d_r$. Its exponent is $e=d_r$, and there is an element of order exactly $e$.

Every element satisfies $X^e-1=0$. A nonzero polynomial of degree $e$ over a field has at most $e$ roots, so $|H|\leq e$. But an element of order $e$ contributes $e$ distinct group elements, so $e\leq|H|$. Equality follows. An element of order $e=|H|=q-1$ generates all of $H$, proving cyclicity.

The polynomial root bound is the additional restriction that an arbitrary finite abelian group need not satisfy. The units modulo eight give a noncyclic group of order four, but they live in a ring with zero divisors. Their behavior does not contradict the finite-field theorem.

If $g$ is primitive, the primitive elements are exactly $g^k$ with $\gcd(k,q-1)=1$. Therefore there are $\varphi(q-1)$ of them. This is a group-theoretic count, not a claim that every possible quotient generator is primitive. In $\mathbb F_9$, the chosen root of $X^2+1$ had order four, while $1+\beta$ had order eight.

A primitive multiplicative element also generates the field over its prime subfield: a subfield containing $g$ contains every power of $g$, hence every nonzero element and zero. Its minimal polynomial over $\mathbb F_p$ has degree $n$. This proves that for every positive $n$ there exists an irreducible polynomial of degree $n$ over $\mathbb F_p$.

## 8. Frobenius organizes all the conjugates

In characteristic $p$, define the Frobenius map $\sigma(x)=x^p$. The binomial identity proves that it preserves addition, and powers preserve multiplication and one. It is injective: $x^p=y^p$ implies $(x-y)^p=0$, hence $x=y$ in a field. On a finite set, injectivity implies surjectivity, so Frobenius is an automorphism.

On $\mathbb F_{p^n}$, its $n$th iterate is the identity because $x^{p^n}=x$. No smaller positive iterate can be the identity: if $\sigma^k$ fixed every element for $0<k<n$, then all $p^n$ elements would be roots of $X^{p^k}-X$, whose degree is only $p^k$. Thus Frobenius has order exactly $n$.

The fixed elements of Frobenius are the roots of $X^p-X$. There are exactly $p$ of them, and the prime subfield already supplies $p$ roots. Thus its fixed field is precisely $\mathbb F_p$. This conclusion is a direct root count; the general Galois correspondence is not being used before it has been taught.

For $\alpha\in\mathbb F_{p^n}$, apply Frobenius repeatedly until the element returns. If its orbit length is $r$, the elements $\alpha,\alpha^p,\ldots,\alpha^{p^{r-1}}$ are distinct. Their product polynomial

$$
g(X)=\prod_{i=0}^{r-1}(X-\alpha^{p^i})
$$

has coefficients fixed by Frobenius, since Frobenius cyclically permutes the factors. Therefore its coefficients lie in $\mathbb F_p$. The minimal polynomial of $\alpha$ divides $g$, giving degree at most $r$.

Conversely, if the minimal degree is $d$, the field $\mathbb F_p(\alpha)$ has $p^d$ elements, so its Frobenius iterate $\sigma^d$ fixes $\alpha$. Hence $r\leq d$. The two inequalities give $r=d$, and $g$ is exactly the minimal polynomial. Its roots are the Frobenius orbit. This proves that every irreducible polynomial over a finite field is separable: its roots in its splitting field are distinct.

## 9. Subfields are controlled by divisibility

If $K\subseteq\mathbb F_{p^n}$ is a subfield, then $|K|=p^d$ for some $d$. Its dimension over $\mathbb F_p$ is $d$, and the tower law gives $d\mid n$. Thus subfield sizes have a divisibility restriction, just as subgroup orders do.

Conversely, suppose $d\mid n$. Work in a common splitting field of $X^{p^d}-X$ and $X^{p^n}-X$. The first polynomial has $p^d$ distinct roots, and any such root satisfies $x^{p^d}=x$. Iterating this identity $n/d$ times gives $x^{p^n}=x$. Hence every root of the first polynomial already belongs to $\mathbb F_{p^n}$, the full root set of the second. Its roots form a subfield with $p^d$ elements by the same closure argument used in the existence proof.

This subfield is unique inside the specified field, because any subfield of that size consists exactly of the roots of $X^{p^d}-X$. There is therefore exactly one subfield for each positive divisor $d$ of $n$, and no others.

For $\mathbb F_{64}$, where $64=2^6$, the subfields have sizes $2,4,8,64$. The four-element field is not contained in the eight-element field because two does not divide three. Their intersection is the two-element field, since an intersection is a subfield whose degree divides both two and three. A diagram of containment must reflect divisibility of extension degrees rather than ordinary comparison of the field sizes.

More generally the fixed field of $\sigma^k$ has size $p^{\gcd(n,k)}$. An element fixed by both $\sigma^n$ and $\sigma^k$ is fixed by $\sigma^{\gcd(n,k)}$, using Bezout and inverse powers of the automorphism. The converse follows because the gcd divides both exponents. The unique root field for that divisor has the stated size.

## 10. A subfield calculation inside sixteen elements

We can make the subfield theorem concrete without drawing a large addition table. The polynomial $f=X^4+X+1$ is irreducible over $\mathbb F_2$. It has no roots in $\mathbb F_2$. If a rootless quartic over this field were reducible, it would be a product of two irreducible quadratics. There is only one monic irreducible quadratic over $\mathbb F_2$, namely $X^2+X+1$, and its square is $X^4+X^2+1$, not $f$. Thus no such factorization exists.

Let $a$ be the class of $X$ in the resulting sixteen-element field. Its powers satisfy $a^4=a+1$. The multiplicative group has order fifteen, so the possible orders of $a$ divide fifteen. It is not one, since $a$ is not the class of one. It is not three, because $a^3-1$ is a nonzero polynomial of degree below four and therefore cannot vanish at $a$. It is not five, since

$$
a^5=a(a+1)=a^2+a\ne1.
$$

The final inequality again follows from uniqueness of reduced polynomial representatives. Therefore $a$ has order fifteen and is primitive. This checks primitiveness rather than inferring it from irreducibility.

Put $b=a^5$. Its order is three, because the order of $a^k$ in a cyclic group of order fifteen is $15/\gcd(15,k)$. Hence $b^3=1$ and $b\ne1$. Factoring $b^3-1=(b-1)(b^2+b+1)$ and using the field property gives $b^2+b+1=0$. The four elements $0,1,b,1+b$ therefore form a copy of $\mathbb F_4$ inside this particular $\mathbb F_{16}$.

This is the unique four-element subfield promised by the theorem. It consists exactly of the elements fixed by the second Frobenius iterate, because those elements solve $x^4=x$. The element $a$ itself is not fixed by that iterate: $a^4=a+1$, which differs from $a$. The subfield is present without containing every convenient coordinate generator of the larger field.

Now put $c=a^3$, an element of multiplicative order five. Its Frobenius orbit is $c,c^2,c^4,c^8=c^3$, returning to $c$ after four steps. No earlier return occurs because two has order four modulo five: its powers are $2,4,3,1$. Thus the minimal polynomial of $c$ has degree four. Since $c^5=1$ and $c\ne1$, it satisfies

$$
c^4+c^3+c^2+c+1=0.
$$

The monic degree-four polynomial is therefore its minimal polynomial and is irreducible over $\mathbb F_2$. This is another irreducibility proof obtained from group orders and Frobenius rather than direct coefficient comparisons.

The same reasoning helps organize larger subfield diagrams. Inside $\mathbb F_{2^{12}}$, a subfield of size sixteen has degree four and one of size sixty-four has degree six. Their intersection is fixed by the subgroup of Frobenius powers generated by the fourth and sixth powers, hence is the degree-two subfield with four elements. Alternatively, use the unique root fields and Bezout on the two exponents. The smallest subfield containing both must have degree divisible by both four and six, hence by twelve, so it is the full field of size 4096.

This computation requires that both subfields sit in the same ambient field. Saying that two abstract finite fields have some common elements is meaningless until embeddings identify them. Uniqueness of the subfield inside a specified finite field gives exactly the setting needed to speak of their intersection and their generated field.

## 11. Why finite fields have no inseparable irreducibles

The Frobenius-orbit proof already established separability, but the derivative viewpoint gives a second useful explanation. Suppose an irreducible polynomial over a finite field had a repeated root. It would have a nonconstant gcd with its derivative. Because the polynomial is irreducible and the derivative has lower degree, that can happen only if the derivative is zero.

In characteristic $p$, a polynomial has derivative zero exactly when every nonzero term has exponent divisible by $p$. Write it as $f(X)=a_0+a_1X^p+\cdots+a_rX^{rp}$. Frobenius is surjective on the finite coefficient field, so each $a_i$ has a $p$th root $b_i$ there. The characteristic-$p$ power identity then gives

$$
f(X)=(b_0+b_1X+\cdots+b_rX^r)^p.
$$

A positive-degree polynomial of this form is reducible unless its degree is zero; in particular it cannot be irreducible of degree greater than one. This contradiction proves the assertion. Linear irreducibles have derivative one and already are separable.

A field for which every irreducible polynomial is separable is called perfect. Every finite field is perfect. Fields of characteristic zero are also perfect, since the derivative of a positive-degree irreducible cannot vanish identically there, and the same gcd argument applies. These two important families will cover many examples in the next chapter.

Positive characteristic alone does not imply perfection. In the rational-function field $\mathbb F_p(t)$, the indeterminate $t$ has no $p$th root in the field. The polynomial $X^p-t$ is irreducible, for example by Eisenstein at $t$ in $\mathbb F_p[t]$ followed by Gauss's lemma, but its derivative is zero. In a field containing a root $u$, it becomes $(X-u)^p$ and has just one distinct root. This is the basic example of inseparability. The obstruction disappears in a finite field because Frobenius there is surjective.

Notice the different uses of finiteness in our arguments. It gave a finite basis and therefore a prime-power size. It turned injectivity of Frobenius into surjectivity. It also turned an injective embedding between two equally sized fields into an isomorphism. These are separate steps. Replacing a finite field by an infinite field of the same characteristic requires revisiting each one rather than keeping the conclusions automatically.

## 12. Factoring polynomials by Frobenius tests

A monic irreducible polynomial of degree $d$ over $\mathbb F_p$ divides $X^{p^n}-X$ exactly when $d\mid n$. If it divides, one root lies in $\mathbb F_{p^n}$, so its degree divides $n$ by the tower law. Conversely, its root field has size $p^d$ and embeds as the unique subfield for that divisor. Every root then satisfies the larger power equation.

Because the derivative of $X^{p^n}-X$ is minus one, each irreducible factor occurs once. Hence

$$
X^{p^n}-X=\prod_{d\mid n}\ \prod_{\substack{f\text{ monic irreducible}\\\deg f=d}}f(X).
$$

Taking degrees gives $p^n=\sum_{d\mid n}dN_d$, where $N_d$ is the number of monic irreducibles of degree $d$. Mobius inversion from Chapter 19 yields

$$
N_n=\frac1n\sum_{d\mid n}\mu(d)p^{n/d}.
$$

For $p=2$, this gives $N_1=2$, $N_2=(4-2)/2=1$, $N_3=(8-2)/3=2$, and $N_4=(16-4)/4=3$. Counting irreducibles is thus another meeting point between polynomial algebra and arithmetic functions.

For an actual squarefree polynomial $f$, the gcd $\gcd(f,X^{p^k}-X)$ collects exactly those irreducible factors whose degrees divide $k$. Large powers can be reduced modulo $f$ by repeated squaring, so one need not write out the enormous polynomial in full. This gives the conceptual basis of practical factorization algorithms, though we are not implementing a full optimized factorizer here.

For example, over $\mathbb F_2$, take $f=X^4+X$. Its derivative is one, so it is squarefree. The gcd with $X^2-X=X^2+X$ is $X^2+X=X(X+1)$. Dividing leaves $X^2+X+1$, an irreducible quadratic. Thus $f=X(X+1)(X^2+X+1)$, agreeing with the four-element field construction.

Repeated factors require a preliminary check. The polynomial $X^4+X^2+1=(X^2+X+1)^2$ over $\mathbb F_2$ has derivative zero. Ignoring that fact would cause a factorization routine to lose multiplicity information. Formal derivatives and gcds are exact algebraic tools for detecting this issue.

## 13. Trace, norm and computation limits

For $E=\mathbb F_{p^n}$, define the trace and norm to the prime field by

$$
\operatorname{Tr}_{E/\mathbb F_p}(a)=a+a^p+\cdots+a^{p^{n-1}},\qquad
N_{E/\mathbb F_p}(a)=a^{1+p+\cdots+p^{n-1}}.
$$

The second expression is the product of the Frobenius conjugates. Frobenius cyclically permutes the summands and factors, so both results are fixed by it and lie in $\mathbb F_p$. The trace is additive and $\mathbb F_p$-linear; the norm is multiplicative. For nonzero $a$, its exponent is $(p^n-1)/(p-1)$ by the finite geometric-sum identity.

In $\mathbb F_4$, $\operatorname{Tr}(\alpha)=\alpha+\alpha^2=1$ and $N(\alpha)=\alpha^3=1$. In $\mathbb F_9$ with $\beta^2=-1$, we have $\beta^3=-\beta$, so $\operatorname{Tr}(1+\beta)=2$. Its norm is $(1+\beta)^4=2$, matching the earlier power calculation.

For exact computation, store the coefficients of reduced polynomials. Add them modulo $p$, multiply the polynomials, and reduce modulo the chosen irreducible polynomial. Invert a nonzero element with polynomial Bezout or exponentiation to $q-2$. The latter follows from $a^{q-1}=1$, not from integer modular arithmetic with modulus $q$.

The [browser companion](../companion/index.html) includes a finite-fields lab for the fixed quotient $\mathbb F_2[X]/(X^3+X+1)$ with eight elements. It illustrates exact arithmetic and the effect of that defining relation. It does not certify arbitrary proposed polynomials as irreducible at cryptographic scales. Small examples and ordinary browser arithmetic are learning tools; cryptographic implementations require separate attention to algorithms, representation, side channels, and protocol design.

## 14. Reading a computation rather than trusting a label

Let us trace one multiplication and one inverse in the eight-element field. Write elements as triples of binary coefficients in the basis $1,\alpha,\alpha^2$. The element $\alpha^2+1$ has coordinates $(1,0,1)$, and $\alpha^2+\alpha$ has coordinates $(0,1,1)$. Expand their product before reducing:

$$
(\alpha^2+1)(\alpha^2+\alpha)=\alpha^4+\alpha^3+\alpha^2+\alpha.
$$

Use $\alpha^3=\alpha+1$ and $\alpha^4=\alpha^2+\alpha$. Substitution gives $(\alpha^2+\alpha)+(\alpha+1)+\alpha^2+\alpha$. Two copies of $\alpha^2$ cancel, and three copies of $\alpha$ leave one, so the result is $\alpha+1$. The power table gives an independent check: the factors are $\alpha^6$ and $\alpha^4$, and their product is $\alpha^{10}=\alpha^3$ because exponents are reduced modulo seven.

For an inverse, take $u=\alpha^2+\alpha=\alpha^4$. Its inverse should be $\alpha^3=\alpha+1$. Direct multiplication gives

$$
(\alpha^2+\alpha)(\alpha+1)=\alpha^3+\alpha^2+\alpha^2+\alpha=\alpha^3+\alpha=1.
$$

This direct check is valuable when implementing arithmetic. An exponent table may contain a transcription error, whereas multiplication by the proposed inverse must return one under the actual reduction routine. Testing the identity for every nonzero element is feasible for these small fields.

Computer implementations often encode a binary coefficient vector as a nonnegative integer. This is a storage convention, not an identification with integer arithmetic modulo eight. Under a low-bit-first coefficient convention, the code three represents $1+\alpha$, and code one represents one. Their field sum is $\alpha$, code two, because the two constant coefficients cancel. Ordinary integer addition would produce four, which represents a different polynomial. The field addition is bitwise exclusive-or because it adds each coefficient modulo two without carries.

Multiplication also differs from integer multiplication. The code two represents $\alpha$ and code four represents $\alpha^2$. Their field product is $\alpha^3=\alpha+1$, code three. Integer multiplication modulo eight would give zero. A program that stores finite-field elements as integers must still implement polynomial multiplication and reduction; the storage type alone does not supply the field laws.

For odd characteristic, coefficient arrays avoid the temptation to use binary operations accidentally. In the nine-element field, the inverse of $1+\beta$ is $2+\beta$. The conjugate formula gives $(1-\beta)/(1-\beta^2)=(1-\beta)/2$. Since the inverse of two modulo three is two, this becomes $2-2\beta=2+\beta$. Multiplying gives $2+3\beta+\beta^2=2+0+2=1$ in characteristic three. Every reduction step names the field in which its coefficients are being evaluated.

There is also an exact irreducibility test behind some practical field constructors. For a monic polynomial $g$ of degree $m$ over $\mathbb F_p$, first check that $X^{p^m}\equiv X\pmod g$. This means $g$ divides $X^{p^m}-X$, so all its irreducible factors occur only once and their degrees divide $m$. Next, for each prime divisor $\ell$ of $m$, check

$$
\gcd\bigl(g,X^{p^{m/\ell}}-X\bigr)=1.
$$

These checks exclude every proper divisor of $m$ as a possible irreducible-factor degree. Indeed, if $d$ properly divides $m$, then $m/d>1$ has a prime divisor $\ell$, and $d$ divides $m/\ell$. Such a factor would appear in the corresponding gcd. Thus the surviving factors all have degree $m$. Since their degrees sum to $m$, there is exactly one, proving that $g$ is irreducible.

Conversely, an irreducible degree-$m$ polynomial passes the first test because its roots lie in $\mathbb F_{p^m}$, and it passes every gcd test because its degree does not divide any smaller number $m/\ell$. We have therefore proved both directions of the criterion. Large exponents are handled by repeated powering and reduction modulo $g$, making the test finite and exact. The method still assumes that the coefficient modulus $p$ is prime; if it is composite, the coefficients need not form a field and the polynomial theorems no longer apply.

These examples explain how a representation earns the name finite field. One needs a prime coefficient field and an irreducible defining polynomial. Once those conditions are proved, unique reduced coordinates and exact algorithms follow. The browser lab makes those operations visible for small cases; the theorems explain why the same design works for every prime-power size.

The same arguments apply with a finite base field $\mathbb F_q$ in place of the prime field. An extension of degree $m$ has $q^m$ elements. Its automorphism $x\mapsto x^q$ fixes exactly the base field and has order $m$. Minimal polynomials over $\mathbb F_q$ have roots organized by this $q$-power map, rather than necessarily by one application of the prime-power Frobenius. The distinction is a change of base field, just as the degree of an algebraic number changed when its coefficient field changed in Chapter 22.

For example, $\mathbb F_{16}$ has degree four over $\mathbb F_2$ but degree two over its four-element subfield. The prime Frobenius $x\mapsto x^2$ has order four, whereas the map fixing the four-element subfield is $x\mapsto x^4$, of order two. An element outside that subfield has a quadratic minimal polynomial over it, even when its minimal polynomial over $\mathbb F_2$ is quartic. Always name the base field when stating a degree or a conjugacy orbit.

Finite fields also cannot carry an ordering compatible with field addition and multiplication. In an ordered field, one is positive, and a sum of any positive number of copies of one remains positive. In characteristic $p$, that sum becomes zero after $p$ copies. Thus familiar real-number arguments involving positive elements, inequalities, or smallest roots do not transfer to these fields. Polynomial identities and divisibility remain meaningful; numerical order does not.

A useful final consistency check is cardinality: a proposed field extension with a basis of length $m$ over a field of size $q$ must contain exactly $q^m$ elements. A different count signals either dependent coordinates or missing representatives.

Finite fields now have both an existence proof and a usable coordinate description. Frobenius makes their roots and subfields unusually orderly. In the next chapter, we place that behavior inside the broader theory of automorphism groups and recover field containment from symmetry.
