# Chapter 26 — Cyclotomic polynomials, roots of unity and regular polygons

The equation $x^5=1$ looks like a small arithmetic question. Its solutions actually connect a regular pentagon, a group of units modulo five, an irreducible polynomial, and a field extension of degree four. This chapter follows that connection carefully. It gives a particularly concrete setting in which the abstract Galois correspondence from Chapter 25 can be seen working.

Our central problem is this: how much algebra is needed to describe a regular polygon exactly? A drawing can suggest an answer, but a proof requires distinguishing approximate coordinates from numbers obtainable by finitely many straightedge and compass operations. Along the way we will discover why the function $\varphi(n)$, first encountered while counting invertible residues, also measures the degree of a polynomial over the rationals.

You need the polynomial arithmetic of Chapter 15, field degrees from Chapter 22, and the finite Galois correspondence from Chapter 25. We rebuild the small amount of complex geometry needed here. Trigonometric notation provides a convenient description of points on a circle; no calculus or infinite series is required. The optional practice at the end has [separate hints and solutions](../solutions/26-solutions.md).

## 26.1 Complex numbers as exact planar arithmetic

A complex number has the form $a+bi$, where $a,b$ are real numbers and $i^2=-1$. Addition is coordinate addition. Multiplication is determined by distributivity and the rule for $i^2$:

$$
(a+bi)(c+di)=(ac-bd)+(ad+bc)i.
$$

Thus $(1+i)^2=2i$, whereas $i(1+i)=-1+i$. If a complex number is represented by the point $(a,b)$, multiplication by $i$ rotates the point through a quarter turn. Multiplication is doing something geometrical, even though its definition is purely algebraic.

The complex conjugate of $z=a+bi$ is $\overline z=a-bi$. Multiplication gives $z\overline z=a^2+b^2$. For $z\ne0$, this also supplies an inverse:

$$
z^{-1}=\frac{\overline z}{a^2+b^2}.
$$

The absolute value $|z|=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}$ is the ordinary distance from the origin. The identity $|zw|=|z||w|$ follows by multiplying each number by its conjugate. Consequently the numbers with absolute value one are closed under multiplication and inverses. They form a group, the unit circle group. Here “unit” refers to length one; every nonzero complex number is also a unit in the field-theoretic sense.

To describe a point on the unit circle, write $\cos\theta+i\sin\theta$. The symbols cosine and sine mean the horizontal and vertical coordinates of the point at angle $\theta$. The elementary angle-addition identities show that multiplying two such numbers adds their angles. We abbreviate this point by $e^{i\theta}$. In this chapter that notation is a convenient name for the circle point, not a definition involving an exponential power series.

In particular,

$$
(e^{i\theta})^m=e^{im\theta}
$$

for every integer $m$. You may visualize this as repeated rotation. The formal algebra rests on the angle-addition formulas, so nothing about limiting processes has entered. It is worth keeping this distinction clear: a geometrical picture motivates the multiplication, while the explicit multiplication rule verifies every calculation.

## 26.2 Roots of unity and their orders

An $n$th root of unity is a complex number $z$ satisfying $z^n=1$. Let

$$
\zeta_n=e^{2\pi i/n}.
$$

The $n$ numbers $1,\zeta_n,\zeta_n^2,\ldots,\zeta_n^{n-1}$ are distinct and all satisfy the equation. They account for every root because a nonzero polynomial of degree $n$ over a field has at most $n$ roots. Distinctness follows because two of these angles differ by a full turn only when their exponent difference is divisible by $n$.

These roots form a cyclic group under multiplication, generated by $\zeta_n$. Their order as a group is $n$. An individual root is called primitive if its multiplicative order is exactly $n$. The word primitive means that repeated multiplication visits every vertex before returning to one; it does not mean that the root is especially simple as a complex number.

The order of $\zeta_n^a$ is

$$
\frac{n}{\gcd(a,n)}.
$$

To prove this, put $d=\gcd(a,n)$ and write $a=da'$, $n=dn'$ with $\gcd(a',n')=1$. The equation $(\zeta_n^a)^k=1$ says $n\mid ak$, equivalently $n'\mid a'k$. Euclid's lemma reduces this to $n'\mid k$. The smallest positive possible $k$ is therefore $n'$.

It follows immediately that the primitive roots are precisely $\zeta_n^a$ with $a$ invertible modulo $n$. Their number is $\varphi(n)$. For $n=12$, the exponents $1,5,7,11$ are relatively prime to twelve, so there are four primitive twelfth roots. The exponent $2$ gives a root of order six, not twelve. All twelve roots lie on the same circle, but their algebraic roles differ.

This is the first main bridge of the chapter. The cyclic group of roots has order $n$, while the group of invertible exponents has order $\varphi(n)$. The second group acts on the primitive roots by exponentiation. In a moment it will become a Galois group. Confusing those two groups is a common source of wrong degree calculations.

## 26.3 Sorting roots by their exact orders

Every root of $X^n-1$ has some exact order $d$ dividing $n$. Define the $n$th cyclotomic polynomial by

$$
\Phi_n(X)=\prod_{\substack{1\le a\le n\\\gcd(a,n)=1}}(X-\zeta_n^a).
$$

At first this is visibly a polynomial with complex coefficients. Its degree is $\varphi(n)$, because the displayed product has one factor for each primitive root. We have not yet proved that the coefficients are integers, or that the polynomial is irreducible over $\mathbb Q$. Those are the two substantial arithmetic facts still to establish.

Partitioning the roots of $X^n-1$ by exact order gives

$$
X^n-1=\prod_{d\mid n}\Phi_d(X).
$$

Each root occurs exactly once. Both sides are monic, so equality of the roots with multiplicities gives equality of the polynomials. Since the derivative of $X^n-1$ is $nX^{n-1}$, the roots are distinct in characteristic zero; multiplicity causes no complication here.

For example, $\Phi_1=X-1$ and $\Phi_2=X+1$. The remaining roots of $X^3-1$ have order three, giving $\Phi_3=X^2+X+1$. For $n=4$, remove the factors associated with orders one and two:

$$
\Phi_4(X)=\frac{X^4-1}{(X-1)(X+1)}=X^2+1.
$$

For $n=6$, remove the factors for $d=1,2,3$ and obtain $\Phi_6=X^2-X+1$. In particular, not every root of $X^6-1$ is a root of $\Phi_6$. The latter polynomial keeps only the primitive sixth roots.

Taking degrees in the factorization recovers

$$
n=\sum_{d\mid n}\varphi(d).
$$

This is the same identity previously obtained by counting elements of different orders in a cyclic group. It is not a second unrelated coincidence. The polynomial factorization records exactly that group-theoretic partition. A useful habit in algebra is to ask which underlying set a degree identity is counting.

## 26.4 Why the coefficients are integers

We prove $\Phi_n\in\mathbb Z[X]$ by induction on $n$. The assertion is true for $n=1$. Suppose it is true for every proper divisor of $n$, and let

$$
D(X)=\prod_{\substack{d\mid n\\d<n}}\Phi_d(X).
$$

By induction, $D$ is a monic polynomial in $\mathbb Z[X]$. Perform polynomial long division of $X^n-1$ by $D$. Because the leading coefficient of $D$ is one, every subtraction uses an integer coefficient. The quotient $Q$ and remainder $R$ therefore belong to $\mathbb Z[X]$.

Over $\mathbb C[X]$, we already know $X^n-1=D\Phi_n$. Uniqueness of division implies that the remainder is zero and $Q=\Phi_n$. Hence the coefficients of $\Phi_n$ are integers. Notice the role of monicity: division by a polynomial with leading coefficient two could introduce fractions. We did not merely observe that several initial examples happened to have integer coefficients.

There is also a useful recursive computation hidden inside this proof. To compute $\Phi_{12}$, start from $X^{12}-1$ and remove the polynomials associated with $1,2,3,4,6$. Alternatively observe that the primitive twelfth roots square to primitive sixth roots and calculate directly. The result is

$$
\Phi_{12}(X)=X^4-X^2+1.
$$

Multiplying this by $X^2+1$ gives $X^6+1$. This is compatible with $X^{12}-1=(X^6-1)(X^6+1)$: the roots appearing in $X^6+1$ have order four or twelve, precisely the two types removed by this multiplication.

For a prime $p$, the only divisors are one and $p$, so

$$
\Phi_p(X)=1+X+\cdots+X^{p-1}.
$$

For a prime power $p^r$, the same factorization yields

$$
\Phi_{p^r}(X)=1+X^{p^{r-1}}+\cdots+X^{(p-1)p^{r-1}}.
$$

These formulas are excellent computational examples, but they should not be used to guess that every cyclotomic polynomial has coefficients only zero, one, or minus one. That tempting pattern eventually fails. Integer coefficients are the theorem just proved; small coefficients are a separate question.

## 26.5 A first irreducibility proof: prime indices

Before tackling all $n$, consider $\Phi_p$ for prime $p$. Substitute $X+1$ for $X$:

$$
\Phi_p(X+1)=\frac{(X+1)^p-1}{X}
=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\binom pjX^{j-1}.
$$

The leading coefficient is one. Every other coefficient is divisible by $p$, because $p\mid\binom pj$ for $1\le j<p$. The constant term is $p$, which is not divisible by $p^2$. Eisenstein's criterion therefore proves that the shifted polynomial is irreducible over $\mathbb Q$.

Why does shifting preserve irreducibility? The substitution map $f(X)\mapsto f(X+1)$ is an automorphism of $\mathbb Q[X]$, with inverse $f(X)\mapsto f(X-1)$. A nontrivial factorization before the substitution would give one afterward, and conversely. Thus $\Phi_p$ itself is irreducible.

For $p=5$, the shift gives

$$
\Phi_5(X+1)=X^4+5X^3+10X^2+10X+5.
$$

Eisenstein at five applies visibly. Hence a primitive fifth root has degree four over $\mathbb Q$. That degree is larger than the degree two of the real coordinate associated with the pentagon. A complex vertex contains both coordinates together; passing to its real part forgets conjugate information. We will calculate that reduction explicitly below.

The proof illustrates why choosing coordinates can matter. The unshifted polynomial $X^4+X^3+X^2+X+1$ does not satisfy Eisenstein's divisibility conditions at any prime. This does not mean it is reducible. A criterion is a sufficient test, not a necessary characterization. A suitable change of variable exposes arithmetic that was previously hidden.

## 26.6 Irreducibility for every index

**Theorem.** For every positive integer $n$, the polynomial $\Phi_n$ is irreducible in $\mathbb Q[X]$.

Here is a complete proof using the reduction methods of Chapters 15 and 16. Let $f$ be a monic irreducible factor of $\Phi_n$ over $\mathbb Q$, and let $\zeta$ be a root of $f$. Gauss's lemma implies that $f$ and the monic complementary factor $g=\Phi_n/f$ have integer coefficients. We will show that $f$ already contains every primitive $n$th root.

Take a prime $p$ with $p\nmid n$. The number $\zeta^p$ is again primitive. Suppose, toward a contradiction, that it is not a root of $f$. It must then be a root of $g$. Consequently $g(\zeta^p)=0$, so $\zeta$ is a root of $g(X^p)$. The minimal polynomial $f$ divides $g(X^p)$ over $\mathbb Q$. Because the polynomials are monic integral polynomials, the quotient is integral too.

Reduce coefficients modulo $p$, writing a bar for reduction. In characteristic $p$, the binomial theorem gives

$$
\overline{g(X^p)}=\overline g(X)^p.
$$

Therefore $\overline f$ divides $\overline g^p$. Since $\overline f$ is monic of positive degree, choose an irreducible factor $h$ of it over $\mathbb F_p$. Primality of irreducible polynomials over a field implies $h\mid\overline g$. Thus $h^2$ divides $\overline f\,\overline g=\overline{\Phi_n}$. Since $\Phi_n$ divides $X^n-1$ integrally, the reduction of $X^n-1$ has a repeated irreducible factor.

That is impossible. The derivative is $nX^{n-1}$, and $n$ is nonzero modulo $p$. The polynomial $X^n-1$ has no common factor with $X^{n-1}$, because its constant term is minus one. Hence it is relatively prime to its derivative, so it has no repeated factor. We conclude that $\zeta^p$ must be a root of $f$.

Every positive integer $a$ relatively prime to $n$ is a product of primes none of which divides $n$. Apply the preceding conclusion repeatedly, each time starting with another primitive root already known to be a root of $f$. We obtain that $\zeta^a$ is a root of $f$ for every such $a$. These are all $\varphi(n)$ primitive roots. Since $f$ divides the degree-$\varphi(n)$ polynomial $\Phi_n$, equality follows.

The proof's engine is worth isolating. A missing conjugate would force two factors to collide after reduction modulo a carefully chosen prime. Such a collision would create a repeated root of $X^n-1$, but the derivative prevents it when $p\nmid n$. This is a precise interaction between characteristic zero and positive characteristic, not a formal manipulation of complex angles.

## 26.7 The Galois group is a familiar unit group

For this section take $n\ge2$, in accordance with the course convention for moduli. The case $n=1$ gives $\mathbb Q(\zeta_1)=\mathbb Q$ with trivial Galois group and can be handled separately. Let $K=\mathbb Q(\zeta_n)$. Every root of $X^n-1$ is a power of $\zeta_n$, so $K$ is the splitting field of that polynomial. In characteristic zero the polynomial is separable. Thus $K/\mathbb Q$ is a finite Galois extension.

An automorphism $\sigma$ fixing $\mathbb Q$ must send $\zeta_n$ to another root of its minimal polynomial, namely another primitive root. Therefore

$$
\sigma(\zeta_n)=\zeta_n^a
$$

for a uniquely determined invertible residue $a$ modulo $n$. An automorphism is determined by this image because every field element is a rational expression in $\zeta_n$ with rational coefficients.

Conversely, irreducibility of $\Phi_n$ implies that every primitive root gives a $\mathbb Q$-embedding of $K$ into $\mathbb C$. Its image is $\mathbb Q(\zeta_n^a)=K$, since an inverse exponent modulo $n$ expresses $\zeta_n$ as a power of $\zeta_n^a$. Thus each embedding is an automorphism of $K$.

If $\sigma_a(\zeta_n)=\zeta_n^a$ and $\sigma_b(\zeta_n)=\zeta_n^b$, then

$$
(\sigma_a\circ\sigma_b)(\zeta_n)=\zeta_n^{ab}.
$$

Composition corresponds to multiplication of residues. We have proved

$$
\operatorname{Gal}(\mathbb Q(\zeta_n)/\mathbb Q)
\cong(\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z)^\times,
\qquad [K:\mathbb Q]=\varphi(n).
$$

For $n=8$, the unit group is $\{1,3,5,7\}$ and each nonidentity element has order two. The Galois group is $C_2\times C_2$, not $C_4$. For $n=5$, the units modulo five form $C_4$. Equal degrees do not force equal Galois groups. The distinction becomes visible in the different arrangements of intermediate fields.

## 26.8 Complex conjugation and real subfields

On a unit circle point, conjugation reverses the angle, so $\overline{\zeta_n}=\zeta_n^{-1}$. In the exponent description, complex conjugation is the automorphism associated with $-1$ modulo $n$. For $n\ge3$, it is nontrivial and has order two.

Put $t=\zeta_n+\zeta_n^{-1}$. This number is real and equals $2\cos(2\pi/n)$. The element $\zeta_n$ satisfies

$$
X^2-tX+1=0.
$$

Therefore $[K:\mathbb Q(t)]\le2$. It is not one because $\mathbb Q(t)$ is a real field, whereas $\zeta_n$ is nonreal for $n\ge3$. Hence this degree is exactly two and

$$
[\mathbb Q(t):\mathbb Q]=\frac{\varphi(n)}2.
$$

Conjugation fixes $\mathbb Q(t)$, and its fixed field has the same degree, so $\mathbb Q(t)=K\cap\mathbb R$. This is called the maximal real subfield. “Maximal” here refers to inclusion among real subfields inside this particular cyclotomic field, not to a largest real number field everywhere.

For the pentagon, divide $1+\zeta+\zeta^2+\zeta^3+\zeta^4=0$ by $\zeta^2$. The result is

$$
(\zeta^2+\zeta^{-2})+(\zeta+\zeta^{-1})+1=0.
$$

Since $\zeta^2+\zeta^{-2}=t^2-2$, we get $t^2+t-1=0$. The angle $2\pi/5$ is less than a quarter turn, so its cosine is positive. Choose the positive root:

$$
t=\frac{-1+\sqrt5}{2},\qquad
\cos(2\pi/5)=\frac{\sqrt5-1}{4}.
$$

The square root of five, familiar from elementary geometry, has emerged from a degree-four extension through a degree-two fixed field. This calculation turns the Galois correspondence into an exact coordinate construction.

## 26.9 What straightedge and compass can produce

Begin with points at coordinates $(0,0)$ and $(1,0)$. A straightedge draws a line through two previously constructed points; a compass draws a circle with previously constructed center and radius. A new point is obtained by intersecting two available lines or circles. We consider finite constructions only.

The coordinate equations of a line are linear. Solving two line equations uses addition, subtraction, multiplication and division. Intersecting a line with a circle reduces to a quadratic equation, so a square root may also be needed. Intersecting two circles first permits subtraction of their equations, giving a line, and then the same quadratic calculation applies.

It follows inductively that every real constructible coordinate lies in a tower

$$
\mathbb Q=F_0\subseteq F_1\subseteq\cdots\subseteq F_r,
\qquad [F_j:F_{j-1}]\le2.
$$

The converse also holds: field operations and positive square roots can be realized geometrically using similar triangles and the geometric-mean construction. To see the square-root step, place segments of lengths $a$ and one end to end on a line, draw the semicircle on their combined length, and erect the perpendicular at their junction. The altitude has squared length $a$ by the similar-triangle relations. Thus it has length $\sqrt a$.

A necessary consequence is that the degree over $\mathbb Q$ of any algebraic constructible real number is a power of two. The tower law proves this because its degree divides $2^r$. This condition alone is not sufficient for an arbitrary algebraic number: its degree says nothing by itself about how the extension is assembled. The Galois structure supplies the additional control needed for regular polygons.

For a quick obstruction, $\sqrt[3]2$ has degree three by Eisenstein's criterion at two. It cannot be obtained by these constructions. Doubling the volume of a cube requires multiplying its side by $\sqrt[3]2$, so exact cube duplication is impossible with the stated tools. Numerical approximations remain possible; the theorem concerns exact finite operations.

## 26.10 The regular polygon criterion

A regular $n$-gon is constructible exactly when its vertex coordinates $\cos(2\pi/n)$ and $\sin(2\pi/n)$ are constructible. For $n\ge3$, this is equivalent to the cyclotomic field being obtainable inside a sequence of quadratic extensions of $\mathbb Q$: the real coordinates determine $\zeta_n$ after adjoining $i$, while the cosine coordinate is obtained from $\zeta_n+\zeta_n^{-1}$ and the sine coordinate is obtained by taking the appropriate signed square root of one minus the squared cosine. Equivalently, both coordinates can be recovered after also adjoining $i$.

Necessity is now immediate. If the polygon is constructible, the degree $\varphi(n)$ of $\mathbb Q(\zeta_n)$ must be a power of two. For sufficiency, suppose $\varphi(n)=2^r$. The cyclotomic Galois group is an abelian group of order $2^r$. A finite abelian group of this order has a chain of subgroups with successive indices two, obtained from its cyclic prime-power decomposition. By Galois correspondence, the reversed chain is a tower of quadratic extensions.

For geometric precision, one can work in the maximal real subfield. Its Galois group is the quotient by conjugation, still an abelian group of power-of-two order. It therefore has such a subgroup chain, giving a quadratic tower entirely inside $\mathbb R$. Each real quadratic step adjoins a square root of a positive element of the preceding real field. Consequently its elements are constructible. The sine coordinate is then obtained from $\sqrt{1-\cos^2(2\pi/n)}$, with the appropriate sign.

We have proved that a regular $n$-gon is constructible if and only if $\varphi(n)$ is a power of two. Translating that condition into prime factorization produces the familiar form. Write $n=2^a\prod_jp_j^{e_j}$ with distinct odd primes $p_j$. Then

$$
\varphi(n)=\varphi(2^a)\prod_jp_j^{e_j-1}(p_j-1).
$$

If this is a power of two, each $e_j$ is one, because an odd factor $p_j^{e_j-1}$ cannot occur. Also each $p_j-1$ is a power of two. If $p=1+2^m$ is prime, then $m$ must itself be a power of two: if $m=uv$ with $u$ odd and greater than one, $2^{uv}+1$ is divisible by $2^v+1$. Thus the odd primes have the form $2^{2^s}+1$, called Fermat primes.

The exact criterion is therefore

$$
n=2^a p_1\cdots p_t,
$$

where the $p_j$ are distinct Fermat primes. The word distinct is essential. A regular fifteen-gon is constructible because $15=3\cdot5$, but a regular nine-gon is not because $\varphi(9)=6$. A regular seventeen-gon is constructible because seventeen is a Fermat prime. The criterion does not claim that every number of the Fermat form is prime.

## 26.11 Computing without confusing evidence and proof

The [Galois companion lab](../companion/index.html) provides a finite, inspectable setting for subgroup and fixed-field ideas. Its listed examples are examples, rather than a general symbolic procedure for all cyclotomic fields. For a new index $n$, you can still perform a valuable hand investigation: list the units modulo $n$, compute their multiplication, determine orders, and compare the resulting group with possible subgroup chains.

Take $n=12$. The four units are $1,5,7,11$, all nonidentity units having order two. Here $\zeta_{12}$ has real and imaginary parts $\sqrt3/2$ and $1/2$, so the cyclotomic field is $\mathbb Q(i,\sqrt3)$. Its three quadratic intermediate fields are $\mathbb Q(i)$, $\mathbb Q(\sqrt3)$, and $\mathbb Q(\sqrt{-3})$. Their three associated index-two subgroups are visible in the unit group. This gives a complete example in which both sides of the correspondence can be listed.

For $n=7$, the degree is six. The real subfield has degree three, so the regular heptagon fails the power-of-two requirement. A convincing drawing cannot remove that obstruction. Conversely the regular pentagon's exact square-root expression supplies more than a numerical guess: it gives the field tower required for a construction.

When doing these computations, keep four questions separate. Which complex roots have exact order $n$? What polynomial has precisely those roots? Which rational automorphisms permute them? Which real coordinates describe a drawing? These questions interact, but their answers are not interchangeable. The root group, unit group, Galois group and coordinate field all appear for a reason, and the established maps tell you exactly how to pass between them.

## 26.12 Further worked comparisons and proof diagnosis

Consider $n=10$ before reaching for a new field. A primitive tenth root can be written as minus a suitable primitive fifth root, and $\varphi(10)=\varphi(5)=4$. More explicitly, if $\zeta_{10}=e^{\pi i/5}$, then $-\zeta_{10}$ is a primitive fifth root. Thus $\mathbb Q(\zeta_{10})=\mathbb Q(\zeta_5)$. Two different polygon indices may lead to the same number field. The degree formula does not assert that different indices always produce different fields.

There is also a subtle issue in changing the base field. We proved $\Phi_n$ irreducible over $\mathbb Q$, not over every field. Over $\mathbb R$, $\Phi_5$ factors into two quadratic polynomials, pairing each root with its complex conjugate. Over $\mathbb C$ it splits into linear factors by its definition. Over a finite field it may split in a pattern governed by the multiplicative order of the field size modulo $n$, provided the characteristic does not divide $n$. That last connection uses Chapter 24, but the main point already follows from the definition of irreducibility: the allowed coefficients are part of the question.

Why is the condition $p\nmid n$ indispensable in the irreducibility proof? If $p\mid n$, the derivative $nX^{n-1}$ becomes zero after reduction. For example, over $\mathbb F_3$,

$$
X^3-1=(X-1)^3.
$$

The three characteristic-zero roots collapse into one repeated root after reduction. There is no contradiction in that collision. The proof specifically chooses a prime avoiding this behavior. Examining this failed case explains the hypothesis more effectively than memorizing a derivative calculation in isolation.

Another tempting argument says that because the primitive roots are geometrically indistinguishable, every permutation of them gives a field automorphism. That is false. An automorphism must preserve addition as well as multiplication. For fifth roots, the allowed permutations come from exponent multiplication and form a group of size four, while all permutations of the four primitive roots form a group of size twenty-four. Most permutations destroy algebraic relations. The minimal polynomial and embedding argument are what identify the permitted ones.

The Galois correspondence also explains a practical limitation of numerical data. Approximating a real number to ten decimal places does not determine its minimal polynomial. Rational approximations can imitate irrational numbers very closely, and distinct algebraic numbers can lie near one another. To prove a degree statement, use a polynomial relation together with irreducibility or a degree comparison. Decimal output is useful for choosing the intended root of an already justified equation, as in the pentagon calculation; it cannot replace that justification.

Finally, distinguish building a field tower from writing a pleasant closed formula. The group-theoretic proof of polygon constructibility guarantees that a chain of quadratic steps exists. It need not supply a short expression or an elegant drawing. A large constructible polygon can require complicated intermediate calculations. Existence, efficiency and presentation are different mathematical questions. The same distinction will recur in solvability by radicals: knowing that a radical expression exists is different from finding one that a person would want to read.

## 26.13 Möbius inversion inside a polynomial identity

Chapter 19 developed Möbius inversion for sums over divisors. There is a multiplicative counterpart here. Work temporarily in the field $\mathbb Q(X)$ of rational functions, where division by a nonzero polynomial is legitimate. The divisor factorization implies

$$
\Phi_n(X)=\prod_{d\mid n}(X^d-1)^{\mu(n/d)}.
$$

An exponent minus one means reciprocal, not a polynomial of negative degree. The expression initially lives in a rational-function field; the earlier integrality proof tells us that all denominators cancel in the final result. This distinction prevents a circular argument: one should not assume cancellation merely because the notation on the left looks polynomial.

To verify the formula, replace each $X^d-1$ with its product of cyclotomic factors. The total exponent of $\Phi_e$ is then the sum of $\mu(n/d)$ over divisors $d$ satisfying $e\mid d\mid n$. Put $d=ef$ and $m=n/e$. The exponent becomes $\sum_{f\mid m}\mu(m/f)$, equal to one when $m=1$ and zero otherwise. All factors disappear except $\Phi_n$ itself. The proof is exactly the same divisor cancellation responsible for additive Möbius inversion, expressed through multiplication.

For $n=12$, the divisors with nonzero Möbius contributions give

$$
\Phi_{12}(X)=\frac{(X^{12}-1)(X^2-1)}{(X^6-1)(X^4-1)}.
$$

Cancel $X^{12}-1=(X^6-1)(X^6+1)$ and $X^4-1=(X^2-1)(X^2+1)$. What remains is $(X^6+1)/(X^2+1)=X^4-X^2+1$. Each cancellation is an identity in a rational-function field, so there is no issue about substituting a root at an intermediate denominator. Only after simplifying should one evaluate the resulting polynomial at a number for which the unsimplified expression might have looked like zero divided by zero.

Taking degrees in this formula gives another identity,

$$
\varphi(n)=\sum_{d\mid n}d\,\mu(n/d).
$$

We already know this from arithmetic functions. The polynomial version explains why it is natural: extracting exact-order roots is a multiplicative inclusion-exclusion procedure. Removing roots whose orders lie in proper divisors is analogous to removing integers with unwanted common prime factors. Number theory has supplied the bookkeeping, while field theory supplies the meaning of the resulting degree.

## 26.14 The eighth roots as a complete correspondence exercise

Let $\zeta=\zeta_8=(1+i)/\sqrt2$. We claim that

$$
K=\mathbb Q(\zeta)=\mathbb Q(i,\sqrt2).
$$

For one inclusion, $\zeta^2=i$ and $\zeta+\zeta^{-1}=\sqrt2$, so $K$ contains both generators on the right. For the other, the displayed expression for $\zeta$ lies in $\mathbb Q(i,\sqrt2)$. Both inclusions have been checked by field operations; equality is established without guessing from decimal values.

The field has basis $1,\sqrt2,i,i\sqrt2$ over $\mathbb Q$. To verify the degree, first $\mathbb Q(\sqrt2)$ has degree two. This field is real, so it does not contain $i$, whose minimal polynomial over a real field is $X^2+1$. Adjoining $i$ adds another factor two. The tower law gives degree four. Linear independence of the proposed basis also follows from this two-stage basis construction.

There are four automorphisms. Write $\alpha$ for the map that negates $\sqrt2$ while fixing $i$, and $\beta$ for the map that negates $i$ while fixing $\sqrt2$. Each preserves the defining squares of the generators, and the basis description verifies that their formulas preserve the field operations. Their composition negates both generators. All three nonidentity maps have order two and commute.

The fixed field of $\langle\beta\rangle$ is $\mathbb Q(\sqrt2)$. Indeed, applying $\beta$ to

$$
a+b\sqrt2+ci+di\sqrt2
$$

changes the signs of the last two terms. Equality forces $c=d=0$ by basis independence. Similarly the fixed field of $\langle\alpha\rangle$ is $\mathbb Q(i)$. Under $\alpha\beta$, the terms involving $\sqrt2$ alone and $i$ alone change sign, but $i\sqrt2$ remains fixed. Its fixed field is $\mathbb Q(i\sqrt2)=\mathbb Q(\sqrt{-2})$.

This calculation is more informative than merely listing the three fields. It demonstrates how to compute a fixed field: choose a basis, express a general element, apply the automorphism, and compare coefficients. It also checks that the Galois theorem is consistent with explicit algebra. Three subgroups of order two yield three fields of degree two, with inclusion reversed. The entire group fixes $\mathbb Q$; the identity subgroup fixes the entire degree-four field.

In exponent notation, complex conjugation is $\sigma_7$. The map $\alpha$ sends $\zeta$ to $-\zeta=\zeta^5$, so it is $\sigma_5$. The product $\alpha\beta$ corresponds to exponent $5\cdot7\equiv3\pmod8$. Thus the geometrical sign changes and the modular exponent calculations give the same group table. This is an excellent place to verify a correspondence in two independent languages.

## 26.15 A careful boundary for the polygon theorem

The proof of the polygon criterion used both arithmetic and group structure. Suppose an arbitrary real algebraic number $u$ has degree four. The degree condition says only that $[\mathbb Q(u):\mathbb Q]=4$. It does not provide an intermediate quadratic field. A tower of two quadratic extensions would require such an intermediate field, so one cannot obtain sufficiency from the degree alone.

For a cyclotomic field, however, normality and separability give the Galois correspondence, and the Galois group is explicitly abelian. If its order is a power of two, subgroup theory supplies the missing chain. This explains exactly why the argument works in this special setting. A proof should not quietly import an intermediate field just because the total degree factors as two times two.

There is a related point about angles. A constructible polygon means its vertices are constructible starting from the initial unit segment. It does not mean every angle of a given integer number of degrees can be trisected. Trisecting an arbitrary supplied angle is a different problem with a different initial field. In any construction problem, the field generated by the starting data is the correct base field. Here that field is $\mathbb Q$, because the coordinates zero and one are the only initial numerical data.

The method also reveals why the final theorem is exact rather than probabilistic. The obstruction is a divisibility statement about extension degrees. A coordinate cannot both have degree three over the starting field and belong to a quadratic tower. No increase in drawing accuracy changes that incompatibility. At the same time, the theorem places no restriction on approximate numerical plotting. Mathematics is separating two well-defined tasks that ordinary language sometimes calls by the same word, “drawing.”

A final coordinate caution sharpens the geometric argument. The maximal real subfield contains $\zeta_n+\zeta_n^{-1}$, hence the cosine coordinate. The sine is $(\zeta_n-\zeta_n^{-1})/(2i)$. This expression is real, but it need not lie in $\mathbb Q(\zeta_n)$ because $i$ need not lie there. For a regular hexagon, the cyclotomic field is $\mathbb Q(\sqrt{-3})$, whose real elements are rational, whereas the sine coordinate is $\sqrt3/2$. To obtain both coordinates one may pass to the compositum with $\mathbb Q(i)$, or take a positive square root of one minus the squared cosine. Both operations preserve constructibility. Thus the polygon proof is valid, but the careless claim that every imaginary coordinate belongs to the maximal real subfield would be false.

This is a useful general lesson about extracting coordinates. A complex number is not itself an ordered pair of elements of its generated number field. Its real and imaginary parts are obtained using conjugation and, for the imaginary part, division by $i$. Whether those operations remain within a particular field is an algebraic question that must be checked. A familiar representation can hide an additional field extension.

## 26.16 Optional practice

1. List the primitive eighth roots as powers of $\zeta_8$, and find the order of every eighth root.
2. Compute $\Phi_8$ and $\Phi_{12}$ from the divisor factorization, showing the removed factors.
3. Prove that $\Phi_7$ is irreducible using a shift and Eisenstein's criterion.
4. Compute the Galois group of $\mathbb Q(\zeta_8)/\mathbb Q$ and identify its three quadratic intermediate fields.
5. Determine whether regular polygons with $n=9,10,15,20,21,34$ are constructible, giving the degree obstruction or factorization criterion.
6. Let $t=2\cos(2\pi/7)$. Derive $t^3+t^2-2t-1=0$ and prove that it is irreducible over $\mathbb Q$.
7. Explain exactly which step in the general irreducibility proof fails when the auxiliary prime divides $n$, using $n=p=3$.
8. Show that $\mathbb Q(\zeta_6)=\mathbb Q(\sqrt{-3})$, and distinguish this complex field from the real coordinate field of a regular hexagon.
