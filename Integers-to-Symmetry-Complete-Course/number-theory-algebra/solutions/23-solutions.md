# Chapter 23 — Hints and worked solutions

## 1. Denominators and monic polynomials

**Hint.** Rational algebraic integers have denominator one in lowest terms.

The rational-integrality lemma proves $3/2$ is not integral. For $\alpha=2+\sqrt3$, its conjugate is $2-\sqrt3$. Their sum is four and product is one, so $(X-\alpha)(X-\bar\alpha)=X^2-4X+1$. This is monic with integer coefficients and vanishes at $\alpha$, proving integrality.

## 2. Two integer rings

**Hint.** First check squarefreeness and the residue modulo four.

The integers thirteen and minus six are squarefree. Since $13\equiv1\pmod4$, the first ring is $\mathbb Z[(1+\sqrt{13})/2]$. Since $-6\equiv2\pmod4$, the second is $\mathbb Z[\sqrt{-6}]$. These are statements about all integral elements, not merely subrings containing the displayed generators; the quadratic formula proved in the chapter establishes that completeness.

## 3. Trace, norm and a unit

**Hint.** Multiply by the conjugate.

The trace of $2+\sqrt3$ is four and its norm is $4-3=1$. Its inverse is $2-\sqrt3$, which lies in $\mathbb Z[\sqrt3]$, the full integer ring because three is congruent to three modulo four. Thus it is a unit. Direct multiplication verifies the inverse.

## 4. An irreducible that two does not divide

**Hint.** Its norm is six; inspect possible nonunit factor norms.

Any nonunit factorization of $1+\sqrt{-5}$ would have norms two and three, since norms are positive integers and multiply to six. Neither norm is represented by $a^2+5b^2$: at $b=0$ the value is a square, and at nonzero $b$ it is at least five. Thus the element is irreducible. If it were twice an element of the ring, its coefficients in the unique integer basis $1,\sqrt{-5}$ would both be even. They are both one, so it is not divisible by two.

## 5. Multiplying two ideals

**Hint.** Use all pairwise products of generators and prove both containments.

The product is generated by $9$, $3(1+s)$, $3(1-s)$, and $(1+s)(1-s)=1-s^2=6$. Each generator lies in $(3)$, proving one containment. Conversely $9-6=3$ belongs to the product, so the whole ideal $(3)$ belongs to it. Hence equality holds. Merely finding that all generators are multiples of three would establish only one containment.

## 6. A nonprincipal ideal

**Hint.** Its quotient has two elements; compare with indices of principal ideals.

Substituting $s=1$ modulo two gives a surjective map $R\to\mathbb F_2$ with kernel $(2,1+s)$. Thus its additive index is two. If it equaled $(a+bs)$, Smith normal form applied to multiplication by $a+bs$ would give index $a^2+5b^2$. There is no integer solution to $a^2+5b^2=2$, so it is not principal. The ideal is nonzero, so its hypothetical generator would be nonzero and the multiplication matrix would have full rank, as the index argument requires.

## 7. Two prime behaviors

**Hint.** Reduce $X^2+5$ modulo the chosen prime.

Modulo seven, $-5\equiv2$ and $3^2\equiv2$, giving distinct roots three and minus three. Thus $(7)$ splits as $(7,s-3)(7,s+3)$. Modulo eleven, the nonzero squares are $1,3,4,5,9$; six is absent. Therefore $X^2+5$ is irreducible and $(11)$ remains prime, with quotient field of 121 elements. Seven being split does not imply seven is reducible as an element; the chapter proves it is irreducible but not prime.

## 8. A Pell recurrence

**Hint.** Expand the product and use $(\sqrt2)^2=2$.

We have $(3+2\sqrt2)(a+b\sqrt2)=(3a+4b)+(2a+3b)\sqrt2$. Since $3+2\sqrt2$ has norm one, this preserves norm one. From $(17,12)$ it gives $(3\cdot17+4\cdot12,2\cdot17+3\cdot12)=(99,70)$. The check $99^2-2\cdot70^2=9801-9800=1$ verifies the result directly.
