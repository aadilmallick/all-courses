# Chapter 21 — Hints and worked solutions

These are optional reference solutions. Work with the chapter's definitions before reading the complete argument.

## 1. A pair of coprime clocks

**Hint.** Compute the order of $(1,1)$.

Since $\gcd(15,4)=1$, its order is $\operatorname{lcm}(15,4)=60$. The whole product has sixty elements, so $(1,1)$ generates it. Thus the group is cyclic and isomorphic to $C_{60}$.

## 2. Two mixed-prime clocks

**Hint.** Separate the two-primary and three-primary parts.

The order is $12\cdot18=216$. The exponent is $\operatorname{lcm}(12,18)=36$. The elementary divisors are $4,2,3,9$. Align $(2,4)$ with $(3,9)$ to obtain invariant factors $6,36$. Hence the group is $C_6\times C_{36}$. The divisibility $6\mid36$ confirms invariant-factor form.

## 3. A relation matrix

**Hint.** The gcd of the entries is two.

The determinant is $6\cdot8-4\cdot2=40$, so the Smith entries are two and twenty. They satisfy $2\mid20$. The quotient is $C_2\times C_{20}$, of order forty. One direct reduction swaps rows to obtain $\begin{pmatrix}2&8\\6&4\end{pmatrix}$, subtracts three times the first row from the second, and subtracts four times the first column from the second. This produces $\operatorname{diag}(2,-20)$; negating the second row gives the claimed form.

## 4. All groups of order thirty-six

**Hint.** The prime-power parts have order four and nine.

The two-primary part is either $C_4$ or $C_2\times C_2$. The three-primary part is either $C_9$ or $C_3\times C_3$. Their four combinations have invariant-factor forms $C_{36}$, $C_3\times C_{12}$, $C_2\times C_{18}$, and $C_6\times C_6$. Existence and uniqueness of the finite abelian classification prove completeness and absence of duplicates.

## 5. Counting elements killed by scalars

**Hint.** Multiply the counts for each cyclic coordinate.

For $2a=0$, the number is $\gcd(2,8)\gcd(2,4)\gcd(2,2)=2\cdot2\cdot2=8$. For $4a=0$, the number is $\gcd(4,8)\gcd(4,4)\gcd(4,2)=4\cdot4\cdot2=32$. These counts include the identity. They count elements whose order divides the scalar, rather than only elements of exactly that order.

## 6. Distinguishing two groups

**Hint.** Follow what multiplication by two does.

The exponents are four and eight, so the groups cannot be isomorphic. Their layer-dimension sequences give more detail. For $C_4\times C_4$, dimensions of $2^{k-1}P/2^kP$ are $2,2,0,\ldots$. For $C_8\times C_2$, they are $2,1,1,0,\ldots$. Their first quotients have equal dimension, showing why one invariant may miss a difference detected by later layers.

## 7. Finite multiplicative subgroups of fields

**Hint.** Use the exponent and count roots of $X^e-1$.

Let $H$ be such a subgroup. If $H=\{1\}$, it is already cyclic, generated by $1$. Assume now that $|H|>1$. It is abelian because field multiplication is commutative. By the classification, write $H\cong C_{d_1}\times\cdots\times C_{d_r}$ with $d_1\mid\cdots\mid d_r$. Its exponent is $e=d_r$ and it contains an element of that order. Every element of $H$ is a root of the nonzero polynomial $X^e-1$. The root bound over a field gives $|H|\leq e$. But $e\leq|H|$ by Lagrange, so $|H|=e$. An element of order $e$ therefore generates all of $H$. This proof does not assert that the multiplicative group of an arbitrary infinite field is cyclic; finiteness was used in the classification and counting argument.

## 8. Another presentation

**Hint.** Put relation vectors in columns and compute the determinant.

The relation matrix is $\begin{pmatrix}2&6\\4&8\end{pmatrix}$. Its entry gcd is two and its determinant is $16-24=-8$. The Smith entries are two and four. The quotient is $C_2\times C_4$, of order eight and exponent four. It is not cyclic, since a cyclic group of order eight contains an element of order eight.
