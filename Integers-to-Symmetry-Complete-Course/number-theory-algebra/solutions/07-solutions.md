# Chapter 07 — Hints and reference solutions

Use [Chapter 07](../chapters/07-cyclic-groups-and-lagrange.md) for the order and coset theorems.

## 1. Orders modulo eighteen

**Hint.** Use the additive form $\operatorname{ord}([k])=18/\gcd(18,k)$.

**Solution.** The three orders are $9,3,2$, respectively. A class generates the additive group exactly when its representative is coprime to $18$. The generators are $[1],[5],[7],[11],[13],[17]$. Every generator has additive order $18$; this says nothing about its multiplicative order in the unit group.

## 2. Powers of an order-twenty element

**Hint.** Simplify the step sizes using gcds with $20$.

**Solution.** The orders are $20/\gcd(20,6)=10$ and $20/\gcd(20,15)=4$. The first subgroup is $\langle a^2\rangle$, consisting of all even powers modulo $20$, and the second is $\langle a^5\rangle=\{e,a^5,a^{10},a^{15}\}$. To justify the first equality, $a^6$ lies in $\langle a^2\rangle$, and $(a^6)^7=a^{42}=a^2$. The intersection consists of exponents divisible by $2$ and $5$, so it is $\langle a^{10}\rangle=\{e,a^{10}\}$ and has order $2$.

## 3. Subgroup uniqueness

**Hint.** A subgroup is generated by the smallest positive exponent it contains.

**Solution.** If $G=\langle a\rangle$ has order $n$, every subgroup is $\langle a^d\rangle$ for a divisor $d$ of $n$. Its size is $n/d$. Hence a prescribed subgroup order $m$ forces $d=n/m$, proving uniqueness. For $n=18$, the subgroup orders are $1,2,3,6,9,18$. The corresponding subgroups are $\langle a^{18}\rangle$, $\langle a^9\rangle$, $\langle a^6\rangle$, $\langle a^3\rangle$, $\langle a^2\rangle$, and $\langle a\rangle$. These exhaust all possibilities because no other divisor can occur.

## 4. Left and right cosets

**Hint.** Use $sr=r^{-1}s$ and $sr^2=r^2s$.

**Solution.** We obtain $rH=\{r,rs\}$ and $Hr=\{r,r^3s\}$, so those two differ. We also obtain $r^2H=\{r^2,r^2s\}$ and $Hr^2=\{r^2,r^2s\}$, which agree. Equality for one selected element does not prove the subgroup's left and right cosets agree for every element.

## 5. Prime-order groups

**Hint.** A subgroup order is a positive divisor of the group order.

**Solution.** A subgroup of a group of prime order $p$ has order $1$ or $p$, by Lagrange. The first case is the identity subgroup and the second is the whole group, since the ambient group is finite. For a group of order $p^2$, divisibility also allows the intermediate order $p$, so the proof no longer excludes proper nontrivial subgroups. In fact, choosing a nonidentity element and considering its order produces a subgroup of order $p$ either directly or by taking an appropriate power when that element has order $p^2$.

## 6. Products of cycles

**Hint.** The maximum possible order is the least common multiple of the two cycle lengths.

**Solution.** In $C_4\times C_6$, every element order divides $\operatorname{lcm}(4,6)=12$. A pair of coordinate generators attains order $12$. The group has $24$ elements and therefore cannot be cyclic. In $C_4\times C_9$, coordinate generators have order $\operatorname{lcm}(4,9)=36$, equal to the group size. Thus the product is cyclic and its maximum element order is $36$.

## 7. A gcd proof of the totient identity

**Hint.** For $g\mid n$, count the $k$ with $\gcd(k,n)=g$ by writing $k=gj$.

**Solution.** Fix a positive divisor $g$ of $n$. The integers $k$ in the required range with gcd $g$ correspond bijectively to integers $j$ with $1\le j\le n/g$ and $\gcd(j,n/g)=1$. Their number is $\varphi(n/g)$. Every $k$ belongs to exactly one such class, so $n=\sum_{g\mid n}\varphi(n/g)$. As $g$ runs over divisors, so does $d=n/g$, giving the desired formula.

## 8. Commuting products

**Hint.** A common multiple of the two orders kills both factors.

**Solution.** Let $L=\operatorname{lcm}(m,n)$. Since $a,b$ commute, $(ab)^L=a^Lb^L=e$. The order-divides-return-exponent theorem gives $\operatorname{ord}(ab)\mid L$. Equality can fail: in a cyclic group of order $5$, choose $b=a^{-1}$ with $a$ a generator. Both factors have order $5$, while $ab=e$ has order $1$. The coprimality assumption in the stronger theorem excludes this example and forces the cyclic subgroups to have trivial intersection.
