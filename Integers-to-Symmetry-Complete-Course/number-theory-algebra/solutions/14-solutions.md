# Chapter 14 — Hints and reference solutions

Return to [Chapter 14](../chapters/14-ideals-and-ring-quotients.md). The whole ring is an ideal, but prime and maximal ideals are proper by definition.

## 1. Three operations on integer ideals

**Hint.** Sum uses the greatest common divisor, intersection the least common multiple, and product the ordinary product.

**Solution.** We have $(12)+(18)=(6)$ by Bezout and common divisibility. An integer lies in the intersection exactly when it is divisible by both twelve and eighteen, hence by thirty-six, so $(12)\cap(18)=(36)$. Products of multiples of twelve and eighteen are multiples of $216$, and every multiple of $216$ is such a product, so $(12)(18)=(216)$. Intersection and product are different because the two ideals are not comaximal.

## 2. Ideals modulo twelve

**Hint.** Apply the correspondence theorem to integer ideals containing $(12)$.

**Solution.** The ideals are generated by the classes of $1,2,3,4,6,12$. Their sizes are respectively $12,6,4,3,2,1$. The last is the zero ideal. Quotienting by the ideal generated by $[d]$ gives $\mathbb Z/d\mathbb Z$ for each divisor $d>1$, as follows from the composite reduction map and its kernel. Exactly $d=2,3$ give fields and domains. Thus the ideals generated by $[2]$ and $[3]$ are both prime and maximal; no other ideal is prime or maximal. The whole-ring ideal, generated by $[1]$, is excluded by properness.

## 3. An ideal containing a unit

**Hint.** Use absorption with the inverse, which need not lie in the ideal beforehand.

**Solution.** If $u\in I$ and $u$ is a unit in $R$, then absorption gives $u^{-1}u=1\in I$. For every $r\in R$, absorption again gives $r=r1\in I$. Hence $I=R$. The argument is why proper ideals cannot contain units.

## 4. Two noncoprime moduli

**Hint.** The two coordinates must agree modulo four.

**Solution.** The kernel consists of integers divisible by both eight and twelve, so it is $24\mathbb Z$. Any image pair $([a]_8,[b]_{12})$ has $a\equiv b\pmod4$. Conversely, if $4\mid b-a$, seek $x=a+8t$. Its second condition becomes $8t\equiv b-a\pmod{12}$, solvable exactly when four divides $b-a$. Thus the image is precisely the twenty-four compatible pairs with matching reductions modulo four, a proper subring of the ninety-six-element product. The first isomorphism theorem identifies this image with $\mathbb Z/24\mathbb Z$.

## 5. Explicit CRT reconstruction

**Hint.** Use $3\cdot5-2\cdot7=1$.

**Solution.** Set $i=15\in(5)$ and $j=-14\in(7)$, so $i+j=1$. For target residues two modulo five and four modulo seven, the CRT construction gives $x=2j+4i=-28+60=32$. All solutions are $32+35k$, because the kernel of the simultaneous reduction map is $(5)\cap(7)=(35)$. The residue thirty-two checks as two modulo five and four modulo seven. Uniqueness is modulo thirty-five, not uniqueness as an integer.

## 6. Coordinate-axis quotients

**Hint.** Project onto the coordinate not being collapsed.

**Solution.** The first axis $I=\mathbb F_2\times\{0\}$ is the kernel of the surjective second projection. The first isomorphism theorem gives $(\mathbb F_2\times\mathbb F_2)/I\cong\mathbb F_2$, so $I$ is maximal. The other axis is the kernel of the first projection and has the same quotient-field description. Each axis is proper and contains two elements. The quotient criterion proves maximality without listing every possible intermediate ideal.

## 7. Intersection equals product under comaximality

**Hint.** Write one as a sum of one element from each ideal.

**Solution.** Always $IJ\subseteq I\cap J$ by absorption and addition. If $I+J=R$, choose $i\in I,j\in J$ with $i+j=1$. For $x\in I\cap J$, we have $x=xi+xj$. The first term is a product of an element of $J$ with one of $I$, and the second a product of an element of $I$ with one of $J$. Both lie in $IJ$, so $x\in IJ$. This proves the reverse inclusion and equality.

## 8. Prime but not maximal

**Hint.** A domain that is not a field gives the needed quotient.

**Solution.** The zero ideal in $\mathbb Z$ is prime, since its quotient is $\mathbb Z$, an integral domain. It is not maximal because that quotient is not a field: the nonzero integer two has no multiplicative inverse there. Equivalently, the ideal chain $(0)\subsetneq(2)\subsetneq\mathbb Z$ explicitly exhibits an intermediate proper ideal. This example shows why the implication maximal implies prime cannot be reversed in general.
