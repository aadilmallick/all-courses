# Chapter 22 — Hints and worked solutions

## 1. A quadratic inverse

**Hint.** Multiply by the conjugate.

Since $(2+\sqrt3)(2-\sqrt3)=4-3=1$, the inverse is $2-\sqrt3$. This is a field identity, so no decimal approximation is required.

## 2. Minimal polynomial and basis

**Hint.** A quadratic over a field is irreducible precisely when it has no root in that field.

If a rational number $a/b$ in lowest terms satisfied $(a/b)^2=5$, then $a^2=5b^2$. Unique factorization would give an odd exponent of five on one side and an even exponent on the other. Thus $X^2-5$ has no rational root and is irreducible. It vanishes at $\sqrt5$, so it is the minimal polynomial. Polynomial division supplies spanning by $1,\sqrt5$, and irrationality supplies independence. This pair is a basis.

## 3. Reducing powers

**Hint.** Replace $\alpha^3$ by two and then reduce $\alpha^4$.

We have $(1+\alpha^2)^2=1+2\alpha^2+\alpha^4$. Since $\alpha^4=2\alpha$, the result is $1+2\alpha+2\alpha^2$. Its coordinates in the stated basis are $(1,2,2)$.

## 4. Whether a second adjunction is new

**Hint.** Express $\sqrt8$ using $\sqrt2$.

Since $\sqrt8=2\sqrt2$, the first field is just $\mathbb Q(\sqrt2)$, of degree two. For $\sqrt3$, suppose it were $a+b\sqrt2$ with $a,b\in\mathbb Q$. Squaring and comparing rational and irrational coordinates forces $ab=0$. The two cases require either a rational square root of three or a rational square root of three halves, both impossible by prime-exponent parity. Thus the second adjunction has degree two over $\mathbb Q(\sqrt2)$, giving total degree four by the tower law.

## 5. Recovering both roots

**Hint.** Find the inverse of $\theta=\sqrt2+\sqrt3$.

Squaring twice shows $\theta^4-10\theta^2+1=0$. Also $\theta^{-1}=\sqrt3-\sqrt2$. Hence $\sqrt3=(\theta+\theta^{-1})/2$ and $\sqrt2=(\theta-\theta^{-1})/2$. Thus $\mathbb Q(\theta)$ equals the degree-four field from Exercise 4. Its minimal polynomial has degree four. Since the displayed monic quartic vanishes, it is that minimal polynomial. This proves irreducibility as well as identifying the polynomial.

## 6. A degree obstruction

**Hint.** Use the actual quadratic tower, then the tower law with the smaller field generated by the proposed length.

Every constructible real number lies in a finite tower whose successive degrees are one or two. The total degree is a power of two. If a degree-five number $\alpha$ lay in its top field $K$, then $[K:\mathbb Q]=[K:\mathbb Q(\alpha)]\cdot5$, impossible because five does not divide a power of two. Therefore $\alpha$ is not constructible under the stated rules.

## 7. Why square brackets and parentheses differ

**Hint.** Ask whether $t$ has an inverse in the polynomial ring.

If $t g(t)=1$ for a polynomial $g$, then a nonzero $g$ would make the product have positive degree, whereas one has degree zero. Thus $t$ is a nonzero nonunit and $F[t]$ is not a field. For an algebraic element $\alpha$ with irreducible minimal polynomial $m$, every nonzero $g(\alpha)$ has $\gcd(g,m)=1$. Polynomial Bezout gives an inverse after evaluation. The irreducible relation is what changes the situation.

## 8. Prime extension degrees

**Hint.** Factor the total degree through an intermediate field.

Let $F\subseteq K\subseteq E$ and $[E:F]=p$ prime. The tower law gives $p=[E:K][K:F]$. Both factors are positive integers, so one is one. If $[K:F]=1$, then $K=F$ because its $F$-vector space is spanned by one. If $[E:K]=1$, then $E=K$. There are no other intermediate fields. No normality or Galois hypothesis is needed for this degree argument.
