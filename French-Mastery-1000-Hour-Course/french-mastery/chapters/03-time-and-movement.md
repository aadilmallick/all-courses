# 03 — Time and movement: past events, future plans, and everyday choices

**Level:** A2. **Time:** 100 hours in ten ten-hour units. **Prerequisites:** the present tense, noun phrases, agreement, questions, negation, numbers, and everyday routines of Chapters 01–02. By the end, you will organize a trip, report what happened, buy food, give directions, and explain an intention or obligation. You will also begin replacing repeated objects with pronouns. The sequence is a course design, not an official CEFR grammar prescription.

Consider a small travel problem. Yesterday you bought a ticket. Today you discover that the departure time has changed. Tomorrow you will take a different train. You need language that keeps these three times distinct while allowing you to ask what to do next. French does not express every time relationship by changing a single verb ending: it also uses auxiliary verbs, infinitives, time expressions, and context. This chapter makes those mechanisms visible in manageable messages.

## Locating an event before choosing a form

Begin with **Aujourd'hui, je prends le train** — “Today, I am taking the train.” The time expression and present tense cooperate. **Hier, j'ai pris le train** moves the event into a completed past frame. **Demain, je vais prendre le train** presents a future plan. Notice what changes: **prends** is one conjugated word; **ai pris** combines an auxiliary with a participle; **vais prendre** combines conjugated **aller** with an infinitive.

An **auxiliary** is a verb used to help form a grammatical construction. It is still conjugated, but it does not necessarily keep its ordinary lexical meaning. In **j'ai pris**, **ai** does not mean that I possess taking. It helps place **prendre** in the passé composé. A **past participle** is a form such as **pris**, **parlé**, or **fini**. An **infinitive** is a dictionary form such as **prendre**. Confusing these categories causes errors like *j'ai prendre* or *je vais pris*.

Useful time anchors include **hier**, yesterday; **avant-hier**, the day before yesterday; **ce matin**, this morning; **la semaine dernière**, last week; **aujourd'hui**, today; **ce soir**, this evening; **demain**, tomorrow; **après-demain**, the day after tomorrow; and **la semaine prochaine**, next week. Their interpretation depends on the speaking time. **Le lendemain**, which you will use more in narratives, means the next day relative to a past reference point, not necessarily tomorrow relative to now.

Try a timeline before translating: purchase yesterday, complaint now, replacement trip tomorrow. Then write **J'ai acheté un billet hier. Le train ne part pas à l'heure prévue. Demain, je vais prendre un autre train.** The purpose of the timeline is to protect the event relationships. If you merely choose a French form that resembles an English tense name, you can produce a grammatical sentence that tells the wrong story.

## Building the passé composé with avoir

The most common pattern is present **avoir** plus a past participle: **j'ai parlé, tu as parlé, elle a parlé, nous avons parlé, vous avez parlé, ils ont parlé**. The auxiliary changes with the subject. In this basic construction, the participle does not change merely because the subject is feminine or plural: **Elle a parlé**, **Elles ont parlé**. Later chapters will examine agreement with preceding direct objects; do not invent subject agreement here.

Regular participles are **-er → -é**, **-ir** of the finishing family **→ -i**, and many **-re → -u**: **parler → parlé**, **finir → fini**, **vendre → vendu**. These patterns do not cover all verbs. Learn frequent irregular participles in useful phrases: **faire → fait**, **prendre → pris**, **voir → vu**, **lire → lu**, **écrire → écrit**, **dire → dit**, **mettre → mis**, **avoir → eu**, **être → été**, **boire → bu**, **pouvoir → pu**, **vouloir → voulu**, **devoir → dû**.

Work through **J'ai acheté un billet**. First identify the event: buy a ticket. The subject is I, so choose **ai**. The verb **acheter** belongs to the **-er** pattern, so its participle is **acheté**. Keep **un billet** as the direct object. The complete sentence can mean “I bought a ticket” or “I have bought a ticket,” depending on context. French passé composé and English present perfect do not map one-to-one.

Negation surrounds the auxiliary: **Je n'ai pas acheté de billet**. In a question with **est-ce que**, keep ordinary subject–auxiliary order: **Est-ce que vous avez acheté un billet ?** In inversion: **Avez-vous acheté un billet ?** A short adverb commonly appears between auxiliary and participle: **J'ai déjà acheté un billet**, “I have already bought a ticket”; **Je n'ai jamais pris ce train**, “I have never taken this train.” Longer adverbial phrases can occur elsewhere.

The passé composé commonly presents bounded events or completed sequences: **J'ai fermé la porte, j'ai pris le bus et je suis arrivé à neuf heures**. It is not a universal equivalent of every English past form. Describing a background situation, an ongoing past action, or a repeated past habit will often require the imparfait, developed in Chapter 04. Here, establish the completed event frame first rather than forcing every past meaning into the one form you know.

## Building the passé composé with être

A limited set of intransitive verbs commonly uses **être**, including **aller, venir, arriver, partir, entrer, sortir, monter, descendre, retourner, rester, tomber, naître**, and **mourir** in the relevant constructions. Their participles agree with the subject: **Il est arrivé**, **Elle est arrivée**, **Ils sont arrivés**, **Elles sont arrivées**. The feminine **-e** and plural **-s** are often silent, but they remain part of written grammar.

Do not conclude that “all movement verbs take être.” **Marcher**, **courir**, **voyager**, and **nager** normally take **avoir**: **J'ai marché pendant une heure**. The rule concerns particular verbs and constructions, not whether a person physically changes location. A mnemonic list can help retrieval, but examples reveal the real boundary. **Je suis allé à la gare** and **J'ai couru jusqu'à la gare** describe movement with different auxiliaries.

Several verbs alternate according to whether they take a direct object. **Elle est sortie** means “She went out.” **Elle a sorti les clés** means “She took out the keys.” **Nous sommes montés** means “We went up”; **Nous avons monté les valises** means “We carried the suitcases up.” Do not choose the auxiliary from the infinitive alone when the construction changes. Ask whether the verb is describing the subject's own movement or an action on a directly expressed object.

Now build **Mes amies sont parties**. The subject is third-person plural, so choose **sont**. **Partir** uses **être** here and has participle **parti**. **Mes amies** is feminine plural, so write **parties**. If the group were mixed and referred to as **mes amis**, write **partis** under traditional standard agreement. The written endings encode information that the spoken sentence may not reveal independently.

Pronominal verbs use **être** in compound tenses: **Je me suis levé**, **Elle s'est couchée**. Their agreement is more complex than “always agree with the subject.” Compare **Elle s'est lavée** and **Elle s'est lavé les mains**. In the second, **les mains** is the direct object and follows the verb, while **se** has an indirect role. At this stage, learn these common models accurately; Chapter 04 and later reference material will give you a method for analyzing the roles.

## Reporting an event without overloading the listener

A useful event report answers when, where, what happened, and what the result was. **Hier soir, j'ai perdu mon portefeuille dans le métro. Je suis descendu à République. J'ai cherché dans mon sac, mais je n'ai rien trouvé. Ensuite, j'ai téléphoné au service des objets trouvés.** “Yesterday evening, I lost my wallet on the metro. I got off at République. I searched my bag but found nothing. Then I called the lost-property service.”

Notice the alternation of **avoir** and **être** according to the verb construction, not according to narrative importance. **Descendre** has no direct object here and uses **être**. **Chercher** and **trouver** take **avoir**. **Rien** appears after the auxiliary in **je n'ai rien trouvé**. The connectors **mais** and **ensuite** signal an obstacle and the next response. You do not need a new sophisticated connector for every sentence; clarity comes from a well-chosen sequence.

When reporting a problem, include what help you need. **J'ai perdu ma carte. Je voudrais savoir où déclarer la perte.** The polite **je voudrais** is a fixed useful form of the conditional, which will be studied systematically later. You may use a helpful chunk before knowing its full paradigm if you understand its function and limits. It does not authorize a random mixture of present, future, and past endings.

For listening practice, first hear the report without its transcript and list only events. On the second pass, add locations and time references. Then transcribe one sentence and mark the auxiliary in one color and participle in another. If you miss **je suis descendu**, compare the complete sound group with **j'ai cherché**. Sound recognition often improves when you understand the grammatical alternatives that could plausibly occur.

## Future plans: aller plus an infinitive

The near future, often called **futur proche**, uses present **aller** plus an infinitive: **je vais partir, tu vas partir, il va partir, nous allons partir, vous allez partir, elles vont partir**. It commonly presents an intention, a planned development, or something connected to the present situation. “Near” is not a rigid calendar limit. **L'année prochaine, je vais étudier à Lille** can be natural when the plan is established now.

Only **aller** is conjugated. In **Nous allons réserver une chambre**, **allons** agrees with nous, while **réserver** remains an infinitive. Negate the conjugated verb: **Nous n'allons pas réserver ici**. Put a reflexive pronoun with its infinitive: **Je vais me reposer**. Later, when replacing **une chambre** with **la**, the object pronoun also belongs before the infinitive: **Nous allons la réserver**. The learner's question should be “Which verb owns this object?” rather than “Do all pronouns go at the very start?”

Present **aller** can also mean movement toward a destination: **Je vais à la gare**. In **Je vais prendre le train**, it helps construct the future. You can combine the meanings: **Je vais à la gare parce que je vais prendre le train**. Context and the following complement distinguish them. A preposition plus a place points to ordinary going; a following infinitive commonly supplies a planned action.

Use the form to make a real weekend plan: **Samedi, je vais retrouver une amie. Nous allons visiter un musée. Après, nous allons déjeuner près du fleuve. S'il pleut, nous allons prendre le bus.** The last sentence introduces a useful conditional pattern with **si + present** and a future meaning in the main clause. Do not put a future tense immediately after ordinary conditional **si**: **si tu viens**, not *si tu viendras*.

### A just-completed action and an imminent action

Present **venir de + infinitive** presents a recent completion: **Je viens de réserver une chambre** — “I have just booked a room”; **Nous venons d'arriver** — “We have just arrived.” Conjugate venir for the subject, retain de, and keep the following verb in the infinitive. De elides before an appropriate vowel sound. Compare ordinary movement **Je viens de Lyon**, “I come from Lyon,” where de introduces a place rather than an infinitive. The following complement tells you which construction is present.

**Être sur le point de + infinitive** means to be about to do something: **Le train est sur le point de partir**. It emphasizes imminence more strongly than a general plan such as **Je vais voyager cet été**. Neither construction is a new one-word verb tense; both combine familiar conjugated verbs with infinitives. On a small timeline, put **je viens de partir** just before now, **je suis sur le point de partir** just after now, and **je vais partir en août** at the planned future date. The actual distance meant by “just” depends on context, so it has no universal fixed number of minutes.

## The simple future and its practical boundaries

The **futur simple** adds **-ai, -as, -a, -ons, -ez, -ont** to a future stem. For many **-er** and **-ir** verbs, the stem is the infinitive: **je parlerai**, **nous finirons**. For many **-re** verbs, remove the final **e**: **je prendrai**, **vous attendrez**. These endings resemble forms of **avoir**, a historical connection that can help memory, but the modern result is one written word.

Common irregular stems include **être → ser-**, **avoir → aur-**, **aller → ir-**, **faire → fer-**, **venir → viendr-**, **voir → verr-**, **pouvoir → pourr-**, **vouloir → voudr-**, **devoir → devr-**, and **savoir → saur-**. Thus **je serai**, **tu auras**, **nous irons**, **elles pourront**. Practice the stem and ending as separate choices: desired verb gives **viendr-**; subject vous gives **-ez**; result **vous viendrez**.

The future can announce a prediction, promise, or scheduled consequence: **Le train arrivera à dix-huit heures**; **Je vous téléphonerai demain**; **Vous recevrez une confirmation**. The near future and simple future overlap; do not reduce them to “soon” versus “far away.” Compare **Attention, le verre va tomber !**, tied to an immediate visible development, with **Un jour, tu comprendras**, a prediction. Real choice also depends on register, context, and how the speaker presents the event.

In a future time clause with **quand**, French often uses future where English uses present: **Quand tu arriveras, nous dînerons** — “When you arrive, we will have dinner.” Contrast **Si tu arrives tôt, nous dînerons ensemble** — “If you arrive early, we will have dinner together.” Ordinary conditional **si** takes present here; future **quand** can take future. The conjunction changes the grammatical expectation. Learn the pair rather than a vague rule that all subordinate clauses use present.

## Instructions: the imperative is a conversation action

The imperative gives an instruction, invitation, or request without an expressed subject pronoun. It has familiar singular, nous, and vous forms: **Écoute**, **Écoutons**, **Écoutez**. With regular **-er** verbs, familiar singular normally drops the present-tense **-s**: **Tu regardes → Regarde !** The same happens in **Va !** from aller. A linking **-s** returns before **y** or **en** in relevant constructions: **Vas-y**, **Manges-en**.

Common irregular imperatives are **sois/soyons/soyez**, **aie/ayons/ayez**, and **sache/sachons/sachez**. You will meet **Veuillez patienter** — “Please wait” — as a polite service formula. Do not assume that an imperative is always rude; tone, relationship, wording, and situation matter. **Entrez, je vous en prie** is welcoming. **Vous pouvez tourner à gauche** may be a gentler instruction than a bare command in some conversations.

Negation surrounds the imperative: **Ne prenez pas cette rue**. In an affirmative command, object pronouns follow with hyphens: **Prenez-le**, “Take it.” In a negative command they return before the verb: **Ne le prenez pas**. With reflexive commands, compare **Lève-toi** and **Ne te lève pas**; **me/te** generally become **moi/toi** after an affirmative imperative, with special behavior before **en/y**. The full pronoun-order system will be developed in Chapter 04.

Give directions as a sequence with landmarks: **Sortez de la gare. Traversez la place. Tournez à droite après la pharmacie. Continuez tout droit pendant deux minutes. La mairie est à gauche, en face de la poste.** A listener can follow this because each instruction has an action and a recognizable reference point. “Go there, then turn” is grammatically possible but practically weak. Ask the listener to repeat the crucial turn if the route is complicated.

## Food and quantities: not every noun names a countable unit

Compare **un pain** and **du pain**. **Un pain** can refer to one loaf or a type of bread in context; **du pain** refers to some bread without counting units. The partitive articles are **du** before a masculine singular noun, **de la** before a feminine singular noun, and **de l'** before an appropriate vowel sound. **Des** commonly introduces plural quantities: **des pâtes**, **des légumes**. The exact article depends on how the speaker construes the noun in that message.

Use **Je voudrais du fromage et de la salade** for unspecified amounts. Compare **Je voudrais un fromage** if asking for a whole cheese or one variety, and **Je voudrais le fromage de la région** if identifying a particular cheese. English can hide these choices behind “cheese”; French makes you choose a determiner. Begin from meaning: category, specific referent, countable unit, or unspecified amount.

After many quantity expressions, use **de/d'**: **un kilo de pommes**, **une bouteille d'eau**, **beaucoup de travail**, **un peu de sel**, **trop de bruit**, **assez de temps**. Do not add a partitive after **beaucoup de**: *beaucoup du pain* is not the ordinary way to say a lot of bread. **Beaucoup du pain que tu as acheté** is a different, specific construction involving part of a defined set; it should not become your default quantity model.

An ordinary negative often changes indefinite or partitive articles to **de**: **Je mange du pain → Je ne mange pas de pain**; **J'achète des pommes → Je n'achète pas de pommes**. Definite preference retains its article: **Je n'aime pas le pain**. With **être**, identification retains the article: **Ce n'est pas de la soupe**. Contrast also matters: **Je ne bois pas du thé, je bois du café** corrects the kind of drink. These patterns make sense when you ask what the negative targets.

## Shopping as a complete interaction

At a market, begin with greeting and request: **Bonjour, je voudrais deux cents grammes de fromage, s'il vous plaît.** The seller may ask **Celui-ci ?**, “This one?” or **Vous en voulez combien ?**, “How much of it do you want?” You need not produce every form immediately to understand its role. Reply with a quantity: **Deux cents grammes, merci.** Then ask **Ça fait combien ?** or **Combien je vous dois ?** The latter is conversational; **Combien est-ce que je vous dois ?** is another option.

**Vendeur : Bonjour, vous désirez ? — Cliente : Bonjour. Je voudrais un kilo de tomates et une bouteille d'huile d'olive. — Vendeur : Vous voulez autre chose ? — Cliente : Oui, est-ce que vous avez du pain complet ? — Vendeur : Il n'y en a plus aujourd'hui. — Cliente : D'accord, ce sera tout. Je peux payer par carte ?**

The customer wants tomatoes, oil, and wholemeal bread; the bread has run out. **Il n'y en a plus** is a useful chunk meaning there is no more of it. Chapter 04 will unpack **y** and **en**; for now, connect the whole phrase to an unavailable product. **Ce sera tout** uses the simple future to close the order, “That will be all.” **Par carte**, **en espèces**, and **sans contact** cover common payment methods.

Practice a substitution with genuine constraints: a limited budget, an allergy, or an unavailable product. **Je suis allergique aux noix** conveys an allergy; **sans noix** means without nuts. Do not rely on a language exercise for medical safety: in a real transaction, clearly confirm ingredients with the person responsible. The language goal is an unambiguous request and confirmation, not memorizing one sentence without understanding the answer.

## Objects and the first useful pronouns

An object completes a verb's meaning. In **Je cherche la gare**, the station is what I am looking for. French **chercher** directly takes its object, unlike English “look for,” so do not add **pour** by translation. A **direct object** is attached without a preposition in the relevant verb construction. An **indirect object** is introduced by a preposition such as **à**. The French construction, not the English translation, determines the role.

Replace a previously identified direct object with **le**, **la**, or **les**: **Je cherche le musée → Je le cherche**; **Je cherche la gare → Je la cherche**; **Je cherche les clés → Je les cherche**. Before a vowel sound, **le/la** become **l'**: **Je l'attends**. These pronouns go before the conjugated verb in a simple statement. Their written form can look like an article, but a following verb reveals the pronoun's job.

For people receiving something through **à**, **lui** can mean to him or to her, and **leur** to them: **Je téléphone à ma sœur → Je lui téléphone**; **J'écris à mes parents → Je leur écris**. Do not use **la** because the English sentence mentions a woman: **téléphoner à** requires an indirect role. Some **à + person** constructions behave differently, as in **penser à elle**. You will build a verb-construction inventory rather than apply lui indiscriminately.

Other object pronouns include **me, te, nous, vous**. **Vous m'attendez ?** means “Are you waiting for me?” **Je vous parle** means “I am speaking to you.” The same surface pronoun can serve a direct or indirect role, so its spelling alone does not identify the construction. In a two-verb phrase, place the pronoun with the verb it completes: **Je vais les acheter**, “I am going to buy them.” Here **les** is the object of acheter, not of aller.

## Travel prepositions and a useful distinction about duration

For many feminine countries use **en**: **en France**, **en Belgique**. Many masculine countries take **au**: **au Canada**, **au Japon**. Plural country names take **aux**: **aux États-Unis**. Countries beginning with a vowel often take **en** regardless of masculine gender: **en Iran**. Cities usually take **à**: **à Paris**, **à Montréal**. Some place names have articles and their own conventions, so learn actual destinations as phrases.

Return or origin commonly uses **de/d'**, **du**, or **des** according to the name: **de France**, **du Canada**, **des États-Unis**, **de Paris**. Transport expressions include **en train**, **en voiture**, **en bus**, **à pied**, and **à vélo**; actual usage has some variation, especially with bicycle and motorbike phrases. You do not need a speculative geometric rule about being inside every vehicle. Store standard high-frequency combinations.

Compare **pendant**, **depuis**, **il y a**, and **dans**. **J'ai travaillé pendant deux heures** gives the duration of a completed activity. **Je travaille ici depuis deux ans** says the situation began two years ago and continues now; French uses present in this basic continuing situation. **J'ai commencé il y a deux ans** locates a beginning two years ago. **Je partirai dans deux heures** locates departure two hours from now. These four messages answer different time questions.

English “I have lived here for two years” can therefore become **J'habite ici depuis deux ans**. Translating it as **J'ai habité ici pendant deux ans** normally presents that residence as a completed period and may imply you no longer live here. That is not merely a stylistic difference. Draw an arrow continuing into now for **depuis** and a closed interval for completed **pendant**; the picture helps you preserve the intended relationship.

Make the contrast personal with four controlled messages. Choose a course that began last month and continues: **J'étudie le français depuis un mois**. Choose a completed study session: **J'ai étudié pendant une heure ce matin**. Locate its beginning relative to now: **J'ai commencé il y a trois heures**. Locate the next session relative to now: **Je vais recommencer dans deux heures**. Check that the invented clock times are compatible before practicing the sentences aloud. If it is noon and you began three hours ago, the beginning was nine; a one-hour session then ended at ten. A new session in two hours begins at fourteen hours. This small calculation exposes a real meaning difference that a list of translations can hide. Ask a partner to reconstruct the schedule from your four sentences, then change the starting time and repeat without reading.

## A travel narrative with practical consequences

**Samedi dernier, Élodie est partie de Nantes avec son frère. Ils ont pris un train pour Bordeaux. À la gare, ils ont acheté des sandwichs et une bouteille d'eau. Élodie a mis les billets dans son sac, mais son frère a oublié son portefeuille à la maison. Ils sont arrivés à midi et ils ont rejoint leur amie Inès devant la gare. L'après-midi, ils ont visité le centre-ville. Aujourd'hui, dimanche, ils vont louer des vélos si le temps reste beau. Ils rentreront lundi soir. Avant le départ, Inès leur dit : « Vérifiez les horaires et n'oubliez pas vos affaires ! »**

This account reports a completed outward journey and then shifts to the characters' plans. **Sont partis** and **sont arrivés** agree with a mixed group under traditional standard grammar. **Ont pris**, **ont acheté**, and **ont visité** use avoir without subject agreement. **Aujourd'hui, dimanche** establishes the current speaking date, while **lundi soir** identifies the planned return. **Leur** in **Inès leur dit** replaces **à Élodie et à son frère**. The command **n'oubliez pas** uses vous because Inès addresses both travelers.

Retell the story from Élodie's viewpoint: **Je suis partie… Nous avons pris… Mon frère a oublié…** Several forms change because the subject changes, while the basic events remain the same. Then move the whole story into an itinerary written before the trip: **Nous allons partir… Nous prendrons…** This is not a word-swapping exercise; you must also remove information that a traveler could not reasonably know in advance, such as an accidental forgotten wallet.

For a listening task, let the site read only the first five sentences. Type the French events, then an English account. Compare meaning before spelling: did you correctly identify who forgot the wallet? Next inspect auxiliary choice and accents. A transcript that is orthographically close but assigns the mistake to the wrong person has failed the communication task, even if an automatic string comparison happens to reward several matching words.

## Guided translation with explained alternatives

Translate: 1. Yesterday I bought two tickets. 2. My sister arrived at nine. 3. We did not find the station. 4. Tomorrow we are going to rent bicycles. 5. When you arrive, I will call you. 6. If it rains, we will take the bus. 7. Take this street and turn left. 8. Do not forget your passport. 9. I would like a bottle of water. 10. I have lived here for three months.

Models: **1. Hier, j'ai acheté deux billets.** Acheter uses avoir. **2. Ma sœur est arrivée à neuf heures.** Arriver uses être here, and the subject is feminine singular. **3. Nous n'avons pas trouvé la gare.** The definite object remains **la gare**; negation surrounds avons. **4. Demain, nous allons louer des vélos.** Simple future **nous louerons** is also possible, depending on how the plan is presented.

**5. Quand tu arriveras, je t'appellerai.** Future quand takes a future form; **te** elides before appeler. With polite address use **Quand vous arriverez, je vous appellerai**. **6. S'il pleut, nous prendrons le bus.** Si takes present in this ordinary future condition. **7. Prenez cette rue et tournez à gauche.** The vous imperative omits the subject. **8. N'oubliez pas votre passeport.** The possessive agrees with passeport and the address is polite singular or plural.

**9. Je voudrais une bouteille d'eau.** A quantity container is followed by **de**, elided before eau. **10. J'habite ici depuis trois mois.** The continuing residence uses present plus depuis. Compare the reverse translation **J'ai habité ici pendant trois mois**, “I lived here for three months,” which presents a completed period. An answer checker should explain the difference rather than merely mark both strings as past-time expressions.

Translate into English: **Elle a sorti les clés. Elle est sortie à huit heures. Je vais les acheter. Il n'y a plus de pain. Vous recevrez une confirmation demain.** Models: “She took out the keys.” “She went out at eight.” “I am going to buy them.” “There is no bread left.” “You will receive a confirmation tomorrow.” The first pair is a compact test of construction-based auxiliary choice; the third tests whether you link the object pronoun to its infinitive.

## Ten ten-hour units and a cumulative project

Use the same broad allocation as Chapter 02: two hours of explanation and examples, two of drills and vocabulary retrieval, two of listening/dictation, two of speaking/interaction, and two of writing/correction/review. Each unit should include a short return to earlier material so that present forms and noun agreement remain accessible while past and future forms become familiar. Space the work across days rather than treating a ten-hour unit as one sitting.

| Unit | Concrete ten-hour outcome |
| --- | --- |
| 03.1 | Build event timelines and ten avoir-based past reports; distinguish infinitive and participle by identifying their jobs. |
| 03.2 | Learn common irregular participles through travel messages; transcribe short past-tense statements and correct auxiliary placement. |
| 03.3 | Contrast être and avoir constructions; explain **elle est sortie/elle a sorti les clés**; report a journey with agreement. |
| 03.4 | Produce a lost-property report, including event sequence, location, description, and requested next step. |
| 03.5 | Make a weekend plan with aller plus infinitive; add negative plans and reflexive actions; interview another learner. |
| 03.6 | Build simple future forms and compare future quand with conditional si; write a confirmation message and a weather contingency. |
| 03.7 | Give and follow directions; practice affirmative and negative imperatives; design a route with four clear landmarks. |
| 03.8 | Shop within a budget; distinguish countable items and partitive amounts; request an alternative when a product is unavailable. |
| 03.9 | Replace repeated direct and indirect objects; contrast travel prepositions and duration expressions in contextual tasks. |
| 03.10 | Complete the travel project, a mixed grammar/translation test, two dictations, and a spoken problem-solving exchange. |

The project is a **three-day trip dossier**. Before departure, write a 180-word itinerary with dates, transport, accommodation, budget, and one contingency. Create a shopping list with quantities. Record a two-minute explanation and directions from the station. After a simulated trip, write a 180-word event report in the past, including one problem and how you responded. Ask a partner to challenge one plan so you must clarify rather than recite.

Review in four dimensions, each 0–4: whether a listener can reconstruct the itinerary and events; whether past and future reference remain consistent; whether transactions include quantities and polite requests; and whether you can repair misunderstandings. A 3 means mostly successful with occasional gaps; a 4 means clear and stable within this familiar task. Include a correction log with the original sentence, corrected sentence, reason, and a new example. A corrected answer without a reason rarely prevents the same error next week.

## Building durable vocabulary and using the companion

Group vocabulary around actions rather than a single English label. Learn **réserver une chambre**, **rater un train**, **annuler un billet**, **faire une correspondance**, and **demander un remboursement**. The noun's article and the verb's complement structure are part of the entry. A word such as **prendre** belongs in several frames: take a train, have a drink, take a street. Keep the frames separate until you can retrieve them reliably.

Use the companion's chapter filter for a manageable set of grammar questions and bilingual sentences. For dictation, listen before revealing the French, type what you heard, and then provide an English translation. For speaking, repeat the model once and then change a meaningful detail, such as date or destination. A transcription tool can support comparison but does not measure the full quality of pronunciation, interaction, or register. The course's scoring is practice feedback rather than certification.

When you can report a completed sequence and make a coherent plan, Chapter 04 will show how narratives become richer: what was already happening, what used to happen, and what had happened earlier. It will also connect pronouns to explicit grammatical roles so that a sentence such as **Je les lui ai donnés** becomes explainable instead of a memorized puzzle. Keep a few completed reports from this chapter; you will revise them with those new relationships.

For additional French-language reference, consult the [Vitrine linguistique](https://vitrinelinguistique.oqlf.gouv.qc.ca/). For the communicative setting of DELF qualifications, see [France Éducation international](https://www.france-education-international.fr/diplome/delf-tout-public). The chapter is original teaching and does not reproduce an official exam syllabus or promise a qualification after a fixed number of hours.
