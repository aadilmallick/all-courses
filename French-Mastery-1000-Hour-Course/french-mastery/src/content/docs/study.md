## A week that includes the whole language

At ten hours a week, one ten-hour unit is one week. Use two hours to understand the lesson and inspect worked examples; two for focused listening; two for vocabulary and review; two for interaction, shadowing, or recording; one for reading and writing; and one for exercises and revision. Individual chapters adjust this balance for advanced projects. Count each period once. Reading a chapter while an audio track plays is one period, not two study hours.

At this pace the complete plan is about 100 weeks. Fifteen hours a week is about 67 weeks, twenty about 50. Add breaks and extra practice when needed. Study time alone does not guarantee C1: your experience, feedback, consistency, input, and real interaction matter.

## A repeatable learning session

1. Recall something from the previous session before looking at it.
2. Read a small section and explain one example in your own words.
3. Listen to comprehensible French and investigate one difficulty.
4. Produce something: a short response, translation, recording, or paragraph.
5. Compare with a reference or feedback, then make a new attempt.

Repetition works best when you know what you are changing. “My listening is bad” is not a diagnosis. “I recognized the words on paper but missed the liaison” suggests a focused remedy. “I knew the meaning but not the verb’s preposition” suggests a different one.

## Vocabulary beyond a finite bank

The site provides a curated core bank with articles, example sentences, and translations. Do not treat the number of entries as a C1 threshold. Expand it with useful items from your reading, work, hobbies, and conversations. Store noun gender, verb constructions, a contextual meaning, register, and a natural example. Learn collocations such as **prendre une décision**, not only **décision**.

At A1–A2, use short comprehensible dialogues and graded reading. At B1, add regular short articles and topic-specific listening. At B2–C1, work with sustained interviews, essays, reports, literature, and more than one francophone variety. Choose easier material for extensive input and harder short passages for intensive analysis. The book’s original readings provide starting material; the hour plan deliberately includes independent input and conversation.

## Quizzes are one kind of evidence

Grammar questions have authored keys and explanations. Typed translation and vocabulary use conservative reference matching. Accents are checked; punctuation and equivalent apostrophe styles are normalized. An unmatched answer may still be a valid translation. Compare its meaning, grammar, register, and scope with the reference. Practice mode offers an explicit self-mark; test mode preserves the original reference result for later review.

Listening mode asks for both the French transcription and the English meaning. Both must match their references for an automatic match. In a test, feedback appears at the end. Tests use up to the selected number of available questions and a twenty-minute limit; an advanced-stage selection is not an official proficiency examination.

## Bring a human into the loop

Use regular conversations, even short ones, and periodic feedback on a portfolio. At early stages ask whether the message is understandable. At later stages ask about organization, precision, register, interaction, and how naturally you respond to unexpected questions. A speech recognizer cannot settle these questions. Neither a timer nor an AI comment certifies a level.

## Keep your own evidence

Log study time and unit completion on the Progress page. This data stays in your current browser. Export a backup before moving devices, clearing browser storage, or importing another log. In your portfolio keep an early and revised version of a task; a final score alone cannot show what improved.
