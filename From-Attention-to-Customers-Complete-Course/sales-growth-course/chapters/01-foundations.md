# 1. Foundations: build a business system before chasing reach

**Allocation: 70 hours — study 12, guided practice 12, production 22, field application 16, review 8.** No marketing knowledge is assumed. This chapter establishes the vocabulary and arithmetic used throughout the course. Use the companion's Funnel and Economics experiments after the worked examples.

## Begin with a sale, then work backward

Imagine that you publish an excellent explanation of database indexes. Ten thousand people see it. A hundred thank you. Nobody buys your database notes. Another developer publishes a less polished explanation to eight hundred people, and three purchase a workshop. Neither result tells us who is the better teacher. The second creator may have reached people facing a deadline, offered a relevant next step, or already earned trust through earlier interactions. The first creator may never have made an offer. Attention is one input to a commercial system; its presence does not establish that the rest of the system works.

Marketing helps a particular group recognize a problem, understand a possible solution, and choose whether to act. Sales helps a prospective customer make a decision through conversation or a buying process. Delivery makes the promised result possible. Retention gives satisfied customers reasons to remain, return, or recommend you. These activities overlap. A useful tutorial can sell without a conversation, while an honest sales conversation can reveal what your marketing failed to explain.

For this course, the recurring business is a fictional developer education and consulting practice called **Build Clearly**. It has three possible offers: a $49 debugging field guide, a $399 guided workshop, and a $3,000 implementation diagnostic. These are teaching prices, not market benchmarks or recommendations about what you personally should charge. We will repeatedly ask which buyer needs which offer and what evidence supports the price. Assume all sample customers, performance figures, testimonials, and conversations are fictional unless explicitly described as your own future evidence.

The practice helps working developers diagnose recurring engineering problems. Its audience could include curious students, experienced developers, and team leaders. Those groups can enjoy the same tutorial while needing different products. A student may need fundamentals. A developer may need a reproducible debugging method. A manager may need a team process. An early business improves by distinguishing these situations, not by insisting that everyone who likes a post must be a potential customer.

Write down one concrete commercial goal before choosing a platform. For example: “Within the next eight weeks, conduct enough customer conversations to determine whether working developers will pay for a guided debugging workshop.” This goal does not promise revenue. It tells you what you need to learn. Compare it with “grow my personal brand,” which gives no stopping condition and leaves you vulnerable to whatever number the platform makes most visible.

## Translate the vocabulary into observable behavior

An **audience** is a set of people you can repeatedly reach or who repeatedly choose to consume your work. A **segment** is a group sharing a commercially relevant situation. A **lead** is a person or organization that has expressed interest or whose need you have a legitimate reason to explore. A **qualified lead** satisfies your explicitly chosen criteria for fit. A **customer** has completed the purchase or agreed commercial commitment you use as the definition of a sale. Keep these terms distinct in your records.

An impression is an instance of content being displayed under a platform's definition. Reach usually concerns distinct accounts or people, but definitions and estimation methods differ. A view is not a universal unit: a short video starting, a thumbnail being shown, and somebody deliberately reading a thread are different events. Chapter-specific source notes explain platform definitions. You should never add views across platforms and call the sum unique people reached. Some individuals will appear repeatedly, and some measurements count events rather than people.

A **conversion** is a chosen action, such as subscribing, booking, or buying. A conversion rate is the number of those actions divided by the eligible opportunities for that action. “Our conversion rate is three percent” is incomplete until you identify the denominator. Three purchases among one hundred product-page visitors differs from three purchases among one hundred email subscribers, especially if only twenty subscribers visited the page. Label numerator, denominator, population, and time window every time.

The **customer journey** is the sequence of decisions and experiences a person has. A **funnel** is a simplified measurement model that groups some of those decisions into stages. People do not always move neatly forward. They return, ask colleagues, watch older videos, abandon checkout, or buy through a referral. A funnel is useful because it makes constraints visible. It becomes misleading when you treat it as a literal description of every person's mind or assume that an observed sequence proves causation.

The **offer** is the commercial promise plus the terms: who it is for, the result or deliverable, what is included, how it works, the price, and the conditions. Your skill is not itself an offer. “I know TypeScript” describes a capability. “A two-session diagnostic that identifies why your extension fails during onboarding and delivers a prioritized repair plan” describes something a buyer can evaluate. The offer is specific enough to decline, which is a strength: rejection can teach you about fit.

## Map the complete system

There are five useful questions behind nearly every growth problem. Do the right people encounter you? Do they understand the relevance? Do they trust the promise? Can they take the next step without confusion? Does the experience justify the decision? These questions correspond loosely to discovery, understanding, evaluation, action, and delivery. They are diagnostic categories rather than an algorithm or a law of human behavior.

Consider a tutorial that brings visitors to a workshop page. Discovery happens through a LinkedIn post, an X conversation, or YouTube search. Understanding happens when the title connects debugging frustration to a concrete skill. Evaluation happens through a sample lesson, instructor evidence, prerequisites, and clear scope. Action happens through a functioning checkout. Delivery begins when the buyer receives access and knows what to do first. A missing access email can destroy an otherwise successful campaign.

Write your own map using actual destinations. “LinkedIn post → profile → diagnostic page → booking form → discovery call → proposal → signed agreement → onboarding” is more actionable than “awareness → consideration → conversion.” The second version names stages; the first tells you what to inspect. For a low-priced guide, the route may be shorter: tutorial → sample page → checkout → download. Do not force a sales call into a transaction that does not need one.

Some routes work through trust accumulated elsewhere. A person may see a YouTube lesson, follow you on LinkedIn, read two emails, and then purchase from an untagged bookmark. A last-click report might label that sale direct. The sale is real; the report's simplified explanation is incomplete. Keep financial truth in your order records, behavioral estimates in analytics, and customer explanations in interview notes. None of those sources alone fully describes the journey.

## Work through the arithmetic slowly

Suppose a hypothetical campaign generates 20,000 impressions. In this teaching model, two percent produce landing-page visits. Multiply 20,000 by 0.02 to get 400 visits. Ten percent of those visitors subscribe, producing 40 subscribers. Five percent of those subscribers purchase within the defined campaign window, producing an expected two purchases. At $49 each, expected gross order revenue is $98. The rates describe this invented sequential funnel; they are not platform benchmarks.

The general form is impressions × visit rate × subscription rate × subscriber purchase rate. Every rate is conditional on the preceding stage. This matters because a five percent purchase rate among subscribers cannot be substituted for a five percent purchase rate among impressions. The denominators are different. In the example, the overall impression-to-purchase rate is 0.02 × 0.10 × 0.05 = 0.0001, or 0.01 percent. Two purchases out of 20,000 impressions agrees with that calculation.

Now ask which improvement is valuable. Increasing the visit rate from two to three percent, holding everything else fixed, gives 600 visits, 60 subscribers, and three expected purchases. Improving the subscriber purchase rate from five to ten percent gives four expected purchases from the original 40 subscribers. Both are mathematically valid scenarios. Neither proves you can obtain the change, hold all other rates steady, or serve the resulting customers. Forecasts are conditional statements, not evidence that the conditions will occur.

Expected purchases can be fractional. A model might return 2.4 purchases. That means an average under repeated similar conditions, not that you will receive forty percent of an order. Real outcomes are discrete and variable. If you have only two sales, the next campaign might produce none even if the underlying business has not changed. Use the calculator to inspect relationships, then write down uncertainty in words rather than presenting a precise forecast as a promise.

For services, the useful sequence changes. Suppose thirty relevant people begin a legitimate conversation. Twelve agree to discovery, nine attend, six fit the offer, four request a proposal, and one becomes a client. Calculate stage rates separately: booking is 12/30, attendance is 9/12, qualification is 6/9, proposal progression is 4/6, and proposal acceptance is 1/4. A low total close rate might come from poor fit before the call rather than weak closing language.

## Revenue, contribution, profit, and cash are different

Gross order revenue is the total price of orders before deductions in the chosen model. Refunds reduce realized revenue. Transaction fees and delivery costs consume money. Contribution is what remains from revenue after the variable costs you explicitly include. Operating profit also subtracts fixed operating costs and other relevant expenses. Cash flow concerns when money arrives and leaves. A business can report attractive booked revenue and still struggle to pay bills if customers pay late or delivery costs arrive first.

Use a simple guide example. Twenty customers buy at $49, so gross revenue is $980. Assume one order is refunded in full: that removes $49, leaving $931 before other costs. Suppose the processor charges an illustrative $1.77 on every original transaction and none of those fees is recovered. Twenty times $1.77 is $35.40. Suppose support and fulfillment cost an expected $3 per original order: another $60. Contribution before acquisition is $835.60. This calculation is an invented fee model, not the fee schedule of a particular platform.

If the campaign cost $300 in ads, contribution after that spend is $535.60. If it also took ten hours of your time, the cash number does not explain whether the work was worthwhile. Assign an internal time value if useful. At an illustrative $40 per hour, those hours represent $400 of opportunity cost. The campaign then generated $135.60 after that additional allocation, before fixed overhead and tax. Calling the original $980 “profit” would hide the actual decision.

An internal time value is a planning convention, not necessarily a wage paid to yourself or an accounting treatment. Its purpose is to compare uses of scarce hours. A guide that produces modest revenue but creates a reusable asset may be worth more than a one-time campaign. A consultation that pays well but requires exhausting custom preparation may be less attractive than its headline fee suggests. Keep the monetary calculation separate from strategic benefits so that neither becomes an excuse to ignore the other.

The companion's Economics experiment uses a stated simplified formula. For one order, expected contribution before acquisition is price × (1 − refund probability) − percentage fee × price − fixed fee − delivery cost. Fees and delivery costs are assumed to apply to every original order. Real refund policies, fee reversals, taxes, chargebacks, discounts, and currency conversions can require a different model. Read the assumptions before changing an input, because a calculation is only useful when you know what it includes.

## Understand acquisition cost without inventing lifetime value

Customer acquisition cost, abbreviated CAC, is acquisition spending divided by acquired customers under a chosen scope. If a campaign spends $300 and produces ten new customers, media CAC is $30. If it requires another $200 in contractor work, fully including that cost gives $50 per customer. Label whether you include staff time, tools, production, sales labor, or only advertising. Comparing two CAC figures with different scopes produces false precision.

Break-even CAC is the contribution available before acquisition under your chosen horizon. If a first purchase contributes $38, spending $38 to acquire it leaves nothing for fixed costs or profit. A target CAC below that level provides a buffer. If you choose a target of $20, explain whether the $18 remainder covers overhead, uncertainty, or desired contribution. The target is a managerial choice, not an external rule that every business should use.

Lifetime value often tempts new marketers to justify losses. If you have not observed repeat purchases, do not assume each customer will buy three courses. Start with first-order contribution. As you accumulate cohorts, measure repeat behavior over a fixed window, such as ninety days or one year. A cohort is a group defined by a shared starting period or event. Comparing customers acquired in January with customers acquired in June requires allowing them similar time to repeat.

For a monthly community, distinguish contracted revenue, collected cash, and expected future payments. A customer paying $30 this month is not proof of twelve future payments. Even a yearly contract can involve cancellation, nonpayment, service obligations, or refunds. Forecast using explicit scenarios and retain the conservative case. In this course, we use repeated-customer contribution only when the hypothetical dataset actually contains repeated purchases or when a scenario is clearly labeled as an assumption.

## Choose a business model that matches your evidence

Services generally allow close contact with customers and can produce detailed learning quickly. They also consume delivery capacity. Digital products can be distributed repeatedly, but they require discovery, trust, product quality, support, and updates. Tutoring sells guided progress and therefore needs a clear teaching process. Cohorts combine group delivery with a schedule. Communities sell ongoing access or interaction and need ongoing value. These models solve different buyer problems; none is automatically passive.

Consider your developer background as a collection of possible advantages. You can demonstrate working systems, explain technical tradeoffs, create usable templates, and understand customer constraints. These advantages matter only when a buyer values them. A detailed explanation of event loops may attract learners; an assessment of an unstable deployment pipeline may interest a team leader. The content can share your voice while pointing to different offers. Early on, choose one primary commercial route to keep the experiment interpretable.

A useful beginning sequence is customer learning, a small scoped service or workshop, then a product built from recurring patterns. This is a suggested learning sequence, not a universal requirement. If you already have a well-tested course, you may start with distribution. If you have neither customers nor audience, spending months recording an enormous course makes demand harder to test. The purpose of a small offer is to reduce uncertainty while delivering something complete and worthwhile.

Opportunity cost constrains expansion. If you can spend ten hours per week, producing a polished long video, daily X commentary, three LinkedIn posts, a newsletter, and client work may exceed capacity. Choose a primary platform based on buyer access and format fit. Use a second platform for adapted distribution when the workflow works. The course covers all three so you can make informed choices; it does not require maximizing activity on all three simultaneously.

## Give each channel a job

LinkedIn can support professional credibility, relationships, and business conversations. X can support public discussion, rapid feedback, and connections within relevant communities. YouTube can support searchable explanations, demonstration, and sustained viewing. These are proposed roles for this business, not fixed properties that guarantee a result. A different buyer might prefer different channels. Channel choice begins with where the buyer learns and whom the buyer trusts.

A website gives you a place to explain an offer in your own structure. An email list gives you permission to send recurring messages under the terms of the subscription. Neither is absolute ownership of attention. Domains can fail, providers can change terms, and subscribers can leave. What you can build is portability: records you may lawfully retain, exportable content, backups, and clear customer relationships. Do not treat every social follower as someone who has consented to email marketing.

Map one job per channel for a trial period. For example, LinkedIn introduces a practical problem and starts conversations with team leads. YouTube shows the diagnostic process. A website describes the service. A permission-based email sequence helps interested people evaluate fit. The channel jobs make your analysis more useful: a YouTube video that produces few immediate purchases may still reduce repeated explanations on sales calls. Record that benefit through evidence rather than assuming it exists.

Be careful with cross-platform dependency. If every X post only tells people to leave X, the account offers little immediate value. If every LinkedIn post requires watching a thirty-minute video to understand the point, many readers will not receive the promised lesson. Adapt each piece so it stands on its own and make the next step optional. Repurposing should preserve the useful insight while changing presentation for the reading or viewing situation.

## Learn persuasion as decision support

People often hesitate because they are uncertain about relevance, effort, risk, credibility, or timing. Good marketing reduces uncertainty with clear explanations and evidence. It does not require manufacturing anxiety. If a workshop needs intermediate TypeScript knowledge, say so. If an implementation service excludes ongoing maintenance, make that visible. A customer who declines because of an honest boundary saves both parties a poor engagement.

Trust has several practical components. Competence means you can do the work. Integrity means your descriptions are accurate. Care means you consider the customer's situation rather than forcing a sale. Reliability means you do what you said you would do. A code demonstration can support competence. A clear cancellation policy supports integrity and predictability. A recommendation to use a free resource when appropriate supports care. These observations are a working framework for practice, not a validated scoring instrument.

Specificity helps evaluation, but specificity alone is not proof. “Reduce debugging time by forty percent” sounds concrete; without a defined measurement and credible evidence it remains an unsupported claim. “In this demonstration, the reproduced failure was isolated in twelve minutes using the included checklist” is narrower and verifiable if the demonstration actually exists. Distinguish results under your control, like delivering a written assessment, from outcomes influenced by the buyer, like implementation speed.

Scarcity and urgency should come from real conditions. A live cohort has a real start date. A consultant may have limited delivery capacity. A discount can have a real expiration. A timer that resets for every visitor creates a misleading impression when the offer never changes. Use clear terms because they help a person decide. Avoid turning a refusal into a contest where the seller's persistence matters more than the buyer's judgment.

## Set goals that support learning and business health

There are input measures, intermediate outcomes, commercial outcomes, and guardrails. Input measures include research sessions completed or original demonstrations produced. Intermediate outcomes include qualified conversations or sample downloads. Commercial outcomes include customers, collected revenue, and contribution. Guardrails include refunds, complaints, support burden, and hours worked. A balanced view prevents one attractive number from concealing a damaging system.

Suppose you double publishing volume and increase impressions by thirty percent. If production time doubles, qualified conversations remain flat, and client delivery slips, the change may be undesirable. Another week might show lower reach but two highly relevant inquiries and a useful interview. Compare the result to the job of the channel and the constraints of the business. “Growth” should identify what is growing and why that matters.

Use a baseline period. During the first two weeks, record what you do and what happens without trying to optimize every detail. Baselines are imperfect but give you something better than memory. Record missing values as missing, not zero. Zero purchases means you observed no purchases. An unavailable purchase report means you do not know. These states require different responses and should remain distinct in your tracking sheet.

Choose a review window appropriate to the decision. Technical errors can be fixed immediately. A recurring content series needs several pieces before you can judge its pattern. A long sales cycle needs enough time for decisions to mature. Avoid changing a strategy every few hours because the first post underperformed. Also avoid clinging to a strategy for months when repeated buyer evidence shows a fundamental mismatch. The later experimentation chapter teaches how to make that distinction more systematically.

## Diagnose a bottleneck without blaming the wrong stage

Imagine that a workshop page receives two hundred visits and no purchases. It is tempting to rewrite the price immediately. First establish whether the visits were real, whether the purchase mechanism worked, and whether the visitors matched the intended learner. Open the page on another device, inspect the mobile layout, follow every link, and test the transaction using a permitted test mode or a clearly recorded internal purchase. A broken checkout and a weak offer produce the same visible sales count, but require different repairs.

Next examine the route into the page. If a broad beginner tutorial sends people to an advanced workshop, the apparent conversion problem may be an audience mismatch. Read the promise in the original content and the promise on the page together. A person who expected free introductory notes will reasonably hesitate when encountering a paid technical clinic. This does not mean paid offers are wrong. It means the transition should accurately explain the next step before asking the reader to invest attention or money.

Then inspect the evidence for the offer itself. Can a suitable buyer tell what they receive, what they need beforehand, how much work it requires, and what result is outside the promise? Does the page contain a useful sample, or only adjectives about quality? If buyers ask the same scope question repeatedly, that is evidence of a communication gap. If they understand the scope and consistently describe a different urgent problem, the offer may need to change. Distinguishing these explanations prevents cosmetic edits from replacing customer learning.

Finally consider sample size and timing. Two hundred visits might include repeat visitors, automated traffic, existing customers, or people who return later. A new page with a few days of traffic offers limited evidence about a long buying cycle. Record what you know: two hundred recorded visits, no recorded purchases within seven days, and perhaps three detailed questions. Do not convert that into a universal claim that the market will never buy. Equally, do not dismiss every disappointing result as too small to matter. Repeated specific objections can justify research before a large dataset exists.

## Establish a small evidence hierarchy

For this course, use four evidence labels. An observation records something you actually saw, such as a failed checkout or a customer's description of their current process. A pattern is a repeated observation across relevant cases. A hypothesis explains why the pattern might exist. A decision states what you will do next. These labels keep a compelling story from quietly turning into an established fact. They also make disagreement productive: two people can agree on the observations while testing different explanations.

Consider a post that attracts ten comments asking for code. The observation is that ten comments asked for code. A possible pattern is repeated interest in implementation detail. One hypothesis is that readers want a runnable template; another is that they want to verify your claim. The decision could be to publish a small complete example and observe how people use it. Selling a large course immediately would be a much bigger leap, because a request for code does not establish willingness to pay for a learning program.

Store enough context to make an observation interpretable: date, audience situation, source, exact question when permission allows, and your confidence. Avoid retaining unnecessary personal information. A short note saying “technical owner preparing a launch, asked how to reproduce failures across browser versions” can be more useful than a large collection of names. The goal is better decisions and respectful relationships. The research chapter develops this discipline into interviews, competitor analysis, and offer validation.

## A complete first-week operating example

Build Clearly begins with a hypothesis: small development teams repeatedly lose time to extension onboarding failures and may value a scoped diagnostic. On Monday, the founder writes what counts as the target situation: a real extension, active users or testers, a repeated onboarding issue, and someone responsible for fixing it. This is a hypothesis about fit, not a purchased contact list. The founder identifies places where such people voluntarily discuss the problem and listens before offering anything.

On Tuesday, the founder records a demonstration using a synthetic extension. The demonstration shows one failure, the reproduction steps, a diagnostic trace, and a fix. No customer result is claimed. On Wednesday, the founder writes a short LinkedIn explanation aimed at the person responsible for onboarding reliability. The next step is a public diagnostic checklist, not an immediate demand for a call. On Thursday, the founder responds to substantive questions and notes the language people use.

On Friday, the founder conducts two voluntary research conversations. One person mainly needs a beginner tutorial. Another has a live system but lacks permission to change it. Neither is automatically a qualified consulting lead. The founder records what would have to be true for the service to help. The week ends with a revised hypothesis: the diagnostic may fit technical owners preparing a launch more closely than general extension enthusiasts.

Nothing in that week proves scalable demand. It does produce reusable evidence: a clear demonstration, audience language, a narrower segment, and better qualification criteria. This is progress even if no sale occurs. The alternative would be declaring the channel broken after a single post, or declaring success because several friends liked it. The course repeatedly returns to the discipline of asking what the evidence actually supports.

## Your 70-hour learning sequence

Use the twelve study hours to read this chapter in sections, reproduce the arithmetic, and inspect the corresponding calculator assumptions. You do not need to read for twelve uninterrupted hours. Spend some time examining your current offers or public profile as a buyer would. Identify terms you cannot define, and write your own definitions with observable examples. Keep this document small enough that you will actually refer to it during work.

Use the twelve guided-practice hours to build three business-system maps: one for a guide, one for a workshop, and one for a service. Calculate a deliberately conservative first-order economic scenario for each. State exactly what is missing from each model. If you have no fee schedule or delivery data, mark the input as assumed and use a range. Do not search for a universal conversion rate to fill every blank; a range is more honest than a falsely authoritative average.

Use the twenty-two production hours to create an initial offer page draft, a simple measurement sheet, an evidence folder, and a one-page channel strategy. The page can remain private. Include buyer, problem, deliverable, boundaries, next step, and the assumptions needing validation. Your measurement sheet should distinguish activity date from purchase date, and include hours. The evidence folder should separate observations, interpretations, and decisions so that you can revise an interpretation without rewriting what happened.

Use the sixteen field-application hours to observe real questions, conduct voluntary conversations where feasible, and inspect your current buying process. If you cannot contact people, use public discussions and label the work as desk research; do not pretend it establishes willingness to pay. Test your own links and download flow using synthetic data. Field application means confronting reality where possible, not completing a prescribed number of unsolicited messages.

Use the eight review hours to identify the largest unresolved assumption, revise the business map, and choose the next chapter's research question. A useful output is: “I can explain the route from a relevant person to a delivered result, but I do not yet know whether the problem is important enough to pay to solve.” That sentence leads naturally to customer research. Completion is judged by a coherent system and honest evidence, not by hitting a follower or revenue target.

## Troubleshooting the first decisions

If you cannot choose an audience, narrow by a shared situation rather than an identity label alone. “Developers” is enormous. “Developers shipping their first paid browser extension who cannot reproduce onboarding errors” gives you something to investigate. If that becomes too narrow to find people, broaden one dimension and observe whether the problem remains coherent. Segmentation is a learning instrument; it should help you see patterns rather than trap you in a clever sentence.

If you feel compelled to buy tools before starting, list the task each tool would perform. A plain document can hold research notes. A spreadsheet can track a small pipeline. A basic recording setup can show a demonstration. Buy software when you have a specific constraint and a reasonable comparison, not because a tool implies professional status. The marginal time saved should matter more than the number of features in a dashboard.

If your current audience mainly contains peers, do not assume that audience is commercially useless. Peers can buy learning materials, recommend you, collaborate, or introduce opportunities. Also do not assume peer approval equals buyer demand. Separate the education offer from the consulting offer and observe which people engage with which problem. Your next action is to gather evidence about buyer situations, which is the central problem of Chapter 2.
