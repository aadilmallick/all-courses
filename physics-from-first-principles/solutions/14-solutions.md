# 14. Solutions

1. Exactly one head has probability $\binom41/16=1/4$. At least one is $1-P(0)=1-1/16=15/16$.
2. Mean is $0(3/4)+4(1/4)=1$. The second moment is $16/4=4$, so variance is $4-1^2=3$ in squared units of the variable.
3. At mean 25, standard deviation is 5 and relative standard deviation is $5/25=0.20$. At mean 100 these are 10 and 0.10. More events increase absolute counting fluctuations while reducing fractional uncertainty.
4. $Z=1+3e^{-\beta\Delta}$. The probability of occupying any excited state is $3e^{-\beta\Delta}/Z$. At the given gap, $e^{-\beta\Delta}=1/3$, so this probability is $1/2$. Each individual excited state has probability $1/6$.
5. Differentiating gives $Z_\beta=-\sum E_i e^{-\beta E_i}$ and hence $-(\ln Z)_\beta=\sum p_iE_i=U$. Under $E_i\mapsto E_i+C$, $Z\mapsto e^{-\beta C}Z$, so the factors cancel in $p_i$, while mean energy becomes $U+C$.
6. $v_{\rm rms}(m)/v_{\rm rms}(4m)=\sqrt{4m/m}=2$.
7. $\bar n_F=1/(2+1)=1/3$ and $\bar n_B=1/(2-1)=1$. A bosonic state may contain more than one particle; its mean occupancy can exceed one. The probability of one particular occupation number still lies between zero and one.
8. With $x=\beta\hbar\omega\ll1$, $e^x-1=x+O(x^2)$, giving $\hbar\omega/(e^x-1)\sim\hbar\omega/x=k_BT$. The classical oscillator has $p^2/(2m)$ and $m\omega^2x^2/2$, each contributing $k_BT/2$. The zero-point term is temperature independent and becomes negligible relative to thermal energy in this limit.
