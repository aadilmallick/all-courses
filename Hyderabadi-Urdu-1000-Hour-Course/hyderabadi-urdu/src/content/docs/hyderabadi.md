## A local form carries a relationship

The aim is comfortable family participation. Shared grammar and local speech work together: a speaker may say **hau** in one turn and **haan ji** in another, or switch to English for a topic while retaining local rhythm. The course does not prescribe a comic accent or treat all people from the Deccan as interchangeable. Dakhni’s labels and relationship with Urdu and Hindi are also identity questions; respect the name a speaker uses for their own language.

| Local form to recognize | Shared counterpart | What to check at home |
| --- | --- | --- |
| hau | haan / haan ji | An affirmative; listen for vowel quality and respectful replies. |
| nakko | nahin chahiye / mat, in appropriate contexts | Refusal, unwantedness or prohibition; not every statement negation. |
| kaiku | kyun | Why; tone can sound curious, teasing or challenging. |
| mereku / mere ko | mujhe | Dative/experiencer contexts; not a word-for-word substitute in every construction. |
| tereku / tere ko | tujhe / tumhe, with register differences | Familiarity matters. Do not address an elder this way by default. |
| ich | hi, in many focus contexts | Placement, attachment and emphasis vary; collect whole phrases. |
| kidhar | kahan | A place question heard beyond Hyderabad too; not exclusively Dakhni. |
| idhar / udhar | yahan / vahan | Here/there or this/that direction; shared colloquial usage also exists. |

**Hau, chai chahiye** — yes, I would like tea. **Shukriya, abhi chai nakko** — thank you, I do not want tea now (local, contextual). **Aap kaiku poochh rahe hain?** — why are you asking? That last question can sound confrontational even with **aap**; a softer context and tone matter. A useful repair is **Main samajhne ke liye poochh raha/rahi hoon** — I am asking to understand.

## Local grammar is more than a few words

You may hear contractions, auxiliaries left unspoken where shared textbook speech includes them, different case/agreement patterns, and aspect forms shaped by regional contact. There is no one mechanical conversion that turns shared Urdu into every household’s Dakhni. This course teaches the shared pattern explicitly, points out selected local alternatives, and asks you to collect complete family examples. Do not remove every **ne**, merge every auxiliary, or attach **ich** at random.

A grammatical form can be productive for one relative and something another relative only recognizes. Keep these two abilities separate. A beginner can understand **mereku** while answering respectfully with **mujhe**. Recognition comes before imitation when social meaning is uncertain.

## Recognize a few deeper local patterns

Some Dakkhini varieties put **bol ke** after a quoted message, where shared speech often introduces the message with **ki**. Treat **“Kal aao” bol ke kaha** as a recognition model: someone said “come tomorrow.” Do not translate its **bol ke** as a separate journey or action. This is different from mechanically inserting **bol ke** after every clause.

Research also describes a relative use of **so** after a verbal phrase: a frame like **kal aaye so ...** can identify the person who came yesterday. Shared speech might use **jo kal aaye the ...**. Do not assume the shared **so** meaning “sleep!” applies here. Collect a complete example from your family before producing this pattern.

These are selective recognition notes, not universal conversion rules. [Subbarao’s supplementary syntax material](https://drupal-s3fs-prod.s3.eu-west-1.amazonaws.com/resources/academic/3513/6680/1739/Subbarao_web_material_9780521861489.pdf), printed pages 52–58 and 193–197, discusses these contact-related Dakkhini constructions. Its documented varieties do not establish every household’s current usage.

## A family recording session that respects people’s time

Ask a willing speaker for five minutes, not a performance of an entire dictionary. Say: **Main apni Urdu behtar karna chahta/chahti hoon. Kya aap meri madad karenge?** — I want to improve my Urdu. Will you help me? Explain that the recording stays on your device unless you choose to share it. Let them decline or stop.

Choose five phrases the speaker actually says: welcoming someone, offering tea, checking a plan, refusing gently, ending a call. Ask for one natural-speed version and one clear version. Keep the whole sentence with its situation. Write a Roman transcript, English meaning, speaker relationship, and date. Do not ask the speaker to make a sentence “more Hyderabadi” by adding a stereotype.

In the speaking studio, upload a reference clip with its exact Roman transcript. Selection playback looks for that transcript before attempting synthetic speech. Select a smaller word only if you also have a recording for that word; a sentence recording cannot automatically be cut into accurate word clips. Keep the original files because browser storage can be cleared.

## A productive question after every conversation

Ask **Aap aam taur par isko kaise bolte hain?** — How do you usually say this? Then ask who they would use that version with. Record both the shared frame and the family version. A correction without its situation is easy to misuse. Over the course, collect at least fifty such pairs, ten polite refusals, ten ways to gain a turn, and ten short stories retold by two different relatives. These are personal expansion targets, not extra words counted toward the 1,000-entry core.

## What is verified and what needs a speaker

The shared grammar was cross-checked against institutional Hindi-Urdu materials. The source ledger identifies inspected and partially accessible dialect research. This is an authored course, not a native-speaker-reviewed dialect grammar. The local examples are cautious learning models, and native family review is still needed for your household’s pronunciation, acceptability, register, and preferred vocabulary. The website makes those corrections usable by letting you add recordings and keep a personal notebook.
