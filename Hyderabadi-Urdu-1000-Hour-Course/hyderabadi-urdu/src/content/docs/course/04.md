**80 hours · Prerequisites: chapters 1-3 · Goal: ask for what you need, buy everyday items, understand simple directions, and make considerate requests.**

## Turn a need into a manageable exchange

At a family meal you may know the noun **paani**, “water,” yet still hesitate. Should you name it, demand it, ask whether it exists, or explain that you need some? Language gives you several ways to reach the same practical goal, and each creates a different relationship. **Paani!** may work as an urgent warning. **Zara paani dijiye**, “Please give me some water,” is an ordinary considerate request. **Kya mujhe thoda paani mil sakta hai?**, “Could I have some water?” is more indirect.

This chapter teaches you to choose a request that fits the person and situation. You will order food, check quantities, compare options, refuse politely, and find a destination. Accuracy matters because a missing negative can change an order, a mistaken quantity can change a purchase, and an abrupt command can change the tone. You do not need theatrical bargaining or a stock “local accent” to sound connected to Hyderabad. Clear, warm, ordinary interaction is a better starting point.

## Need and want are different constructions

**Mujhe paani chahiye** means “I need/would like water.” Think “Water is needed to me,” then use natural English to understand it. **Mujhe** identifies the person with the need; **paani** is the thing needed; **chahiye** expresses that need. The grammatical pattern is different from **Main paani peeta hoon**, “I drink water.” Do not replace **mujhe** with **main** simply because English begins with “I.”

Change the person who needs something: **Aap ko kya chahiye?**, “What do you need/would you like?” **Ammi ko chai chahiye**, “Mother would like tea.” **Hamein do cup chahiye**, “We need two cups.” **Hamein** is a common “to us” form. You will also hear **hum ko**. Both belong to the same family of relationships introduced with **ko** last chapter.

**Chaahna** means “to want.” A male speaker can say **Main chai chahta hoon**; a female speaker **Main chai chahti hoon**. In a casual order, **Mujhe chai chahiye** is often a useful model. For an action you want to do, use the infinitive: **Main Urdu seekhna chahta hoon**, male, or **... chahti hoon**, female, “I want to learn Urdu.” The wanted action **seekhna** keeps its infinitive form in these beginner models.

Compare **Mujhe chai chahiye**, “I need/would like tea,” **Mujhe chai pasand hai**, “I like tea,” and **Main chai pee raha hoon**, “I am drinking tea,” male. Need, preference, and current activity are different meanings. A person can like tea without wanting a cup now. Hearing that distinction prevents you from treating every mention of tea as an order.

## Likes, dislikes, and taste

**Pasand** expresses liking: **Mujhe biryani pasand hai**, “I like biryani.” **Aap ko kya pasand hai?**, “What do you like?” Negate it with **nahin**: **Mujhe bahut meethi chai pasand nahin hai**, “I do not like very sweet tea.” The experiencer remains **mujhe**, not **main**. **Meethi** agrees with the feminine **chai**.

Useful taste words include **meetha/meethi**, “sweet”; **teekha/teekhi**, “spicy/hot with chili”; **namkeen**, “salty/savory”; **garam**, “hot in temperature”; **thanda/thandi**, “cold”; **taaza**, “fresh”; **achchha/achchhi**, “good.” **Garam** and **namkeen** do not change like **thanda/thandi** in these phrases. Distinguish **garam chai**, hot tea, from **teekha khana**, spicy food. English “hot” hides that difference.

Build “I would like tea with less sugar.” Start with **Mujhe chai chahiye**. Specify **kam cheeni**, “less sugar.” A clear order is **Chai mein cheeni kam rakhiye, please**, “Please keep the sugar low in the tea.” An even simpler two-clause version is **Mujhe chai chahiye. Cheeni kam, please**. Both communicate. The second relies on a shared ordering context rather than a fully explicit grammatical relationship.

For an allergy or medically important restriction, use a clear complete statement and confirm that the listener understands. For example **Mujhe moongphali se allergy hai**, “I am allergic to peanuts.” Do not rely on a quiz score, a synthetic voice, or a half-understood menu when a restriction matters. Learn the exact food name and phrase with a competent speaker, and repeat the restriction when ordering. This is language practice serving a real communicative need.

## Give, take, and receive

**Dena** means “to give”; **lena**, “to take”; **milna**, “to be obtained/be available” in the useful construction here. **Main aap ko paani deta hoon**, male, “I give you water.” **Main chai leta hoon**, male, “I take/have tea.” A female speaker uses **deti hoon** and **leti hoon**. **Mujhe paani milta hai** means “I get water,” though the natural context determines the best English rendering.

In a shop, you may hear **Kya lenge?**, “What will you have/take?” The future-like form is a conventional service question; full future formation comes later. You can answer with a noun phrase: **Ek chai, please**, or **Mujhe ek chai chahiye**. At a meal, **Lijiye**, “Please take/have some,” is a considerate invitation. Reply **Shukriya** if accepting, or **Nahin, shukriya. Abhi nahin**, “No, thank you. Not now,” if declining.

Do not turn **lena** and **dena** into universal English “take” and “give” substitutions. Languages combine actions and objects differently. Learn **chai lena**, “have/take tea,” and **paani dena**, “give water,” in their actual situations. Later chapters introduce these verbs as helpers in expressions that add shades of completion or benefit; that is a separate use.

## Requests and commands have levels of address

A command or request asks someone to do something. Grammarians call this the imperative. The form changes with the relationship. Familiar **tum** forms often use **-o**: **bolo**, “speak”; **baitho**, “sit”; **aao**, “come”; **jaao**, “go”; **dekho**, “look.” Respectful **aap** forms often use **-iye**: **boliye**, **baithiye**, **aaiye**, **jaaiye**, **dekhiye**. A few very common verbs have special respectful forms: **dijiye**, give; **lijiye**, take; **kijiye**, do.

| Purpose | Respectful model | Familiar model |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Please sit. | Baithiye. | Baitho. |
| Please come here. | Yahaan aaiye. | Yahaan aao. |
| Please speak slowly. | Aahista boliye. | Aahista bolo. |
| Please look. | Dekhiye. | Dekho. |
| Please give water. | Paani dijiye. | Paani do. |
| Please take this. | Yeh lijiye. | Yeh lo. |
| Please do this. | Yeh kijiye. | Yeh karo. |

**Zara** softens many requests: **Zara yahaan dekhiye**, “Please look here for a moment.” **Meherbaani karke** means “please/kindly” more explicitly but can sound more formal. Tone, timing, and burden still matter. A polite verb ending does not make an unreasonable request considerate. Conversely, a close sibling may normally use a short familiar form affectionately. Learn your relationships from actual interaction.

To say “don't” in a shared instruction, use **mat**: **Yahaan mat baithiye**, “Please do not sit here”; **Jaldi mat kijiye**, “Please do not hurry.” **Nahin** normally denies a statement, while **mat** normally prohibits an action. A simple contrast is **Aap yahaan nahin baithte hain**, “You do not sit here habitually,” versus **Yahaan mat baithiye**, “Do not sit here.” Choosing the negative means choosing the intended speech act.

## Nakko in context

In many Hyderabadi households **nakko** expresses “don't,” “not wanted,” or refusal. **Chai nakko, shukriya** can mean “No tea, thank you.” **Fikr nakko karo** means “Don't worry” in a familiar local style; shared **Fikr mat karo** is also useful. The respectful shared form is **Fikr mat kijiye**. Follow family models before choosing local imperative combinations for elders.

Do not use **nakko** for every English “not.” For “I am not at home,” use **Main ghar par nahin hoon**. For “This is not my bag,” use **Yeh mera bag nahin hai**. The local word has its own distribution; substituting it everywhere does not produce native speech. A useful learning card has three parts: an actual local example, its shared alternative, and a situation in which you heard it.

**Hau** is a local affirmative in many homes: **Hau, thoda sa**, “Yes, a little.” **Kaiku** asks why; at a meal **Kaiku?** after a refusal may sound like curious family concern or unwanted pressure according to tone. You may decline without giving a long explanation: **Abhi nahin, shukriya**. Conversational competence includes maintaining a preference courteously.

## Count, measure, and confirm the price

Start with useful order quantities: **ek cup chai**, one cup of tea; **do rotiyaan**, two flatbreads; **aadha kilo**, half a kilogram; **ek kilo**, one kilogram; **thoda paani**, a little water; **thodi cheeni**, a little sugar. **Thoda/thodi** follows the noun's gender: masculine **paani**, feminine **cheeni**. **Thode se chaawal**, a little/some rice, uses the plural-like pattern common with **chaawal**.

**Kitna/kitni/kitne** asks how much/how many. **Kitna paani?**, how much water; **kitni cheeni?**, how much sugar; **kitne cup?**, how many cups. In a price question, **Yeh kitne ka hai?** is a common model for “How much is this?” For feminine **kitaab**, **Yeh kitaab kitne ki hai?** can be used. You will also hear **Is ki qeemat kya hai?**, “What is its price?” The latter phrase avoids some ellipsis and is broadly understandable.

Learn **rupaye**, rupees; **pachaas**, fifty; **sau**, hundred; **do sau**, two hundred; **paanch sau**, five hundred; **hazaar**, thousand. For an amount you do not recognize, ask **Kitne rupaye? Zara phir se boliye**, “How many rupees? Please say it again.” Repeat the amount back: **Do sau?**, “Two hundred?” Confirmation is a skill, not a sign that you should abandon the transaction.

**Aur kuchh?** means “Anything else?” **Bas**, “that's all/enough,” closes an order. **Bas, shukriya** is useful at both a counter and a family table. **Bill dijiye, please** requests the bill. **Cash ya card?** is a common mixed-language service question. English loans coexist with Urdu/Hindi grammar naturally; learn the local transaction rather than manufacturing formal equivalents nobody around you uses.

## Compare options without a special comparative ending

English changes “cheap” to “cheaper.” Urdu/Hindi often places the comparison standard before **se**: **Yeh us se sasta hai**, “This is cheaper than that,” for a masculine item. For a feminine item, **Yeh us se sasti hai**. Add **zyada** when needed: **Yeh zyada mehnga hai**, “This is more expensive.” **Kam**, less, can also make a comparison: **Is mein cheeni kam hai**, “There is less sugar in this.”

Build “This tea is sweeter than that tea.” Identify the topic **yeh chai**. Identify the comparison **us chai se**. Choose the matching adjective **meethi** and add **hai**: **Yeh chai us chai se zyada meethi hai**. In a context with two cups, **Yeh us se zyada meethi hai** is enough. A more explicit phrase is useful while learning and when a listener might confuse the referent.

**Sab se** introduces a superlative comparison, “most/of all”: **Yeh sab se sasta hai**, “This is the cheapest.” Claims about “the best biryani” are personal opinions, not course facts. Use **Mujhe yeh zyada pasand hai**, “I like this more,” to state your preference without implying that a whole city agrees. That frame is especially useful in warm family disagreements about food.

## Directions are a listening sequence

Learn **seedha**, “straight”; **daayen**, “right”; **baayen**, “left”; **paas**, “near”; **door**, “far”; **aage**, “ahead”; **peeche**, “behind”; **mod**, “turn/corner”; **raasta**, “route/way”; **sadak**, “road”; **dukaan**, “shop.” Directions often combine commands and landmarks: **Seedha jaaiye. Phir daayen mudiye**, “Go straight. Then turn right.” **Phir** orders the steps.

Ask **Dukaan kahaan hai?**, “Where is the shop?” For a more considerate opening, **Maaf kijiye, yeh dukaan kahaan hai?** Add a known name if appropriate. **Yahaan se kitni door hai?** asks “How far is it from here?” **Kya main paidal jaa sakta hoon?**, male, “Can I go on foot?” A female speaker uses **sakti hoon**. We introduce this ability frame here because it is useful; the next stages develop modals systematically.

When you hear several steps, repeat the sequence rather than each isolated noun: **Pehle seedha, phir baayen?**, “First straight, then left?” **Pehle** means first. Ask **Daayen ya baayen?**, “Right or left?” if uncertain. A route understood incorrectly at the first junction cannot be rescued by accurate pronunciation of the final shop name.

## Three practical dialogues

### At a family meal

| Roman Urdu/Hindi | English |
| --- | --- |
| Aur biryani lijiye. | Please have some more biryani. |
| Thodi si, please. Bahut achchhi hai. | A little, please. It is very good. |
| Raita bhi chahiye? | Would you like raita too? |
| Hau, thoda sa. | Yes, a little, local affirmative. |
| Chai? | Tea? |
| Abhi chai nakko, shukriya. | No tea now, thank you, local refusal. |
| Theek hai. | All right. |

The speaker accepts one thing and declines another. **Thodi** matches the understood feminine **biryani**; **thoda** matches the masculine **raita**. Natural short answers depend on the preceding turn. When practicing alone, say the question as well as the answer so you preserve the relationship.

### Ordering at a counter

| Roman Urdu/Hindi | English |
| --- | --- |
| Aap ko kya chahiye? | What would you like? |
| Do chai aur ek plate samose, please. | Two teas and one plate of samosas, please. |
| Cheeni kitni? | How much sugar? |
| Ek chai mein cheeni kam rakhiye. | Please put less sugar in one tea. |
| Aur kuchh? | Anything else? |
| Bas. Kitne rupaye? | That's all. How many rupees? |
| Do sau rupaye. | Two hundred rupees. |
| Do sau? Theek hai. | Two hundred? All right. |

The amount is an invented teaching example, not a current price. **Do chai** is common order shorthand for two servings of tea. **Ek plate samose** uses counter-style speech. A fuller phrase can specify **samoson ki ek plate**. Learn to understand ordinary ellipsis without assuming that every word-by-word shorthand is a universal formal grammar rule.

### Asking for the way

| Roman Urdu/Hindi | English |
| --- | --- |
| Maaf kijiye, pharmacy kahaan hai? | Excuse me, where is the pharmacy? |
| Seedha jaaiye. Phir baayen mudiye. | Go straight. Then turn left. |
| Pehle seedha, phir baayen? | First straight, then left? |
| Haan. Dukaan bank ke paas hai. | Yes. The shop is near the bank. |
| Yahaan se kitni door hai? | How far is it from here? |
| Zyada door nahin hai. | It is not very far. |
| Shukriya. | Thank you. |

Repeat directions on a simple drawn map with a partner. Put two possible destinations on it so that the listener cannot succeed by guessing the only visible shop. Swap roles. This creates a real information gap: each person must understand something the other knows.

## Exercises with more than one natural answer

Translate: 1. I would like water. 2. Please give me two cups of tea. 3. I like biryani. 4. Please do not sit here. 5. This room is bigger than that room. 6. Please go straight and then turn right. 7. No tea now, thank you, in a familiar Hyderabadi style. 8. Please keep the sugar low.

Answers: 1. **Mujhe paani chahiye**. 2. **Mujhe do cup chai dijiye, please**; **Do cup chai dijiye** is sufficient in context. 3. **Mujhe biryani pasand hai**. 4. **Yahaan mat baithiye**. 5. **Yeh kamra us kamre se bada hai**; the comparison phrase uses oblique **kamre** before **se**. 6. **Seedha jaaiye aur phir daayen mudiye**. 7. **Abhi chai nakko, shukriya**; shared **Abhi chai nahin chahiye, shukriya** is also natural. 8. **Cheeni kam rakhiye**.

Interpret: 9. **Mujhe chai pasand hai, lekin abhi chai nahin chahiye**. 10. **Yeh us se sasti hai**. 11. **Daayen ya baayen?** 12. **Mereku paani chahiye**, in a household that uses this local form.

Answers: 9. “I like tea, but I do not want/need tea now”; preference does not entail a current order. 10. “This is cheaper than that,” with a feminine item understood. 11. “Right or left?” 12. “I need/would like water”; shared **Mujhe paani chahiye**. The local form does not tell you the speaker's gender.

Repair these: **Main chai chahiye**; **paani dijiye** addressed with an angry bark; **Yeh mera bag nakko hai**; **Yeh kursi us se sasta hai**. The first becomes **Mujhe chai chahiye**. The second already has respectful grammar, but needs a calm delivery and sensible timing. The third becomes **Yeh mera bag nahin hai**. The fourth becomes **Yeh kursi us se sasti hai**. Language repair may concern sentence structure, social delivery, or both.

## Listening, speaking, and transfer

For a listening test, have a partner read five orders varying drink, sugar, quantity, and acceptance/refusal. Write both the Roman message and its English meaning. The partner then reads two similar routes, one turning right and one left. Draw the destination rather than merely translating. Actions reveal whether the listening succeeded.

For speaking, record a ninety-second role-play with both sides of an order. Then have a person interrupt with an unavailable item: **Chai nahin hai**, “There is no tea.” Ask **Coffee hai?**, “Is there coffee?” and change the order. This unscripted adjustment is the bridge from memorized dialogue to conversation. Do not count a perfectly memorized order as proof that you can manage an unavailable item, changed price, or misunderstood quantity.

## The 80-hour allocation and an A1-style checkpoint

| Activity | Hours | Active work |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Need, preference, and request instruction | 12 | Compare meanings and build requests for real situations. |
| Imperative and register practice | 10 | Switch familiar and respectful models without mixing them. |
| Food, quantities, and price listening | 12 | Hear orders, confirm amounts, and correct changed requests. |
| Directions and comparison practice | 10 | Navigate maps and compare concrete options. |
| Dialogue, dictation, and shadowing | 10 | Listen before reading; repeat after a delay. |
| Family and transaction missions | 16 | With consent, order, offer, refuse, and ask for directions. |
| Companion tests and error repair | 6 | Revisit errors affecting quantity, negation, or respect. |
| Review and checkpoint | 4 | Complete a new meal, purchase, and map scenario. |
| **Total** | **80** | Cumulative course time: 290 hours. |

The checkpoint combines the first four chapters. Introduce yourself, describe four relatives, explain a daily routine, locate objects, order a simple meal, decline an extra serving, confirm a price, and follow a short route. You may request repetition. The listener should understand your main message without having to translate every word into English. This is a course checkpoint inspired by beginner communicative goals, not an accredited CEFR examination.

Ask your family which local requests sound ordinary and which sound too abrupt for you to use with elders. Record one warm offer, one refusal, and one reassurance with permission. These three little exchanges contain rhythm and relationship information that a synthetic voice cannot certify. Keep them as models while the next stage gives you the grammar to describe what happened yesterday and tell the stories behind your family's everyday life.
