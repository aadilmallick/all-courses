**85 hours · B1 development · Prerequisites:** **mujhe/aapko**, present and past auxiliaries, adjective agreement, and the advice/obligation patterns from Chapter 6. The central problem is expressing what someone experiences without translating every English “I am” or “I have” word for word. You will also distinguish your own belongings from someone else’s, offer practical care, and discuss a disagreement without making it worse.

## Experience can be the center of a sentence

An English speaker says “I am hungry.” A common Urdu/Hindi sentence is **Mujhe bhook lagi hai**. Its pieces are **mujhe**, to me; **bhook**, hunger, a feminine noun; **lagi**, the feminine form of **lagna**; and **hai**, the present auxiliary. Think “Hunger has come upon me” as a temporary learning bridge. The natural English translation is still “I am hungry.” The bridge explains why the speaker is **mujhe** and why **lagi** does not change when a man says it.

Similarly, **Mujhe thand lag rahi hai** means “I feel cold.” **Thand** is feminine, so **lag rahi** follows the feeling word, not the person experiencing it. A male speaker does not change it to **lag raha** to match himself. By contrast, **Main thak gaya hoon** means “I have become tired,” for a man, while a woman says **Main thak gayi hoon**. Both sentences describe a personal state, but the language organizes them differently. Learn the construction with each expression.

A **dative experiencer** is a useful name for the person marked with **ko** or a fused form such as **mujhe**, **tumhe**, **humein**, or **unhein**, when that person experiences a feeling, need, perception, or mental state. The label does not mean every feeling uses this structure. **Main khush hoon**, “I am happy,” is an ordinary subject-plus-adjective sentence. Knowing the contrast lets you speak accurately without trying to invent one universal emotional formula.

## A small set of experience patterns

| Roman sentence | Meaning | What to notice |
|---|---|---|
| Mujhe bhook lagi hai. | I am hungry. | **Bhook** is feminine. |
| Mujhe pyaas lagi hai. | I am thirsty. | **Pyaas** is feminine. |
| Mujhe thand lag rahi hai. | I feel cold. | The feeling is ongoing. |
| Mujhe garmi lag rahi hai. | I feel hot. | **Garmi** is feminine. |
| Mujhe neend aa rahi hai. | I am sleepy. | Literally sleep is coming. |
| Mujhe dar lag raha hai. | I feel afraid. | **Dar** is masculine here. |
| Mujhe gussa aa raha hai. | I am getting angry. | **Gussa** is masculine. |
| Mujhe aapki yaad aa rahi hai. | I miss you. | **Yaad** is feminine. |
| Mujhe yeh baat samajh aayi. | I understood this point. | The understood point is feminine. |
| Mujhe iska pata nahin tha. | I did not know about it. | Learn **pata hona** as a chunk. |

Do not memorize only the first column without attending to its noun. **Mujhe dar laga** and **mujhe thand lagi** differ because **dar** and **thand** have different genders. Learn each as a phrase with one natural response: **Kya hua?**, “What happened?”; **Paani lijiye**, “Have some water”; **Andar aa jaiye**, “Please come inside”; **Kya main madad karun?**, “Shall I help?” A feeling expression is most useful when you can participate in the next turn.

The local **mereku** can occupy the same role as **mujhe** in many Hyderabadi family sentences: **Mereku neend aa rahi hai**, “I am sleepy.” The shared form is **Mujhe neend aa rahi hai**. Retain the rest of the learned structure unless your family provides a different model. **Tereku** belongs to familiar relationships; **aapko** remains a clear respectful choice with elders. A local accent does not require using the most intimate pronoun with everyone.

## Move the same experience through time

Once the construction is clear, tense becomes easier. **Mujhe bhook lagi hai** is hunger now. **Mujhe bhook lagi thi** describes hunger at a past point. **Mujhe subah jaldi bhook lagti hai** means “I get hungry early in the morning,” a habitual pattern. **Mujhe baad mein bhook lagegi** means “I will get hungry later.” In all four, **bhook** remains the feminine center of agreement.

Work through “Ammi was feeling cold.” Choose the experiencer **Ammi ko**. Choose **thand**. Choose an ongoing past viewpoint **lag rahi thi**. The result is **Ammi ko thand lag rahi thi**. Some families use respectful plural auxiliaries around elders in particular constructions, but with impersonal experience expressions the feeling noun’s agreement remains important. Do not automatically force every verb into plural just because Ammi is respected. Listen to the specific construction rather than generalizing from **Ammi aa rahi hain**.

Compare **Mujhe gussa aaya**, “I became angry,” with **Main gusse mein tha/thi**, “I was angry.” The first reports anger arising; the second describes the state. **Gusse mein** has the oblique form because of **mein**. **Main gussa hoon** is also heard, but the chunk **gusse mein** is useful for a clear description. These alternatives let you say whether an event caused a reaction or merely describe how someone felt.

## Knowing, understanding, remembering, and forgetting

**Jaanna** means to know a fact or person: **Main unko jaanta/jaanti hoon**, “I know him/her/them.” **Mujhe pata hai** means “I know” in the sense that information is known to me. **Mujhe maloom hai** is another common Urdu expression. **Mujhe Urdu aati hai** means “I know Urdu/I can speak Urdu,” with the language conceived as a skill that comes to the person. **Main Urdu jaanta/jaanti hoon** is also possible, but **aati hai** is a productive skill expression.

**Mujhe samajh aaya** can mean “I understood,” with an understood masculine idea left implicit; **Mujhe yeh baat samajh aayi** explicitly uses feminine **baat**. **Main samajh gaya/gayi** describes the person coming to understand. If someone asks **Samajh aaya?**, answer **Ji, samajh aaya** or **Abhi poori tarah nahin**, “Not fully yet.” You need not repeat every component of the full sentence when the conversation already supplies it.

**Mujhe yaad hai** means “I remember.” **Mujhe yaad aaya** means “I remembered/it occurred to me.” **Main bhool gaya/gayi** means “I forgot.” These are not interchangeable pieces. **Main aapka naam bhool gaya/gayi** means “I forgot your name”; use it with an apology if appropriate. **Mujhe aapka naam yaad nahin aa raha** means “I cannot recall your name right now,” which can soften the social effect by describing temporary retrieval difficulty.

Build a repair request with these expressions: **Maaf kijiye, mujhe yeh lafz samajh nahin aaya. Iska matlab kya hai?** “Excuse me, I did not understand this word. What does it mean?” **Lafz** is masculine, so **aaya**. Replace **lafz** with feminine **baat** and use **aayi**. This is a small controlled change with an immediate use in family conversations.

## Apna keeps ownership tied to the subject

English “his” can be ambiguous. In “Ali called his brother,” is it Ali’s brother or someone else’s? Urdu/Hindi can use **apna** to mean the subject’s own: **Ali ne apne bhai ko phone kiya**, “Ali called his own brother.” **Ali ne uske bhai ko phone kiya** generally points to another person’s brother, with the exact reference supplied by context. **Apna** is called a **reflexive possessive** because it refers back to the subject of its clause.

Like **ka/ki/ke** and **mera/meri/mere**, **apna/apni/apne** follows the possessed thing and its case, not the possessor’s gender. **Sara apni kitaab padh rahi hai** means “Sara is reading her own book.” **Ali apni kitaab padh raha hai** means “Ali is reading his own book.” The same **apni** appears because **kitaab** is feminine. **Sara apna bag laayi** means “Sara brought her own bag,” with masculine **bag** in ordinary usage.

Before a postposition, the noun phrase becomes oblique: **apne bhai ko**, **apne ghar mein**, **apni behen se**. Build “I spoke to my own sister”: the doer is **main**; **baat karna** in the completed event gives **maine…baat ki**; the person spoken to uses **se**; **behen** is feminine, giving **apni behen se**. The result is **Maine apni behen se baat ki**. For now keep the possessive inside its own clause. Longer sentences can have more complex reference; when uncertain, name the person again.

**Khud** means self: **Main khud karunga/karungi**, “I will do it myself.” **Apne aap** can mean by oneself or automatically: **Darwaza apne aap khul gaya**, “The door opened by itself.” Do not substitute **apna** for **khud** everywhere. **Apna** accompanies a noun or stands for an owned thing; **khud** emphasizes the person; **apne aap** has its own adverbial uses. Each solves a different ambiguity.

## Each other, together, and looking after people

**Ek doosre** expresses reciprocity, where two or more people act toward one another. **Hum ek doosre ki madad karte hain** means “We help each other.” **Vo ek doosre se baat kar rahe hain** means “They are talking to each other.” The postposition comes from the action: **madad** uses **ki**, talking uses **se**, and calling someone can use **ko**. Learn the reciprocal phrase together with the verb’s relationship.

**Saath** means together/with: **Hum saath rehte hain**, “We live together”; **Main Ammi ke saath rehta/rehti hoon**, “I live with Ammi.” Being together is not automatically reciprocal action. Two people can **saath khaana khana**, eat together, without feeding each other. This distinction prevents elaborate but unintended sentences when a simple **saath** is enough.

Care often uses **khayal rakhna**, to look after or take care of: **Apna khayal rakhiye**, “Take care of yourself,” respectfully. **Main apne chhote bhai ka khayal rakhta/rakhti hoon**, “I look after my younger brother.” The relationship is **ka khayal**, and **rakhna** changes with the construction. **Ammi ne mera khayal rakha** means “Ammi looked after me,” with masculine **khayal** guiding the perfective form.

## Describing discomfort clearly

This section teaches communication, not diagnosis. **Mere sar mein dard hai** means “My head hurts/I have a headache.” **Sar** is masculine; **dard** is commonly masculine. **Mere gale mein dard hai**, my throat hurts; **Mere pet mein dard hai**, my stomach hurts; **Mujhe bukhar hai**, I have a fever; **Mujhe khaansi hai**, I have a cough. The useful information is location, timing, severity in ordinary words, and what help is needed.

To describe onset, use **kab se**, since when: **Aapko kab se dard hai?**, “How long have you had the pain?” Answer **Subah se**, since morning; **kal se**, since yesterday; **do din se**, for two days. **Bahut dard ho raha hai** means “It hurts a lot.” **Thoda behtar hai** means “It is a little better.” **Dard badh raha hai** means “The pain is increasing.” These phrases describe the experience without naming a cause you do not know.

**Mujhe doctor se baat karni hai** means “I need to speak with a doctor.” **Kya aap mere saath chal sakte/sakti hain?** means “Can you come with me?” **Mujhe is dawai se allergy hai** means “I am allergic to this medicine.” If you are uncertain, say **Mujhe theek se maloom nahin**, “I do not know exactly,” rather than guessing. Medication names, doses, and urgent instructions require careful confirmation in a language both parties understand. The course does not use health role plays to award diagnostic skill.

## Offer care without taking over

Begin with **Aap kaisa mehsoos kar rahe/rahi hain?**, “How are you feeling?” **Kya main kuch laun?**, “Shall I bring something?” is an offer, not an assumption about what the person needs. **Aapko kis cheez ki zaroorat hai?** asks “What do you need?” If the person declines tea, **Theek hai, zaroorat ho toh batayiye**, “All right; tell me if you need anything,” respects the answer.

**Nakko** can decline or discourage in some Hyderabadi family contexts: **Chai nakko, shukriya**, “No tea, thank you.” The shared alternative is **Chai nahin chahiye, shukriya**. Tone and relationship affect how abrupt the short form sounds. A learner who is unsure can use **Abhi nahin, shukriya**, “Not right now, thank you,” and add **Baad mein loonga/loongi**, “I will have some later,” only if that is true. Politeness is not a requirement to make a false promise.

## Disagreement, feelings, and boundaries

**Aap hamesha aisa karte hain**, “You always do this,” can turn a small problem into a claim about someone’s character. A more specific account is **Kal jab baat adhoori reh gayi, mujhe bura laga**, “Yesterday, when the conversation was left unfinished, I felt bad.” You will learn the full **jab** connection in Chapter 8; for now use two sentences: **Kal baat adhoori reh gayi. Mujhe bura laga.** Describe the event, the feeling, and a reasonable request.

**Mera matlab yeh nahin tha**, “That is not what I meant,” repairs an interpretation. **Main aapki baat samajhta/samajhti hoon, lekin…**, “I understand your point, but…,” acknowledges the other person without agreeing. **Is waqt main is baare mein baat nahin karna chahta/chahti**, “I do not want to discuss this right now,” states a limit. With an elder, keep **aap** and a calm delivery; respectful language does not require surrendering the practical meaning of your boundary.

Affection also depends on family style. **Mujhe aapki yaad aati hai**, “I miss you,” is widely useful. **Aapse baat karke achha laga**, “It was nice talking to you,” is a warm closing. **Aapne bahut madad ki, shukriya**, “You helped a lot; thank you,” names the care received. Do not memorize an emotional speech and use it regardless of the situation. Specific, truthful words often build more connection than dramatic phrases.

## Three dialogues

### Tea and a small refusal

**Phuppi:** Chai piyoge/piyogi? — Will you have tea?

**Learner:** Abhi nahin, shukriya. Mujhe neend aa rahi hai; main thodi der aaraam karunga/karungi. — Not now, thank you. I am sleepy; I will rest for a while.

**Phuppi:** Khaana khaya? — Have you eaten?

**Learner:** Hau, kha liya. Aapne khaya? — Yes, I have. Have you eaten?

**Phuppi:** Abhi nahin. — Not yet.

**Learner:** Kya main aapke liye kuch garam karun? — Shall I warm something for you?

The learner returns care rather than only declining it. **Aapke liye** means for you. A local cousin might say **Chai nakko**, but the longer refusal here fits an elder and gives a clear reason.

### Checking on a relative

**Learner:** Aaj kaisa mehsoos kar rahe hain? — How are you feeling today? Addressing a man respectfully.

**Relative:** Kal se behtar hoon, lekin gale mein dard hai. — I am better than yesterday, but my throat hurts.

**Learner:** Kya aapko paani chahiye? — Would you like water?

**Relative:** Hau, thoda sa. — Yes, a little.

**Learner:** Yeh lijiye. Agar kisi se baat karni ho, toh batayiye. — Here you are. If you need to talk to someone, tell me.

**Relative:** Shukriya. Tum apna kaam karo. — Thank you. You do your work.

The conversation does not invent a diagnosis. It checks comfort, offers a concrete action, and respects the relative’s decision. The pronoun difference is natural: an elder may use **tum** with a younger person who uses **aap** in return.

### Whose phone?

**Cousin:** Ali mera phone le gaya? — Did Ali take my phone away?

**Learner:** Nahin, vo apna phone le gaya. Tumhara phone mez par hai. — No, he took his own phone. Your phone is on the table.

**Cousin:** Achha, mereku laga mera phone tha. — Oh, I thought it was my phone. Shared: **Mujhe laga mera phone tha.**

**Learner:** Dono ek jaise hain. Main bhi pehle samajh nahin paya/payi. — Both are alike. I did not understand at first either.

**Cousin:** Theek hai. Hum ek doosre ka phone na lein! — All right. Let us not take each other’s phones!

**Apna** resolves ownership; **mujhe laga** introduces an impression; **ek doosre ka** introduces reciprocity. The dialogue combines grammar to solve a small real ambiguity rather than displaying unrelated rules.

## Exercises with answer keys

### A. Choose and explain

1. A man says he is thirsty: **Mujhe pyaas laga/lagi hai**.
2. A woman says she is afraid: **Mujhe dar lag raha/rahi hai**.
3. Ali reads his own book: **Ali apni/uski kitaab padhta hai**.
4. Sara takes her own bag: **Sara apni/apna bag leti hai**.
5. “We help each other”: **Hum saath ki/ek doosre ki madad karte hain**.
6. “I remember”: **Mujhe yaad hai/Main yaad hoon**.
7. “My stomach hurts”: **Mere pet mein dard hai/Main pet hoon**.
8. “Take care of yourself,” respectful: **Apna khayal rakhiye/Apni khayal rakho**.

**Answers:** 1. **Lagi**, because **pyaas** is feminine. 2. **Lag raha**, because **dar** is masculine, regardless of speaker. 3. **Apni** identifies Ali’s own book; **uski** would normally point elsewhere. 4. **Apna**, because **bag** is treated as masculine here. 5. **Ek doosre ki** gives the reciprocal relationship. 6. **Mujhe yaad hai** is the remembered-information construction. 7. **Mere pet mein dard hai** names location and discomfort. 8. **Apna khayal rakhiye** combines masculine **khayal** with a respectful request.

### B. Translate and vary

Translate: “I miss you”; “Ammi was feeling cold”; “I forgot my own book”; “Can you come with me?”; “I need to talk to a doctor”; “That is not what I meant”; “I do not know exactly”; “We live together”; and “It was nice talking to you.” Then change present to past in two of your answers.

**Possible answers:** **Mujhe aapki yaad aa rahi hai** describes missing the person now; **Mujhe aapki yaad aati hai** describes the recurring feeling. **Ammi ko thand lag rahi thi.** **Main apni kitaab bhool gaya/gayi.** **Kya aap mere saath chal sakte/sakti hain?** **Mujhe doctor se baat karni hai.** **Mera matlab yeh nahin tha.** **Mujhe theek se maloom nahin.** **Hum saath rehte hain.** **Aapse baat karke achha laga.** The first sentence can become **Mujhe aapki yaad aa rahi thi**, and the living arrangement can become **Hum saath rehte the**. Context decides whether a past form is appropriate; grammar is not a reason to misstate the facts.

### C. Find the reference

Compare **Sara ne apni behen ko bulaya** and **Sara ne uski behen ko bulaya**. Who is the sister in each? The first normally means Sara’s own sister. The second refers to someone else’s sister, whose identity must already be clear. Now make the sentence unambiguous for a listener who missed the previous turn: **Sara ne Ali ki behen ko bulaya**. Repeating a name can be clearer than extending a chain of pronouns. Your goal is mutual understanding rather than using the shortest possible sentence.

### D. Respond appropriately

A relative says **Mujhe neend aa rahi hai**. Offer one practical response without commanding them. A possible answer is **Kya aap aaraam karna chahenge/chahengi? Main baad mein phone kar sakta/sakti hoon**, “Would you like to rest? I can call later.” If they say no, accept the answer. A cousin says **Mereku bura laga**. Ask **Kya hua? Agar tum batana chaho, toh main sun sakta/sakti hoon**, “What happened? If you want to tell me, I can listen.” These are offers of conversation, not promises to solve someone’s problem.

## Listening and speaking work

Use four short recordings: hunger, tiredness, missing a relative, and an ownership misunderstanding. On the first pass identify the experience or problem. On the second, type the Roman sentence and an English translation. On the third, attend to **mujhe/main**, **apna/uska**, and the verb endings. These small forms carry relationships that a list of emotion vocabulary cannot teach. If a family speaker uses **mereku**, record both that phrase and a shared **mujhe** equivalent.

For speaking, record three versions of the same message: a twenty-second practical request, a one-minute explanation, and a three-minute conversation with interruptions. Example: you are tired, you need to postpone a call, and you propose another time. A partner asks why and offers a different time. Evaluate whether the message stayed respectful and clear after the interruption. Speech recognition may transcribe a dialect form poorly; use a human listener for pronunciation and do not treat a machine transcript score as a judgment about your accent.

For the family-language project, ask how your relatives naturally say “I miss you,” “I am tired,” “Take care,” and “Do you need anything?” Save their preferred Roman spellings beside the course baseline. Ask about differences between speaking to a cousin and an elder. There may be no single answer for the whole family. Record who uses which form without labeling one generation’s speech the only authentic version.

At exit, explain three different personal experiences accurately; ask a caring follow-up; distinguish your own possession from another person’s; use **ek doosre** with an appropriate relationship; and manage a five-minute practical conversation about a changed plan or small misunderstanding. A partner should be able to identify the key facts without reading your preparation. Revisit any pattern that repeatedly changes who experienced or owned something. Chapter 8 connects these individual sentences into longer stories and explanations while keeping those references clear.
