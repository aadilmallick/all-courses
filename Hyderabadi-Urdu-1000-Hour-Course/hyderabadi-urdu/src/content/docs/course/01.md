**60 hours · Starting from zero · Goal: enter a short conversation, introduce yourself, and ask for help.**

## Begin with a real reason to speak

Imagine arriving at a relative's home. Someone opens the door, welcomes you, and asks how you are. You do not need a discussion of every tense. You need a greeting, a respectful way to say “you,” a sentence about yourself, and a way to ask the person to slow down. This chapter builds that small conversation carefully enough that you can change it when a different person opens the door.

The course teaches the shared conversational grammar of Urdu and Hindi, with a deliberate focus on Hyderabadi family speech. These varieties overlap substantially in everyday conversation. Vocabulary, pronunciation, social setting, religion, education, and family history affect what any particular person uses. “Hyderabadi” does not mean that every sentence contains a special local word. A relative may switch between local Dakhni forms, broadly shared Urdu/Hindi, Telugu, and English within one conversation. Your aim is understanding and connection, followed by increasingly confident local choices.

All language examples use Roman letters. You will not need to learn another writing system. Roman spelling is a learning aid rather than a universally standardized representation. Your relatives may write the same word differently in messages. Learn the sound and meaning first, then recognize spelling variation. Keep a small personal list with three columns: our course spelling, my relative's spelling, and a recording or memory of how they say it.

## A usable first exchange

Read this exchange aloud slowly. The names are fictional. The greeting shown fits many Muslim Urdu-speaking families; choose the greetings your own family uses.

| Speaker | Roman Urdu/Hindi | English |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Samira | Assalaam alaikum, Khala. | Peace be upon you, Auntie. |
| Khala | Wa alaikum assalaam. Aap kaisi hain? | And upon you be peace. How are you? |
| Samira | Main theek hoon. Aap kaisi hain? | I am well. How are you? |
| Khala | Main bhi theek hoon. | I am well too. |
| Samira | Main Urdu seekh rahi hoon. | I am learning Urdu. |
| Khala | Achchha! | Oh, good! |
| Samira | Zara aahista boliye, please. | Please speak a little slowly. |

Khala means a mother's sister. Detailed family relationships come next chapter. For now, treat the address as one useful name for a person. Samira uses **rahi** because this example has a female speaker. A male speaker says **Main Urdu seekh raha hoon**. We will explain the entire ongoing-action construction in chapter 3; here it is a phrase you can use immediately. Learning a useful phrase before analyzing all its parts is reasonable when the meaning and situation are clear.

Notice that politeness operates in more than one place. **Aap** is the respectful “you”; **hain** matches it; **boliye** is a respectful request. Adding the English word “please” does not repair a disrespectful pronoun. Choose a respectful complete frame, then relax into family usage as people invite you to do so.

## Roman letters as sound instructions

English spelling is an unreliable guide to Urdu pronunciation. In this course, **aa** usually cues a long open vowel, as in **naam**, “name.” **Ee** cues a long high vowel, as in **theek**, “well/correct.” **Oo** cues a long rounded vowel, as in **door**, “far.” Some familiar spellings retain a single letter: **aap**, **hai**, **mein**, **Urdu**, **chai**. Follow the model audio or a relative's pronunciation rather than forcing every spelling into an English reading rule.

The contrast between short and long vowels can distinguish words. **Kal** means yesterday or tomorrow according to context; **kaal** is a different word used in other senses. You do not need the second word as active vocabulary now. You need to hear that vowel duration belongs to the word. Stretching every vowel equally makes a sentence harder to recognize. Say **aap**, then **naam**, then **Aap ka naam?** with a small pause between phrases; gradually connect them.

**Main**, meaning “I,” and **mein**, meaning “in,” both contain a nasalized vowel in ordinary pronunciation. Nasalization means some airflow passes through the nose while the vowel continues. It does not require a strong separate English “n” after the vowel. Say a comfortable vowel, then let air resonate in the nose without closing the tongue against the roof of the mouth. Compare that sensation with a full **n**, where the tongue blocks the mouth. A native model is more valuable than exaggerated imitation.

Our ordinary Roman spelling does not distinguish every sound. In particular, **t** and **d** can represent dental or retroflex sounds. For a dental sound, the tongue touches near the upper teeth. For a retroflex sound, the tongue tip curls or draws back toward the ridge behind them. English **t** is not an exact substitute for either. Ask a willing relative to say **teen**, “three,” and **theek**, “well,” while you watch and listen. Do not press hard or force the tongue. Comfortable, recognizable speech is the goal.

**Th**, **dh**, **ph**, and **bh** often indicate a consonant with a breath release, called aspiration. **Th** is not the English sound in “think.” Start with a clear **t**, release a small puff of air, and keep the two parts close together. Hold a palm near the mouth to notice the difference between a plain release and an aspirated one. **Ph** is not automatically **f**, although English loanwords and individual speakers introduce variation. **Ch** is the affricate heard in **chai**; **chh** adds stronger breath.

**Kh** in **khana**, “food/to eat,” has a breathy release after **k**. In words such as **khair**, “well-being,” some speakers use a fricative sound made with air passing through a narrow space toward the back of the mouth. **Gh** also has more than one realization across vocabulary and speakers. **Q** in words such as **waqt**, “time,” may sound farther back than **k**, or may merge with it in everyday speech. Build listening flexibility rather than judging relatives against a single accent.

## Practice sound with meaning attached

Use this short sound sequence for five minutes at a time: **naam**, **aap**, **theek**, **teen**, **do**, **door**, **hai**, **hain**, **hoon**. First hear a model; next speak along with it; finally say the word after a short silence. Speaking simultaneously is called shadowing. Repeating after silence is a different task: it makes you retrieve the sound rather than borrow its timing. Alternate them.

Record **Main theek hoon** three times at comfortable volume. Listen for three chunks: **main**, **theek**, **hoon**. Check that **theek** does not become two syllables like “thee-ik.” Check that the final vowel in **hoon** is sustained and nasalized rather than followed by an exaggerated English “n.” Compare your recording with a family member only if they are willing. Ask “Which one word is hardest to understand?” A request for one concrete correction is easier to answer than “Is my accent good?”

Browser speech is synthetic and voice availability varies. A device may have an Urdu voice, a Hindi voice, or neither. A Hindi voice is not evidence of Hyderabadi pronunciation. Local words such as **kaiku** may be misread from Roman spelling even by an Urdu voice. Use provided capability labels and, whenever possible, your family's recordings. Do not train yourself to reproduce a machine's mispronunciation.

## Build a sentence from its job

A sentence can identify someone, describe something, locate something, or report an action. Begin with identification and description. English places a linking verb between a person and a description: “I am well.” Urdu puts the linking form near the end: **Main theek hoon**. The linking verb is often called a copula. That name only means the word connects the subject to information about it.

Build the sentence in three steps. Choose **main**, “I.” Choose **theek**, “well.” Add the linking form that belongs with **main**, which is **hoon**. The result is **Main theek hoon**. Replace the person with **aap**, respectful “you.” The ending must become **hain**: **Aap theek hain**. The sentence now means “You are well.” Word replacement and ending replacement work together.

| Person | Basic linking form | Example | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| main | hoon | Main yahaan hoon. | I am here. |
| tum | ho | Tum yahaan ho. | You are here, familiar. |
| aap | hain | Aap yahaan hain. | You are here, respectful. |
| yeh | hai | Yeh chai hai. | This is tea. |
| woh | hai | Woh ghar hai. | That is a house. |
| hum | hain | Hum yahaan hain. | We are here. |
| yeh, plural | hain | Yeh log yahaan hain. | These people are here. |
| woh, plural | hain | Woh log wahaan hain. | Those people are there. |

**Yeh** can refer to this, he, or she when the person is near or part of the current conversation. **Woh** can mean that, he, she, or they according to context. These pronouns do not themselves specify male versus female. English translation requires information from context. You may hear **ye** and **vo**; these are pronunciation and spelling variants, not separate beginner pronouns to memorize.

The respectful form **aap** takes **hain** even when one person is addressed. Respectful reference to an elder can also take plural agreement: **Ammi yahaan hain**, “Mother is here.” This is grammar serving a social relationship. Do not conclude that more than one mother is present. Conversely, **tum** may refer to one familiar person or several; number and politeness overlap in this system.

## “You” is a relationship choice

Use **aap** with elders, unfamiliar adults, and anyone whose preference you do not know. **Tum** is common with friends, siblings, cousins, and people who invite familiarity, but households differ. **Tu** exists and can express intimacy, devotion, anger, or condescension depending on context. Recognize it in other people's speech. Do not adopt it as a default because an online comedy clip uses it.

When someone addresses you with **tum**, that does not automatically mean you should return it. An older relative may use familiar address to a younger relative while expecting respectful address back. If in doubt, keep **aap**. A useful question is **Main aap ko kya bulaaun?**, “What should I call you?” Learn this as a whole polite phrase. It lets your relative tell you the name or title they prefer.

**Ji** can acknowledge someone respectfully or follow a name/title: **ji**, **Ammi ji**, **haan ji**. It does not have one fixed English translation. Tone matters. A clipped, sarcastic **ji** can sound very different from a warm one. Copy the relationship and rhythm you hear, rather than attaching **ji** to every word.

## Ask, answer, and keep the turn moving

Many yes/no questions use the same order as statements with questioning intonation: **Aap theek hain?** Initial **kya** can also mark a yes/no question: **Kya aap theek hain?** Here **kya** does not ask for the name of a thing. In **Yeh kya hai?**, it does: “What is this?” The position and sentence structure help determine the job.

Use **haan**, “yes,” and **nahin**, “no/not,” in shared conversation. In many Hyderabadi families, **hau** is a local affirmative. Try **Hau, main theek hoon** with a relative who uses it. Keep **haan** as an equally useful shared choice. **Nakko** is especially useful for “don't” or “not wanted”; it is not a mechanical replacement for every **nahin**. This distinction protects you from sounding local in one word and unclear in the whole sentence.

To negate a basic description, put **nahin** before the linking form: **Main taiyaar nahin hoon**, “I am not ready.” **Yeh chai nahin hai** means “This is not tea.” In ordinary speech an ending may be omitted when it is recoverable, but keep complete forms while learning. The full structure makes contrasts visible and helps you transfer the pattern to new sentences.

For conversation, a response plus a return question is more useful than a one-word response. **Main theek hoon. Aur aap?** means “I am well. And you?” **Aur** means “and,” and in this position invites the other person to take a turn. **Bhi**, “also/too,” follows the part being included: **Main bhi theek hoon**, “I am well too.” Say it after hearing that the other person is well; it connects your answer to theirs.

## Three conversations you can adapt

### Meeting a cousin

| Roman Urdu/Hindi | English |
| --- | --- |
| Assalaam alaikum. Main Aamir hoon. | Hello. I am Aamir. |
| Wa alaikum assalaam. Main Imran hoon. | Hello. I am Imran. |
| Aap kaise hain? | How are you? |
| Main theek hoon. Tum kaise ho? | I am well. How are you? |
| Main bhi theek hoon. | I am well too. |
| Yahaan aao. | Come here, familiar. |

The final line is an invitation to a younger or familiar person. For an elder, use **Yahaan aaiye**. Learn the two complete versions rather than combining **aap** with a familiar command. “How are you?” uses **kaise** for a male addressee or mixed/plural group and **kaisi** for a female addressee. You will see adjective agreement next chapter; for now, keep the question attached to the person you are greeting.

### A short video call

| Roman Urdu/Hindi | English |
| --- | --- |
| Aap theek hain? | Are you well? |
| Haan, main theek hoon. Aap kahaan hain? | Yes, I am well. Where are you? |
| Main ghar par hoon. | I am at home. |
| Achchha. Sab theek hai? | Good. Is everything all right? |
| Haan, sab theek hai. | Yes, everything is all right. |
| Phir milte hain. | See you again. |

**Ghar par** is a location phrase, “at home.” The small word **par** follows **ghar**; the system of words after nouns comes in chapter 3. Treat this location as one chunk now. **Sab theek hai** is a useful fixed reassurance; **sab** here means everything. **Phir milte hain** is a conversational leave-taking phrase and does not require you to analyze its verb yet.

### Repairing a misunderstanding

| Roman Urdu/Hindi | English |
| --- | --- |
| Aap ka naam? | Your name? |
| Maaf kijiye. Phir se boliye. | Sorry. Please say it again. |
| Aap ka naam kya hai? | What is your name? |
| Mera naam Samira hai. | My name is Samira. |
| Aap Urdu samajhti hain? | Do you understand Urdu? |
| Thodi si. Zara aahista boliye. | A little. Please speak a little slowly. |

**Samajhti** suits a female addressee; **samajhte** suits respectful address to a male. **Thodi si** is a short answer meaning “a little” here. Repair is successful communication. It is better to request one repetition than to nod through a question you did not understand.

## A small first vocabulary that does work

Build associations around useful pairs: **yahaan/wahaan**, “here/there”; **haan/nahin**, “yes/no”; **achchha/theek**, “good/all right”; **main/aap**, “I/you”; **naam/ghar**, “name/home.” Add **Urdu**, **Hindi**, **English**, **zara**, “a little/just” in requests, **aahista**, “slowly,” **phir se**, “again,” **maaf kijiye**, “excuse me/sorry,” and **shukriya**, “thank you.”

Do not spend this week memorizing a hundred disconnected nouns. Take **Yeh kya hai?**, point at an object, hear the answer, and repeat **Yeh ... hai**. One frame makes many words useful. Pointing also checks that you and your partner mean the same thing. If you cannot identify the object from the explanation, ask for a gesture or a different example rather than guessing silently.

## Hear the question before preparing the answer

A beginner often hears **aap**, recognizes “you,” and starts preparing **Main theek hoon** before the question ends. That works for **Aap kaise hain?** but fails for **Aap kahaan hain?**, “Where are you?” Train yourself to wait for the information-bearing word. **Kaise/kaisi** asks about condition or manner in these greetings; **kahaan** asks location; **kya** asks what. The opening pronoun alone cannot identify the question.

Ask a partner to alternate **Aap kaise hain?**, **Aap kahaan hain?**, and **Aap ka naam kya hai?** First answer only in English to check meaning. Next answer in Roman Urdu/Hindi: **Main theek hoon**, **Main ghar par hoon**, or **Mera naam ... hai**. Finally have the partner change the order unpredictably. If you answer the wrong question, return to listening for the question word rather than repeating the response more quickly.

Connected speech makes word boundaries less obvious than a printed table suggests. A speaker may join **kahaan hain** closely, or shorten a familiar greeting. Listen once for the overall purpose and again for the words you can identify. You do not need to hear every sound perfectly before responding. You do need enough evidence to distinguish a request for your name from a request for your location. When that evidence is missing, **Phir se boliye** is the accurate conversational move.

## Exercises with explained answers

### A. Assemble and understand

1. Put these words in a basic sentence: **theek / hoon / main**.
2. Make “You are here” respectful: **aap / yahaan / ...**.
3. Translate **Yeh chai nahin hai**.
4. Ask a female elder how she is.
5. Translate “I am well too.”
6. Distinguish **Main yahaan hoon** from **Main ghar mein hoon**.

Answers: 1. **Main theek hoon**; the linking form closes the sentence. 2. **Aap yahaan hain**; respectful **aap** selects **hain**. 3. “This is not tea”; **nahin** negates the identification. 4. **Aap kaisi hain?** 5. **Main bhi theek hoon**; **bhi** includes the speaker. 6. “I am here” versus “I am inside the house”; **main** and **mein** are different words despite similar sounds. **Ghar par** means at home and need not emphasize being inside.

### B. Repair the interaction

You hear a question too quickly. Choose a response that keeps conversation possible: **Haan** to everything, **Zara aahista boliye**, or changing the subject. Say the best response and then add **Phir se boliye**. Now imagine the person repeats only the final word. Ask **Is ka kya matlab hai?**, “What does this mean?” Learn this as a complete phrase; its internal possession pattern becomes clearer in chapter 2.

The useful response is **Zara aahista boliye**. A successful repair specifies the problem and invites another attempt. If a person remains difficult to understand, say **English mein, please**, “In English, please,” when they share English. Returning to a shared language for one explanation is compatible with building Urdu ability.

### C. Listening and speaking

Have a partner read **Main theek hoon**, **Aap theek hain?**, **Yeh chai hai**, and **Yeh chai nahin hai** in random order. Keep the page hidden. Type what you heard in Roman letters and then its English meaning. Check meaning first, word sequence second, and spelling last. **Nahi** for **nahin** may represent the same familiar pronunciation; missing **nahin** reverses meaning and deserves immediate correction.

Then record a thirty-second self-introduction using a greeting, your name, a statement that you are learning Urdu, a request to speak slowly, and a return question. Play it back the next day. If a relative understands it without reading a transcript, you have stronger evidence than a high score on a spelling quiz.

## Use the 60 hours deliberately

| Activity | Hours | Concrete work |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Sound and listening foundation | 10 | Short daily sound comparisons, recording, listening again after a delay. |
| Guided sentence building | 10 | Change pronouns and linking forms; explain each change aloud. |
| Core words and phrases | 8 | Retrieve the first useful chunks from situations and pictures. |
| Dialogues and shadowing | 10 | Practice the three dialogues, then replace names and locations. |
| Family interaction and repair | 12 | Many short calls or visits with consent; log one understood and one missed message. |
| Written and listening exercises | 6 | Use companion translation, grammar, and dictation; review reasons for errors. |
| Review and recorded exit task | 4 | Repeat earlier material after several days and make a new introduction. |
| **Total** | **60** | Active study and attentive conversation, not background playback. |

At five hours a week this chapter takes twelve weeks; at ten hours it takes six. Split speaking into small sessions when possible. Ten careful minutes with a willing relative can produce more learning than an hour of distracted imitation. Never require a relative to become an unpaid teacher. Ask for a brief, specific interaction, accept a refusal, and use self-recording or another partner for the rest.

## Exit task and next connection

Complete a two-minute exchange without a written script. Greet someone appropriately, give your name, ask how they are, answer, say that you are learning, and repair at least one deliberately unclear question. Acceptable performance means the partner can identify your intended meaning, your respectful forms are consistent most of the time, and you can recover when understanding breaks. Accent perfection and fast speech are not requirements.

Keep one version of the exchange in your family's preferred style. Ask which greeting, title, and affirmative sound most natural to them. Note their answer as family evidence rather than a rule for all Hyderabadis. Next chapter uses these same people to teach gender, description, possession, and the family relationships that give your conversations personal meaning.
