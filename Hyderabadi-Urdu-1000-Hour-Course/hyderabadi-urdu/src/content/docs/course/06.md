**85 hours · A2 toward B1 · Prerequisites:** present and past descriptions, infinitives such as **jaana**, polite address, and **mujhe/aapko**. Your goal is to arrange a family visit, explain what you can do, negotiate constraints, and change a plan politely. You will distinguish a promise from a possibility and advice from an unavoidable obligation.

## A plan is a conversation, not one future sentence

Suppose a relative asks **Kal aa sakte ho?**, “Can you come tomorrow?” A useful reply may need four pieces: **Kal kaam hai. Main shaam ko aa sakta hoon. Lekin mujhe jaldi wapas jaana padega. Kya hum saat baje mil sakte hain?** A male speaker says, “I have work tomorrow. I can come in the evening. But I will have to go back early. Can we meet at seven?” A female speaker uses **aa sakti hoon**. The sentence about having to go stays **mujhe…jaana padega** for either speaker.

Each piece performs a different job. **Kaam hai** gives a constraint. **Aa sakta hoon** expresses ability or practical possibility. **Jaana padega** presents an obligation. **Mil sakte hain?** proposes an arrangement. Learning them as interchangeable translations of English “can,” “must,” or “will” hides these differences. In conversation the difference matters: “I will come” creates a stronger expectation than “I might be able to come.”

Begin each practice exchange with a purpose. Are you promising, asking permission, offering help, making a tentative suggestion, or explaining why you cannot comply? Once you know the purpose, choose the construction. This is also how you preserve warmth in a family exchange: you can accept the relationship while negotiating the practical request.

## Build a straightforward future

The ordinary future of **karna**, to do, is **karunga** for “I will do” said by a man, and **karungi** said by a woman. **Hum karenge** means “we will do” for a masculine or mixed group; **hum karengi** can refer to an all-female group. **Tum karoge/karogi** is the familiar second person. **Aap karenge/karengi** is the respectful second person. **Vo karega/karegi** is “he/she will do”; respectful or plural **vo karenge/karengi** uses plural agreement.

| Subject | Masculine or mixed reference | Feminine reference |
|---|---|---|
| main | karunga | karungi |
| hum | karenge | karengi |
| tum | karoge | karogi |
| aap | karenge | karengi |
| vo, singular familiar reference | karega | karegi |
| vo, plural or respectful reference | karenge | karengi |

The **n** in first-person and plural forms can represent nasalization rather than a fully released consonant. Roman spelling does not capture every sound precisely; compare a trusted model’s recording. Do not lengthen every vowel merely because a word looks long. The course favors readable Roman forms, and ordinary messages may spell the same ending differently.

Use a small set of reliable future forms before extending the pattern: **jaunga/jaungi**, I will go; **aaunga/aaungi**, I will come; **loonga/loongi**, I will take; **doonga/doongi**, I will give; **hoonga/hoongi**, I will be; **khaunga/khaungi**, I will eat; **piyunga/piyungi**, I will drink. A relative may write **piyoonga**; that is a spelling choice. The learner’s task is to connect the sound and meaning, then use a consistent personal spelling.

Build “I will call you tomorrow” step by step. Choose **main** as the doer. Put the time **kal** before the verb. The person called is **aapko**. Use the familiar borrowed expression **phone karna**. The future follows the speaker, giving **Main aapko kal phone karunga/karungi**. There is no perfective **ne**: this is a future action. Change only the person making the call: **Meri behen aapko kal phone karegi**. The feminine third-person ending belongs to **behen**.

## Present forms can also refer to an arranged future

English says “I am coming tomorrow,” and Urdu/Hindi can also use a present progressive for an arrangement: **Main kal aa raha hoon/aa rahi hoon**. This often sounds like a plan already in place. **Main kal aaunga/aaungi** can be a future statement or promise. The distinction is contextual rather than a rigid rule that one form is always certain and the other uncertain. Tone, earlier agreement, and practical circumstances all contribute.

Do not add a future ending to every word in a sentence because **kal** means tomorrow. **Kal meri chhutti hai** means “I have a day off tomorrow.” It describes a scheduled fact with **hai**. **Kal dukaan band hai** means “The shop is closed tomorrow.” **Kal baarish hogi** predicts rain. Listen for what the speaker is presenting: a schedule, an arrangement, or a prediction.

To soften certainty explicitly, use **shayad**, perhaps, or **lagta hai**, it seems: **Shayad main der se aaunga/aaungi**, “Perhaps I will come late”; **Lagta hai kal baarish hogi**, “It looks like it will rain tomorrow.” Do not interpret **shayad** as a promise. When making a plan, ask a follow-up: **Toh main aapka intezaar karun?**, “So should I wait for you?”

## The subjunctive: an action being considered

A **subjunctive** presents an action as wished for, proposed, conditional, or otherwise not asserted as a fact. You do not need the label to speak, but you need the forms. With **karna**, use **main karun**, **hum karein**, **tum karo**, **aap karein**, and **vo kare** for a singular third person. The familiar **tum karo** also looks like an imperative; the surrounding sentence tells you its function. Subjunctive endings do not change for the speaker’s gender.

Compare **Main phone karunga**, “I will call,” with **Main phone karun?**, “Shall I call?” A woman says **karungi** in the future but still **karun?** in the proposal. **Hum kab chalein?** means “When shall we leave?” For an ordinary polite offer, use **Aap kya lena chahenge/chahengi?**, “What would you like to have?” **Aap kya lenge/lengi?** is an ordinary future-shaped service question, “What will you have?” Do not force every English conditional “would” into a subjunctive formula.

Useful irregular subjunctives include **main jaun**, I might/should go; **vo jaye**, he/she might/should go; **aap jayein**, you might/should go; **main aaun**, I might/should come; **vo aaye**, he/she might/should come; **main loon**, I might/should take; **aap lein**, you might/should take; **main doon**, I might/should give; **aap dein**, you might/should give; **ho**, be; **hoon**, I be; **hon**, we/you respectfully/they be. In this course **main ghar mein hoon** remains the familiar factual present “I am at home”; a subjunctive use is identified by its larger context, such as a wish or condition.

Start with proposals and conditions you can use today: **Main darwaza kholun?**, “Shall I open the door?” **Hum yahin baithen?**, “Shall we sit right here?” **Agar waqt mile, toh phone kijiye**, “If you get time, please call.” In the last sentence **mile** presents time becoming available as a condition; **kijiye** is the polite request already learned. The two clauses have different jobs and therefore need not use the same verb form.

## If this happens, then that will happen

**Agar** means “if”; **toh** often introduces the result. **Agar aap kal aayein, toh hum saath khaana khayenge** means “If you come tomorrow, we will eat together.” The first event is a condition, so **aayein** is subjunctive. The second is the predicted result, so **khayenge** is future. Informal speech may omit **agar** or **toh** when the relationship is clear; keep both while learning.

Build a condition by writing two plain ideas: “You have time” and “We can talk.” Make the first a possibility: **Agar aapke paas waqt ho**. Join the consequence: **toh hum baat kar sakte hain**. The result is **Agar aapke paas waqt ho, toh hum baat kar sakte hain**, “If you have time, we can talk.” Notice **aapke paas** expresses available possession. It does not turn into **aapko** because an English speaker might say “if you have.”

Avoid using **jab**, “when,” and **agar**, “if,” as exact synonyms. **Jab aap aayein, toh phone kijiye** means “When you arrive, please call”; the arrival is being treated as expected within the plan. **Agar aap aayein…** leaves open whether the visit will happen. Chapter 8 develops **jab…tab** as a general connection between events. Here the contrast protects a practical arrangement from misunderstanding.

## Sakna: ability and practical possibility

Put **sakna** after the bare verb stem. **Bolna** becomes **bol sakna**, being able to speak. The second verb carries the changing endings: **Main Urdu bol sakta hoon**, a man says, “I can speak Urdu”; a woman says **bol sakti hoon**. **Aap aa sakte hain?** asks a man or a mixed group whether they can come; **Aap aa sakti hain?** addresses a woman respectfully. **Hum madad kar sakte hain** means “We can help.”

Do not keep the **na** ending: **main bolna sakta hoon** is not the taught construction. Break it into meaningful pieces: **bol** names the action, **sakta/sakti** says able, and **hoon** anchors the sentence to the present first person. For past ability, **Main pehle tez daud sakta tha/sakti thi**, “I used to be able to run fast.” For future ability, **Main kal aa sakunga/sakungi**, “I will be able to come tomorrow.”

Permission can also use **sakna**, especially in ordinary polite requests: **Kya main yahan baith sakta/sakti hoon?**, “May I sit here?” More directly, **Kya main yahan baithun?** asks whether to sit here. Watch the relationship and the decision being requested. **Aap baith sakte hain** may mean “You may sit” or “You are able to sit,” so physical ability and permission require context.

## Pana: managing to do something

**Kar paana** focuses on successfully managing an action, often despite a constraint. **Main kal phone nahin kar paya/payi** means “I could not manage to call yesterday.” This is different from making a broad claim that you lacked the ability to use a phone. **Main aapki baat sun nahin paya/payi** means “I could not catch what you said.” The result did not happen in that situation.

Compare **Main Urdu bol sakta/sakti hoon**, “I can speak Urdu,” with **Main meeting mein Urdu bol paya/payi**, “I managed to speak Urdu in the meeting.” **Main samajh nahin saka/saki** is also a common expression, “I could not understand.” Natural usage overlaps; the purpose of the distinction is to give you a productive choice, not to prohibit one verb wherever English uses the same word.

In the completed ability construction, **Main kaam kar saka/saki** and **Main kaam kar paya/payi** ordinarily follow the subject. Do not add **ne** because **karna** appears inside them. The final verb and the entire construction matter. Compare plain perfective **Maine kaam kiya**, “I did the work,” with **Main kaam kar saka**, “I was able to do the work,” for a male speaker.

## Chahna and chahiye solve different problems

**Chahna** describes wanting: **Main ghar jaana chahta hoon/chahti hoon**, “I want to go home.” The person wanting is the ordinary subject, and **chahta/chahti** follows that person. **Main chai chahta hoon** is grammatically a statement that a man wants tea, but a household request usually sounds more natural as **Mujhe chai chahiye**, “I would like/need tea,” or a polite request such as **Chai milegi?**, “Could I have tea?”

With an action, **mujhe + infinitive + chahiye** commonly gives advice or a sense of what should happen: **Mujhe ab jaana chahiye**, “I should go now.” The speaker is **mujhe**, not **main**, and this sentence stays the same for male and female speakers. **Aapko aaraam karna chahiye** means “You should rest.” It is a language pattern; a real health decision belongs with qualified advice, not a grammar exercise.

With a direct object, the infinitive may agree: **Mujhe yeh kitaab padhni chahiye**, “I should read this book”; **Mujhe yeh khat padhna chahiye**, “I should read this letter.” **Kitaab** is feminine, **khat** masculine. At first, use frequent chunks such as **baat karni chahiye**, “should talk,” and **kaam karna chahiye**, “should work.” Do not change the infinitive because of the speaker’s gender in this construction.

## Padna and hona: constraints and arrangements

**Mujhe jaana padta hai** means “I have to go” as a recurring necessity. **Mujhe jaana pada** means “I had to go” on an occasion. **Mujhe jaana padega** means “I will have to go.” These use dative **mujhe** and present the action as required by circumstances. The form is not **main jaana padta hoon**. If the required action has an agreeing object, that can change the infinitive and verb: **Mujhe dawai leni padi**, “I had to take medicine,” with feminine **dawai**.

**Mujhe kal kaam karna hai** means “I have work to do tomorrow” or “I have to work tomorrow.” It often presents a scheduled task, obligation, or intention without foregrounding external pressure as strongly as **padna**. **Mujhe kal jaana hai** can mean “I am to go tomorrow” or “I need to go tomorrow.” Translation depends on context. The distinction is a conversational tendency rather than a mathematical scale of force.

Keep negative meanings separate. **Mujhe nahin jaana hai** can mean “I do not want/intend to go” in some contexts and “I am not to go” in others. **Mujhe jaana nahin padta** clearly says “I do not have to go.” **Aapko nahin jaana chahiye** means “You should not go.” Not having to do something is different from being advised not to do it. If an arrangement matters, add an explanation rather than relying on a potentially ambiguous short negative.

## Reasons and purposes

**Kyunki** gives a reason: **Main jaldi jaunga/jaungi kyunki kal kaam hai**, “I will leave early because I have work tomorrow.” **Isliye** gives a consequence: **Kal kaam hai, isliye main jaldi jaunga/jaungi**, “I have work tomorrow, so I will leave early.” Keep the relationship clear before trying a long sentence. The same information can be packaged in either order.

To give a purpose, use the oblique infinitive with **ke liye**: **Main aapse milne ke liye aaya/aayi hoon**, “I have come to meet you.” **Milna** changes to **milne** before **ke liye**. **Urdu seekhne ke liye main roz sunta/sunti hoon** means “To learn Urdu, I listen every day.” **Taaki** introduces a fuller purpose clause: **Dheere boliye taaki main samajh sakun**, “Please speak slowly so that I can understand.” It is useful but need not replace simpler sentences.

## Conversations with real negotiation

### Arranging a visit

**Khala:** Kal aaoge? — Will you come tomorrow? Addressing a male relative.

**Learner:** Hau, shaam ko aaunga. Aapko kuch chahiye? — Yes, I will come in the evening. Do you need anything?

**Khala:** Aate waqt doodh le aana. — Bring milk on your way.

**Learner:** Theek hai. Lekin mujhe aath baje nikalna padega. — All right. But I will have to leave at eight.

**Khala:** Toh saat baje aa jao. — Then come at seven.

**Learner:** Zaroor. Agar der ho, toh main phone karunga. — Certainly. If I am late, I will call.

The response accepts the visit, offers help, and states a limit early. A female learner substitutes **aaungi** and **karungi**. **Hau** is a local choice; **haan** works in wider conversation. **Aate waqt** is a useful “while/on the way” chunk; Chapter 8 will unpack such linking phrases.

### Declining without dismissing

**Cousin:** Aaj bahar chalte hain. — Let us go out today.

**Learner:** Aaj nahin ja sakta/sakti. Mujhe kaam khatam karna hai. — I cannot go today. I need to finish work.

**Cousin:** Kaiku? Thodi der ich chalenge. — Why? We will go for just a little while. Shared: **Kyun? Thodi der hi chalenge.**

**Learner:** Samajh raha/rahi hoon, lekin kal subah jaldi uthna hai. Kal shaam chalein? — I understand, but I need to get up early tomorrow. Shall we go tomorrow evening?

**Cousin:** Hau, kal theek hai. — Yes, tomorrow is fine.

**Ich** can add restrictive emphasis in local speech, somewhat like **hi**. Do not attach it to every phrase. The important interaction move is offering an alternative after a clear refusal.

### Asking for help learning

**Learner:** Kya hum roz das minute Urdu mein baat kar sakte hain? — Can we talk in Urdu for ten minutes every day?

**Relative:** Kar sakte hain. Kis baare mein? — We can. About what?

**Learner:** Ghar ki baatein. Agar main galti karun, toh pehle mujhe baat poori karne dijiye. — Household matters. If I make a mistake, please let me finish first.

**Relative:** Phir bataun? — Then shall I tell you?

**Learner:** Ji. Ek ya do galtiyan batayiye, taaki main yaad rakh sakun. — Yes. Point out one or two mistakes so that I can remember.

This asks for a manageable kind of help. A relative need not become a full-time teacher. Short, agreed practice can preserve the enjoyment of conversation and provide clearer correction.

## Exercises and explained answers

### A. Choose the intended meaning

1. A man promises to call tomorrow: **Main kal phone karun/karunga**.
2. A woman asks “Shall I call?”: **Main phone karungi/karun?**
3. “I can speak” for a woman: **Main bolna sakti/bol sakti hoon**.
4. “I had to leave”: **Mujhe jaana pada/Main gaya**.
5. “I do not have to go”: **Mujhe jaana nahin padta/Mujhe nahin jaana chahiye**.
6. “If you have time”: **Agar aapke paas waqt ho/Agar aap waqt hain**.
7. “I want to learn,” a man: **Main seekhna chahta hoon/Mujhe seekhna chahiye**.
8. “I should read this book”: **Mujhe yeh kitaab padhni chahiye/padhna chahti hoon**.

**Answers:** 1. **Karunga**, a promise. 2. **Karun?**, a proposal with no gender change. 3. **Bol sakti hoon**, with the bare action stem. 4. **Mujhe jaana pada** states necessity; **main gaya** only reports going. 5. **Mujhe jaana nahin padta** removes necessity; the other advises against going. 6. **Aapke paas waqt ho** expresses available time. 7. **Main seekhna chahta hoon** states desire; **chahiye** changes the message to should. 8. **Mujhe yeh kitaab padhni chahiye**, with feminine **padhni** following **kitaab**.

### B. Build and translate

Translate: “We will come at seven”; “Can you help me?”; “I could not manage to call yesterday”; “I need to work tomorrow”; “Shall we sit here?”; “Please speak slowly so that I can understand”; “If I get time, I will call”; and “You should not wait for me.” Make the male and female speaker changes where relevant.

**Possible answers:** **Hum saat baje aayenge** for a masculine/mixed group, or **aayengi** for an all-female group. **Kya aap meri madad kar sakte hain?** or **kar sakti hain?** according to the addressee. **Main kal phone nahin kar paya/payi.** **Mujhe kal kaam karna hai.** **Hum yahan baithen?** **Dheere boliye taaki main samajh sakun.** **Agar waqt mile, toh main phone karunga/karungi.** **Aapko mera intezaar nahin karna chahiye.** Other natural word orders can preserve the meaning; explain which word carries the relationship before judging spelling.

### C. Repair a plan

A cousin heard **Main aaunga** and prepared dinner, but you only meant that you might come. Repair it: **Maaf kijiye, mera matlab tha ki shayad main aa sakun. Maine pakka nahin kaha tha. Agli baar main saaf bataunga/bataungi.** “I am sorry; I meant that perhaps I might be able to come. I had not said it definitely. Next time I will make it clear.” This is a model, not a way to deny a promise you actually made. Practice explaining the misunderstanding and then proposing a concrete new arrangement.

## Listening, speaking, and the 85-hour plan

For listening, hide a six-line plan and hear it twice. Record who is coming, when, what they will bring, one constraint, and what happens if they are late. Then type the Roman phrases and their English meanings. Listen especially for **sakta**, **padega**, and **chahiye**: missing one of these can change a plan more than missing an adjective. Use actual family audio when available; a browser’s synthetic voice may not model Hyderabadi rhythm or pronounce Roman text reliably.

For speaking, draw three cards: an invitation, a constraint, and a possible alternative. Respond for ninety seconds without writing a full script. Your partner must change the time once and ask **Kaiku/kyun?** once. Record a second version with a different relationship: a cousin first, then an elder. Keep the practical meaning and adjust address, directness, and tone. Use **aap** for the elder rather than assuming local vocabulary removes the need for respect.

|---|---:|---|
| Read and rebuild future/modal examples | 10 | Twenty comparisons with meaning labels |
| Listen to plans and conditions | 14 | Ten dictations plus planning notes |
| Translation, form retrieval, and repairs | 12 | Eighty reviewed sentences |
| Pronunciation and recorded negotiation | 12 | Twelve short role plays |
| Family or partner planning conversations | 18 | Six arrangements with follow-up confirmation |
| Vocabulary and phrase retrieval | 8 | Useful verbs recalled in three modal patterns |
| Real visit or call planning project | 7 | One agreed plan, change, and recap |
| Exit task and focused review | 4 | Fresh scenario and correction log |
| **Total** | **85** | |

For the project, arrange a modest real event with a willing person: a call, tea, or a short walk. Confirm the time and what each person will do. Later explain what changed and why. Count preparation and review honestly, and avoid manufacturing an inconvenience just to practice. If you cannot arrange a live task, use a role play and label it as such.

At exit, you should negotiate a five-minute plan; distinguish will, might, can, should, and have to; give a reason; propose an alternative; and confirm the final arrangement. Aim to convey all critical details correctly, even if some endings need repair. Repeat with an unfamiliar scenario before treating the skill as stable. Chapter 7 brings these structures into feelings, relationships, and the experience-centered sentences families use every day.
