# 12. B2 capstone and lifelong conversation

**Time: 95 hours. Course total after this chapter: 1,000 hours. Target: independent, B2-oriented spoken family and everyday interaction.**

The course began with a greeting and a way to ask someone to repeat. It ends with a much richer version of the same relationship: you can take part, show interest, make your meaning clear, and ask for help when needed. A confident speaker does not know every word. A confident speaker has ways to keep a meaningful exchange going while respecting the listener and the limits of their own knowledge.

The thousand hours are a study allocation, not a promise that every learner reaches the same level on the same date. The B2 target here concerns listening and conversation. Because you chose Roman script, this course does not claim B2 literacy in either Urdu or Hindi script. The final tasks provide evidence of spoken abilities and show what still needs practice; they are not an official qualification.

## What independence looks like

At this stage, a familiar topic should no longer require a full written script. You should be able to explain an experience, give reasons for a view, compare options, follow a sustained exchange, and repair a misunderstanding. Some searching, a noticeable accent, and grammatical mistakes may remain. The practical question is whether they repeatedly stop communication or whether you can recover and continue.

Independence also includes social judgement. An elder may prefer *aap*, a cousin may prefer *tum*, and a particular family may use local forms in some situations but shared forms in others. You should be able to adapt rather than announce that the course's example is the only correct way. Ask **Aap log ghar mein yeh kaise kehte hain?**—how do you say this at home?—and listen to the answer as language evidence.

The core thousand-word vocabulary is a foundation. B2 conversation requires flexible use of those words, recognition of many more words and combinations, and additional vocabulary tied to your life. You may need special terms for your work, family recipes, faith, hobbies, or a relative's memories. Build that personal layer during this chapter instead of treating a fixed word count as the finish line.

## Putting grammar into a speaking decision

Before a difficult sentence, identify four things: who is involved, when the event happens, how the action unfolds, and what you want the listener to do with the information. Suppose you want to explain a missed family visit. The people are you and Khala. The time is yesterday. The missed action is coming. The conversational purpose is an apology and a new arrangement.

Build it in stages. **Main kal nahin aa saka**, male speaker: I could not come yesterday. Female: *aa saki*. Add the reason: **Kyunki kaam der se khatam hua**, because work finished late. Add responsibility: **Mujhe pehle bata dena chahiye tha**, I should have told you earlier. Add repair: **Agar aap free hon to main itwaar ko aa jaaoon?** If you are free, shall I come on Sunday? You have used past ability, a reason, past obligation, and a respectful proposal to accomplish one social purpose.

Do not begin by asking which advanced tense you can display. Begin with the meaning. Short connected clauses can convey a precise argument more effectively than one long sentence with unstable agreement. Once the listener understands the structure, add detail. If you lose track, repair with **Main doosre tareeqe se kehta/kehti hoon**, let me put it another way.

## Inference and certainty

**Woh ghar par honge** can mean they will be at home or they are probably at home, depending on context. A future-looking form can support an inference. Anchor the meaning when it matters: **Ab tak woh ghar pahunch gaye honge**, by now they have probably reached home, with a respectful male or plural reference. For a woman respectfully, **pahunch gayi hongi** is a useful form. These are reasoned assumptions, not confirmed facts.

Compare **Woh aa gaye hain**, they have arrived; **Shaayad woh aa gaye hain**, perhaps they have arrived; and **Woh aa gaye honge**, they have probably arrived/will have arrived. The confidence differs. If you have a message, attribute it: **Unhone phone par kaha ki woh pahunch gaye hain**—they said on the phone that they have arrived. The listener can now distinguish evidence from your guess.

For reported information, **Mujhe bataya gaya ki waqt badal gaya hai** means I was told the time has changed. If you did not confirm it yourself, add **Lekin maine abhi tasdeeq nahin ki**, but I have not confirmed it yet. This is especially useful when several relatives are relaying a plan. Fluency should make uncertainty clearer, not hide it behind confident delivery.

## Making a point without overclaiming

Use **Aam taur par** for generally, **aksar** for often, **kabhi kabhi** for sometimes, and **har baar nahin** for not every time. **Hamaare ghar mein aksar aisa hota hai** means this often happens in our home. It is a more responsible claim than **Sab Hyderabad mein aisa karte hain**, everyone in Hyderabad does this, unless you truly have evidence for such a broad statement.

To qualify an argument, **Jahaan tak main jaanta/jaanti hoon** means as far as I know. **Mere tajurbe mein** means in my experience. **Iska yeh matlab nahin ki ...** means this does not mean that .... For example, **Mujhe Roman mein padhna aasaan lagta hai. Iska yeh matlab nahin ki doosra tareeqa bekaar hai**—I find reading in Roman script easy. That does not mean another method is useless. The sentence states your preference without dismissing someone else's.

Use these phrases where the distinction matters. Repeating “as far as I know” before every ordinary fact can make you sound hesitant. A good extended answer has a clear main point, supporting reasons, an example, and an acknowledgement of limits. The listener should be able to restate your view in one sentence.

## Capstone scene one: a family history conversation

Read the whole scene once for meaning. Then listen to it, if a willing speaker can record it, without looking. The aim is following a story and asking questions that grow from the answer. The speaker's childhood is fictional, so you can replace details without claiming they describe your own family.

**Learner:** Dadi, jab aap chhoti thin to aapka mohalla kaisa tha? — Grandmother, what was your neighbourhood like when you were little?

**Dadi:** Ghar chhote the, lekin log ek doosre ko achchhi tarah jaante the. Shaam ko sab baahar baithte the. — The houses were small, but people knew one another well. Everyone used to sit outside in the evening.

**Learner:** Kya bachche bhi wahin khelte the? — Did the children play there too?

**Dadi:** Hau. Hum gali mein khelte rehte the. Kisi ke ghar se aawaaz aati to khaane ke liye chale jaate the. — Yes. We would keep playing in the lane. When a call came from someone's house, we would go for food.

**Learner:** Aapko sabse zyaada kis cheez ki yaad aati hai? — What do you miss most?

**Dadi:** Logon ke paas waqt tha. Ab sab masroof hain. Lekin ab suhulat bhi zyaada hai. — People had time. Now everyone is busy. But there are also more conveniences now.

**Learner:** To aapke khayaal mein pehle rishton ke liye waqt zyaada tha, lekin ab rozmarra ke kaam aasaan hain? — So in your view there was more time for relationships before, but everyday tasks are easier now?

**Dadi:** Hau, kuchh aisa hi. Har cheez pehle behtar nahin thi. — Yes, something like that. Not everything was better before.

**Learner:** Koi aisa din yaad hai jab poora mohalla saath tha? — Do you remember a day when the whole neighbourhood was together?

**Dadi:** Ek baar bahut baarish hui thi. Padosiyon ne ek doosre ki madad ki. Us din samajh aaya ki padosi kitne zaroori hote hain. — Once there was heavy rain. The neighbours helped one another. That day we understood how important neighbours are.

Notice the sequence: an open question, a relevant follow-up, a question about personal significance, a fair summary, and a request for a concrete example. The learner does not assume that every memory proves the past was better. For transfer, ask a relative about school, a journey, or a family custom. Do not require them to revisit distressing memories. Offer **Agar aap bataana chaahen**, if you wish to tell me.

Your performance task is to conduct a ten-minute interview and then give a two-minute summary. Preserve the difference between a repeated childhood habit and one particular event. Ask the speaker whether your summary represents their meaning. With permission, save one short excerpt for listening practice; otherwise take notes after the conversation.

## Capstone scene two: organising a gathering

**Farah:** Is baar sabko ghar bulaayen? — Shall we invite everyone home this time?

**Aamir:** Khayaal achchha hai, lekin jagah ka masla hoga. — It is a good idea, but space will be a problem.

**Learner:** Kitne log aa sakte hain? Pehle yeh dekh lete hain. — How many people might come? Let's check that first.

**Farah:** Taqreeban bees. Agar bachche bhi aaye to pachchees. — About twenty. If the children come too, twenty-five.

**Aamir:** Itne logon ke liye kursiyaan kahaan se aayengi? — Where will chairs come from for so many people?

**Learner:** Kursiyaan kiraaye par mil sakti hain. Lekin khaana kaun banaayega? — Chairs can be rented. But who will make the food?

**Farah:** Kuchh ghar mein bana lenge, kuchh baahar se mangwa lenge. — We will make some at home and order some from outside.

**Aamir:** Mujhe lagta hai ki saara kaam aap par aa jaayega. — I think all the work will fall on you.

**Learner:** Yeh baat theek hai. Kaam baant dete hain. Main logon se tasdeeq kar leta hoon, Aamir kursiyon ka intezaam karen, aur khaane ka faisla hum milkar karen. — That is a fair point. Let's divide the work. I'll confirm with people, Aamir can arrange the chairs, and we can decide the food together.

**Farah:** Hau, lekin pehle kharch ka andaaza laga len. — Yes, but first let's estimate the expense.

**Learner:** To abhi teen kaam hain: logon ki tadaad, kursiyaan, aur kharch. Hum kal shaam tak dobara baat karen? — So there are three tasks for now: number of people, chairs, and expense. Shall we speak again by tomorrow evening?

The learner does not solve every problem alone. They structure the decision, share tasks, and identify what remains open. A strong B2-oriented task includes this coordination. For transfer, your partner changes one constraint: the budget is smaller, an elder cannot climb stairs, or half the guests can only come later. Respond to the new information without restarting a memorised plan.

Prepare a spoken summary containing confirmed decisions, assigned tasks, and unresolved questions. Ask another person to act only from your summary. If they cannot identify their task or deadline, revise the message. Grammar matters because it supports responsibility and time, not because a long sentence receives extra credit.

## Capstone scene three: a disagreement with common ground

**Cousin:** Aajkal sab phone par rehte hain. Koi baat hi nahin karta. — These days everyone stays on their phone. Nobody talks.

**Learner:** Main samajh raha hoon ki aapko saath baithkar baat karna yaad aata hai. — I understand that you miss sitting together and talking. Male speaker.

**Cousin:** Hau. Khaane ke waqt bhi phone! — Yes. Phones even at mealtimes!

**Learner:** Khaane ke waqt phone alag rakhna achchha khayaal hai. Lekin phone ki wajah se hum door rehkar bhi baat kar paate hain. — Putting phones aside at meals is a good idea. But because of phones we can talk even while living far apart.

**Cousin:** Woh bhi theek hai, magar hadd honi chahiye. — That is true too, but there should be a limit.

**Learner:** To shaayad masla phone nahin, uska istemaal hai. Agar hum roz kuchh waqt sirf ghar waalon ke liye rakhen to kaisa rahega? — So perhaps the problem is not the phone but its use. What if we set aside some time every day just for the family?

**Cousin:** Kitna waqt? — How much time?

**Learner:** Pehle bees minute. Agar kaam na kare to tareeqa badal sakte hain. — Twenty minutes to begin with. If it does not work, we can change the approach.

**Cousin:** Theek hai. Lekin aap bhi phone door rakhenge! — All right. But you will put your phone away too!

**Learner:** Zaroor. Qaaida sabke liye ek hi hona chahiye. — Certainly. The rule should be the same for everyone.

The practice skill is qualifying an absolute claim and finding a workable proposal. You do not have to agree with the example's conclusion. Argue for a different approach using two reasons and a concession. Your partner should interrupt once with a fair objection. Respond to the objection rather than repeating your original paragraph more loudly.

## Capstone scene four: helping after a misunderstanding

**Khala:** Maine samjha tha ki aap log kal aa rahe hain. Main intezaar karti rahi. — I thought you were coming yesterday. I kept waiting.

**Learner:** Maaf kijiye. Hamein jumme ka waqt bataya gaya tha. Lagta hai paighaam mein galatfehmi ho gayi. — I am sorry. We were told Friday's time. It seems there was a misunderstanding in the message.

**Khala:** Kisi ne mujhe badla hua waqt nahin bataya. — Nobody told me the changed time.

**Learner:** Hamein aapko pehle bata dena chahiye tha. Main abhi sab se tasdeeq kar leta hoon. — We should have told you earlier. I'll confirm with everyone now.

**Khala:** Ab jaldi kaiku kar rahe ho? Pehle saaf batao ki aaoge kab. — Why are you rushing now? First tell me clearly when you will come.

**Learner:** Aap theek keh rahi hain. Hum jumme ko chaar baje aayenge. Kya woh waqt aapke liye theek hai? — You are right. We will come Friday at four. Is that time suitable for you?

**Khala:** Hau, woh theek hai. — Yes, that is fine.

**Learner:** Main yahi waqt sabko likhkar bhej deta hoon. Aur nikalne se pehle aapko phone karoonga. — I'll send everyone this same time in writing. And I'll call you before leaving. Male speaker.

**Khala:** Theek hai. Bas ab galatfehmi nakko ho. — All right. Just let there be no more misunderstanding now.

This exchange does not require the learner to prove who caused the mistake before repairing it. They acknowledge the effect, clarify the agreed time, and give a next step. For transfer, remove the script and have your partner change both the day and one person's understanding. Reconcile the information accurately before closing.

## The final listening assessment

Ask a proficient speaker to prepare three unfamiliar recordings: a two-minute everyday message, a three-minute personal story, and a four-minute discussion with two viewpoints. They should use natural, clear speech, including household forms when appropriate. The course's printed scenes are rehearsal material; the final recordings should change details and wording. If only synthetic speech is available, record that limitation and seek human listening evidence later.

Listen to each recording once for the main purpose. Give a short English or Roman Urdu summary without seeing a transcript. On the second pass, note people, times, reasons, changes, and uncertainty. Write a Roman transcription of a selected thirty-second section and translate it into English. Then compare with the speaker's intended meaning. Score meaning rather than one rigid Roman spelling.

Use twenty content points across the three recordings, chosen before the attempt. Award one point for each correct point, half for an incomplete but nonmisleading point, and zero for a wrong or absent point. Mark critical details such as a changed day separately. Aim for at least sixteen points and no uncorrected critical misunderstanding after one opportunity to ask a clarification. This is a local course threshold, not a validated CEFR cut score.

## The final speaking assessment

Complete four tasks with a partner over two sessions. First, hold a ten-minute personal conversation with follow-up questions. Second, describe a past event for three minutes and answer questions for five. Third, negotiate a practical decision for twelve minutes with one surprise constraint. Fourth, discuss a familiar general issue for ten minutes, giving a view, reasons, an example, and a fair response to an objection.

Do not read or memorise a full response. You may use five keywords per task. The partner should let you seek clarification naturally and should not supply every missing word. Record with consent. If consent is absent, take brief notes after the task. Evaluate performance on two occasions with different topics, because a single familiar script can conceal a major transfer gap.

Rate five dimensions from 0 to 4:

| Dimension | What a 3 looks like |
| --- | --- |
| Range | Enough words and structures to explain, compare, narrate, and give reasons; paraphrase fills many gaps. |
| Control | Errors occur but most intended relationships remain clear; several recurring errors can be corrected. |
| Fluency and coherence | Connected speech with manageable pauses; ideas follow a recognisable order. |
| Interaction and listening | Responds to actual turns, asks targeted clarification, takes and returns the floor. |
| Appropriateness and intelligibility | Generally understandable pronunciation and suitable respect/register; local forms fit the relationship. |

A 0 means the task could not be attempted meaningfully; 1 means heavy support; 2 means partial independence with frequent breakdown; 3 means mostly independent performance; 4 means confident, flexible performance for this task. Seek at least 15/20, no dimension below 2, and no critical breakdown left unresolved on both occasions. A qualified teacher can provide stronger external judgement. These numbers guide practice and do not confer certification.

## Grammar and translation audit

Translate these integrated prompts, then compare the models and explain the choices.

1. I should have told you earlier, but I thought you already knew.
2. If everyone agrees, we can meet at five instead of four.
3. The time has not been confirmed yet.
4. I had the clothes washed because I was busy.
5. As far as I know, they have probably arrived by now.
6. Although I understand your point, I prefer the other method.
7. Let me explain what happened before you decide.
8. The people who are coming tomorrow should be told the new time.

**Models.** (1) **Mujhe aapko pehle bata dena chahiye tha, lekin mujhe laga ki aapko pehle se maaloom hai.** Past responsibility is separate from the earlier assumption. (2) **Agar sab raazi hon to hum chaar ke bajaaye paanch baje mil sakte hain.** The subjunctive leaves agreement open. (3) **Waqt ki abhi tasdeeq nahin hui.** *Tasdeeq* is feminine. (4) **Maine kapre dhulwaaye kyunki main masroof tha/thi.** The causative says someone else washed them. (5) **Jahaan tak mujhe maaloom hai, woh ab tak pahunch gaye honge.** Match the forms to the referent; for a woman respectfully, *pahunch gayi hongi*. (6) **Halaanki aapki baat samajh mein aa rahi hai, mujhe doosra tareeqa zyaada pasand hai.** Understanding does not entail agreement. (7) **Aap faisla karne se pehle mujhe bataane dijiye ki kya hua.** *Bataane dijiye* permits explaining. (8) **Jo log kal aa rahe hain, unko naya waqt bata dena chahiye.** The relative-correlative connection identifies the affected people.

Translate back into English without looking at the original prompts. If a phrase changes meaning, identify whether the problem concerns time, agency, agreement, certainty, or social purpose. That diagnosis tells you what to revisit. Correcting every sentence by memorising one model does not reveal the underlying pattern.

## Building a personal continuation vocabulary

Choose five domains in your actual life. For each, collect twenty useful words or phrases from real conversations, giving you one hundred personal additions. Each entry should contain a Roman form, meaning, one complete sentence, who used it, and a note about register or context. Ask for a recording only with consent. The aim is usable knowledge, not a larger spreadsheet.

Practise each new item in three different turns: a statement, a question, and a response to someone else's view. For example, a new word about a hobby should help you explain what you enjoy, ask your cousin about it, and respond when they disagree. If a specialised term remains hard to retrieve, practise a simpler explanation using familiar words. Circumlocution is a core conversation skill.

Keep recognising more than you actively produce. You may understand a very local phrase after one family explanation but choose a shared form until you hear its social setting several times. That is a sensible learning choice. The long-term goal is belonging in real exchanges, not displaying the maximum number of local markers in one sentence.

## An eight-week continuation cycle

After the course, aim for a sustainable weekly rhythm: two real conversations, three short listening sessions, one focused repair session, and a little vocabulary retrieval most days. Choose one changing theme each fortnight. One fortnight might centre on childhood memories; another on food preparation; another on work and study; another on discussing a film or a general issue. The theme gives vocabulary a purpose while grammar keeps recurring.

Save a two-minute recording every two weeks using a new prompt. Compare it with an earlier recording for clarity, response to questions, and the number of successful repairs. Do not judge progress only by accent. A more intelligible sentence, a fairer summary, or a well-handled disagreement is real improvement. Ask a trusted speaker for one actionable observation instead of a general verdict about whether you “sound native.”

If you want external validation, seek a qualified teacher or assessor who understands your spoken goals and your Roman-only constraint. Explain that your target is conversation and listening, not script literacy. Continue learning from your family while respecting their time and preferences. The course has given you a structure; ongoing relationships provide the changing language that no fixed book can exhaust.
