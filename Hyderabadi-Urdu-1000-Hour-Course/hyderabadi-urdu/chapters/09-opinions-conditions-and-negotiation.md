# 09. Opinions, conditions, and negotiation

**Time: 90 hours. Course total after this chapter: 725 hours. Target: developing B2 spoken interaction.**

You can tell your cousin what happened yesterday. Now the family wants to choose between visiting someone, staying home, and going out. Nobody has the same schedule. Conversation becomes interesting when you can say what would work, explain why, acknowledge another person's reason, and change your proposal. This chapter turns familiar grammar into those social actions.

The goal is not winning arguments. It is making your meaning clear while preserving the relationship. Begin with the shared conversational forms. Add the Hyderabadi choices your relatives actually use. A respectful sentence containing ordinary Urdu/Hindi can sound entirely at home in Hyderabad; a string of local catchphrases does not by itself make a conversation natural.

## From an observation to an opinion

Consider **Yeh jagah door hai**: this place is far away. It presents distance as a description. If you say **Mere khayaal mein yeh jagah door hai**, you frame the same description as your view: in my opinion, this place is far away. The added phrase gives other people room to disagree. **Mujhe lagta hai ki yeh jagah door hai** means I think this place is far away. Here *mujhe lagta hai* is a useful fixed frame, literally something like “it seems to me.” It does not mean that you physically stick to something.

Build a fuller view in steps:

1. **Yeh jagah achchhi hai.** This place is good.
2. **Mujhe lagta hai ki yeh jagah achchhi hai.** I think this place is good.
3. **Mujhe lagta hai ki yeh jagah achchhi hai kyunki yahaan bachchon ke liye jagah hai.** I think this place is good because there is space for the children here.
4. **Lekin agar baarish ho to mushkil hogi.** But if it rains, there will be a problem.

The third step supplies a reason that your listener can evaluate. The fourth recognises a limitation. You now sound more engaged because you are responding to a real choice, rather than reciting a preference. A useful first practice is to give one opinion, one reason, and one limitation about somewhere familiar.

**Mere hisaab se** means according to my assessment or as I see it. **Meri raay mein** also introduces an opinion and can sound more deliberate. **Mujhe lagta hai** is a good everyday default. Do not introduce every sentence with an opinion marker. Once the viewpoint is clear, continue with the reason and example. Too many disclaimers make the main point difficult to hear.

When you are unsure, adjust certainty explicitly. **Shaayad woh masroof hain** means perhaps they are busy. **Ho sakta hai ki woh masroof hon** means it may be that they are busy. In the second sentence, *hon* is the respectful/plural subjunctive of *hona*. The uncertainty is in the situation, not in whether you respect the person.

## What an if-sentence does

A condition connects two situations: the situation you are considering and the consequence that follows. In **Agar waqt hai to chai pi lete hain**, “if there is time, let's have some tea,” *agar waqt hai* supplies the condition and *to chai pi lete hain* supplies the proposal. English often drops “then”; Urdu/Hindi often keeps *to*. Listen for the two halves rather than translating each word as it arrives.

For an open possibility, begin with a condition that could genuinely hold:

| Condition | Result | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Agar aap free hain | to hum abhi baat kar sakte hain | If you are free, we can talk now. |
| Agar kal baarish hui | to hum ghar par rahenge | If it rains tomorrow, we will stay home. |
| Agar aap chaahen | to hum jagah badal sakte hain | If you wish, we can change the place. |
| Agar der ho rahi hai | to pehle phone kar dijiye | If it is getting late, please call first. |

The different verb forms are meaningful. *Hain* asks about a present state. *Hui* presents the rain as an event that would have occurred by the relevant future decision point; a perfective form does not force the whole conversation into the past. *Chaahen* leaves a respectful person's preference open. *Ho rahi hai* describes a developing situation. There is no requirement that every *agar* clause use exactly one tense.

Work through the second row carefully. Start with **Kal baarish hogi**, it will rain tomorrow. You do not actually know that it will. Change the assertion into an open condition: **Agar kal baarish hui**. Then say what you will do: **hum ghar par rahenge**. Join them with *to*. Your future commitment is staying home under the stated condition; you have not predicted certain rain.

In listening, distinguish *agar* from *magar*: if versus but. Their sound is similar enough to matter in a fast conversation. Put both into short recordings: **Agar waqt hai to aaiye** and **Waqt hai, magar main thaka hua hoon**. The first invites someone conditionally; the second explains an obstacle despite having time.

## Imagining a different situation

Suppose your cousin asks what you would do if you lived closer. **Agar main paas rehta to roz milta** means if I lived nearby, I would meet you every day, with a male speaker. A female speaker says **Agar main paas rehti to roz milti**. The habitual-looking forms can describe an imagined alternative when the context makes that reading clear. They are not statements about your current daily routine.

Now use *hona* to imagine possession. **Agar mere paas gaari hoti to main aapko chhor deta** means if I had a car, I would drop you off, with a male speaker. *Gaari* is feminine, so the condition contains *hoti*. The result *chhor deta* reflects the male subject. A female speaker says *chhor deti*. The two agreement decisions belong to different clauses. Do not copy the final vowel from one clause into the other.

**Agar main aapki jagah hota to pehle phone karta** means if I were in your place, I would call first, said by a male speaker. A female speaker uses *hoti* and *karti*. This is an advice frame, but it can still sound intrusive. Ask **Kya main ek mashwara doon?**—may I offer a suggestion?—before giving advice about a sensitive family decision.

For an unrealised past, anchor the time clearly: **Agar aapne kal bataya hota to main aa jaata**: if you had told me yesterday, I would have come, with a male speaker. A female speaker says *aa jaati*. First identify the action that did not happen, *aapne bataya hota*. Then identify its lost consequence, *main aa jaata*. The sentence often implies that the person did not tell you. That implication can sound blaming even when the grammar is correct.

Compare a gentler practical version: **Kal pata chal jaata to achchha hota. Ab hum kya kar sakte hain?** It would have been good to find out yesterday. What can we do now? This does not erase disappointment, but it moves the conversation toward a solution. Practice both the grammatical form and its interpersonal effect.

## Wishes, proposals, and respectful uncertainty

You met subjunctive forms with plans and wishes in chapter six. Here they help people make a decision together. **Hum kahaan milen?** means where should we meet? **Kya main pehle unse baat karoon?** means shall I speak to them first? **Aap bataayen** means please tell me, in a respectful invitation to contribute. The form presents an action for consideration instead of announcing that it definitely happens.

Regular forms are easiest to hear as small families: *main karoon*, *hum karen*, *aap karen*, *tum karo*, *woh kare*. High-frequency irregular forms deserve practice as phrases: *main jaaoon*, *aap jaayen*, *main loon*, *aap len*, *main doon*, *aap den*, *main hoon* in the subjunctive context and *aap hon*. Roman spellings are a reading aid; let a trusted speaker model nasal vowels and syllable boundaries.

**Aap chaahen to hum jaldi nikal sakte hain** means if you wish, we can leave early. **Main chaahta hoon ki sab aaraam se baithen** means I want everyone to sit comfortably, with a male speaker. **Main chaahti hoon** is the female-speaker version. The first *chaahen* agrees with respectful *aap*; the second clause *baithen* refers to *sab*. In a longer sentence, identify who performs each action before choosing its ending.

**Kaash hum paas rehte** means I wish we lived nearby. It expresses a wish, not a logistical proposal. A listener may respond with sympathy rather than a plan. By contrast, **Kyun na hum agle hafte milen?**—why don't we meet next week?—invites a concrete choice. Use a wish when you want to share a feeling and a proposal when you want to agree on action.

## Comparing without losing the point

Comparison provides reasons for choosing. **Yeh raasta us raaste se chhota hai** means this route is shorter than that route. *Se* marks the standard of comparison. The adjective is an ordinary adjective; Urdu/Hindi does not add an English-style “-er.” Before *se*, masculine *raasta* becomes oblique *raaste*. The adjective *chhota* agrees with the subject route, not the word after *se*.

To compare cost, **Yeh jagah us jagah se sasti hai**: this place is cheaper than that place. *Jagah* is feminine, so *sasti* is feminine. For relative preference, **Mujhe pehla tareeqa zyaada munasib lagta hai** means the first method seems more suitable to me. *Munasib* does not change its ending. **Sabse qareeb** means nearest; **utna door nahin** means not that far; **dono taqreeban baraabar hain** means both are approximately equal.

State the dimension you actually mean. “Better” may mean cheaper, quieter, more accessible, or more comfortable for an older relative. **Yahaan shor kam hai, isliye baat karna aasaan hoga**—there is less noise here, so conversation will be easier—is more useful than insisting **Yahi behtar hai**, this is the better one. Reasons make negotiation possible because people can change a proposal while keeping the shared goal.

Equality uses *jitna ... utna* patterns already related to your relative-correlative work: **Jitna waqt aapko chahiye, utna le lijiye** means take as much time as you need. **Yeh utna mushkil nahin hai jitna lagta hai** means this is not as difficult as it seems. Begin with one familiar pattern and avoid building several nested comparisons before the listener knows the topic.

## Disagreement that keeps a conversation open

Disagreeing respectfully has three parts: show what you understood, identify the point you see differently, and offer a reason or alternative. **Aapki baat samajh mein aa rahi hai, lekin mujhe lagta hai ki thoda aur waqt chahiye** means I understand your point, but I think we need a little more time. Agreement with one reason does not commit you to the whole conclusion.

Useful frames include **Is baat se main sehmat hoon** (I agree with this point), **Main poori tarah sehmat nahin hoon** (I do not completely agree), **Mera matlab yeh nahin tha** (that was not what I meant), and **Ho sakta hai main galat samajh raha hoon** (perhaps I am misunderstanding, male speaker; *rahi hoon* for female). Say these at a measured pace. A soft phrase spoken with sarcasm is not automatically polite.

For concession, begin **Halaanki**: **Halaanki jagah door hai, phir bhi jaana achchha rahega** means although the place is far away, going would still be good. **Phir bhi** preserves the second point despite the first. **Lekin** introduces contrast. **Isliye** introduces a consequence. **Kyunki** introduces a reason. If a paragraph becomes difficult, reduce it to two clauses and choose the connector whose job matches your meaning.

Local **Kaiku?** asks why, but a bare “why?” can sound challenging in any dialect. When a relative's preference surprises you, try **Achchha, aap aisa kaiku keh rahe hain?**—oh, why do you say that?—if that wording fits your household. Shared **Aap aisa kyun keh rahe hain?** is equally useful. The respectful verb ending matters more than inserting a local word.

## Dialogue: choosing a family outing

**Sara:** Mere khayaal mein hum subah niklen to behtar hoga. — In my opinion, it would be better if we left in the morning.

**Imran:** Subah kaiku? Bachche der se uthte hain. — Why in the morning? The children get up late.

**Sara:** Hau, woh baat bhi theek hai. Lekin dopahar mein garmi zyaada hogi. — Yes, that is a fair point too. But it will be hotter in the afternoon.

**Imran:** Agar hum das baje niklen to kaisa rahega? — How would it be if we left at ten?

**Sara:** Theek rahega, basharte ki sab waqt par taiyaar hon. — That will work, provided everyone is ready on time.

**Imran:** Main sabko aaj hi bata deta hoon. — I'll tell everyone today itself.

*Basharte ki* means provided that; it is useful but more deliberate than ordinary *agar*. Sara accepts Imran's reason before returning to the heat. They change the time rather than arguing about who is right. Replay the dialogue and notice how the question intonation invites cooperation. In your own version, substitute rain, a working relative, or a long drive as the constraint.

## Dialogue: an older relative offers advice

**Khala:** Agar tum thoda jaldi so jaao to subah aasaani hogi. — If you go to sleep a little earlier, the morning will be easier.

**Nida:** Aap theek keh rahi hain. Masla yeh hai ki mera kaam der se khatam hota hai. — You are right. The problem is that my work finishes late.

**Khala:** To kya kaam ka waqt badal sakta hai? — So can the work schedule change?

**Nida:** Filhaal nahin. Lekin agar main pehle se khaana taiyaar kar loon to waqt bach sakta hai. — Not at present. But if I prepare food in advance, time could be saved.

**Khala:** Hau, yeh achchha khayaal hai. — Yes, that is a good idea.

Nida does not have to accept an impossible solution to show respect. She acknowledges the intention, explains the constraint, and proposes another way. In *kar loon*, *lena* suggests doing the preparation for her own practical benefit. Chapter ten develops these small shades of compound verbs.

## Dialogue: disagreeing about a general topic

**Aamir:** Mere hisaab se bachchon ko ghar mein sirf Urdu bolni chahiye. — As I see it, children should speak only Urdu at home.

**Farah:** Main samajh rahi hoon ki aap zubaan bachana chaahte hain. Main bhi yahi chaahti hoon. — I understand that you want to preserve the language. I want the same thing.

**Aamir:** Phir aap kis baat se sehmat nahin hain? — Then which point do you disagree with?

**Farah:** “Sirf” waali baat se. Agar unko sawaal poochhne ki azaadi ho to woh zyaada bolenge. — With the “only” part. If they have freedom to ask questions, they will speak more.

**Aamir:** Aapka matlab hai ki English bhi chal sakti hai? — You mean English can be acceptable too?

**Farah:** Zaroorat par, hau. Phir hum wohi baat Urdu mein keh sakte hain. — When necessary, yes. Then we can say the same thing in Urdu.

This is an original practice discussion, not a universal claim about child language policy. Its language skill is separating a shared goal from a disputed method. Retell both positions fairly before stating your own. That is harder and more useful than memorising one side's speech.

## Written workshop with explained answers

Translate before opening your notes. Accept a natural equivalent if it preserves time, person, politeness, and the relationship between the two clauses.

1. If you have time, please call me.
2. If it rains tomorrow, we will stay home.
3. If I lived nearby, I would come every day. Give both speaker genders.
4. If you had told me yesterday, I would have helped. Male speaker.
5. I understand your point, but I do not completely agree.
6. Although the place is far, it is quieter.
7. This route is shorter than that route.
8. Shall we meet a little earlier?
9. I wish we lived in the same city.
10. Why do you think that? Address an elder respectfully.

**Answer key and reasoning.** (1) **Agar aapke paas waqt ho to mujhe phone kar dijiye.** *Ho* leaves the availability open; *hai* is also possible for a present fact being checked. (2) **Agar kal baarish hui to hum ghar par rahenge.** The result is future despite the condition's perfective form. (3) **Agar main paas rehta/rehti to roz aata/aati.** Both agreeing forms change with the speaker. (4) **Agar aapne kal bataya hota to main madad karta.** A more explicitly completed result is *main madad kar deta*. (5) **Aapki baat samajh mein aa rahi hai, lekin main poori tarah sehmat nahin hoon.** Understanding is separate from agreeing. (6) **Halaanki jagah door hai, wahaan shor kam hai.** This expresses the intended practical contrast without mechanically translating “quieter.” (7) **Yeh raasta us raaste se chhota hai.** *Us raaste* is oblique before *se*. (8) **Kya hum thoda pehle milen?** A proposal uses the subjunctive. (9) **Kaash hum ek hi shehar mein rehte.** *Hi* focuses “the same/one.” (10) **Aapko aisa kyun lagta hai?** In a household using the local form, *kaiku* can replace *kyun*.

Now repair these sentences: **Agar mere paas gaari hota**; **Yeh jagah us jagah se sasta hai**; **Main aapki baat samajhta hoon isliye main sehmat nahin hoon** when you mean “but”; **Agar aapne bataya to main kal aa jaata** for a missed opportunity yesterday. The repairs are *gaari hoti*, *sasti*, *lekin*, and **Agar aapne kal bataya hota to main aa jaata**. The errors concern agreement, connector meaning, and the unrealised past—not the presence or absence of a fashionable phrase.

## Listening, speaking, and family transfer

For listening, ask a willing relative to record six short decisions. Three should be real possibilities and three imagined alternatives. On the first pass, identify the topic only. On the second, write the condition and result separately in Roman script. On the third, translate the relationship into English. Check whether you heard a prediction, a proposal, advice, or regret. A synthetic voice can help rehearse words but cannot certify local rhythm or intent.

For speaking, record a two-minute comparison of two familiar options. State the shared goal, two advantages, one limitation, a condition, and a proposed compromise. Listen once without reading your script. If you cannot follow the argument, shorten sentences and signpost the turns with *lekin*, *isliye*, and *phir bhi*. Then answer three unprepared follow-up questions from another person.

The family mission is a small real decision: when to call, what to cook, or where to meet. Ask permission to practise for ten minutes. Use a respectful clarification when you miss a point: **Aap keh rahe hain ki ...?**—are you saying that ...? Note one local expression exactly as your relative says it and ask when it sounds natural. Do not turn the conversation into correcting your relative's grammar.

## The 90-hour practice plan

| Work | Hours | Concrete evidence |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Read, annotate, and rebuild worked grammar | 12 | Twenty condition/result pairs with explanations |
| Listening to conditions, reasons, and stance | 16 | Twelve short transcripts and corrected summaries |
| Sentence transformation and translation | 14 | Eighty checked transformations across time and gender |
| Guided discussions and role-play | 18 | Six recordings with a changed constraint each time |
| Vocabulary retrieval and spaced review | 10 | Opinion/negotiation words used in new sentences |
| Family conversations and local-form observation | 14 | Seven short conversations and a usage notebook |
| Exit task, feedback, and targeted repair | 6 | An unseen decision task repeated after feedback |
| **Total** | **90** | **725 cumulative hours** |

An hour means engaged work with breaks outside the count. Replaying a recording without attending to meaning does not automatically become listening practice. Spread this chapter over several weeks and revisit prior grammar during each task. If your partner changes one condition, respond to the change instead of delivering your prepared speech.

## Exit performance

Complete a fifteen-minute conversation about a family decision without reading. Sustain a view with two reasons, represent the other person's view fairly, compare alternatives, use one real condition and one hypothetical condition, and agree on a concrete next step. Ask for clarification when needed. Rate clarity, grammatical control, listening response, turn-taking, and appropriateness from 0 to 4; a 3 means mostly independent, with occasional repair that succeeds.

For readiness, seek at least 3 in clarity, listening response, and appropriateness, and no 0 in any category on two different topics. These are course practice criteria, not an official language certificate. If a single memorised topic succeeds but an unfamiliar one collapses, keep the same structures and vary the situation. Chapter ten adds shades of completion, benefit, emphasis, and responsibility so that your next discussion sounds more precise.
