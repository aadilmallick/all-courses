# 02. Family, belongings, and descriptions

**70 hours · Prerequisite: chapter 1 · Goal: talk about relatives, describe people and objects, and explain whose things they are.**

## Make the conversation about your own people

On a family call, “I am well” lasts only a moment. Someone asks who is in a photograph, whether a cousin is older or younger, whose bag is by the door, or how a grandparent is. These questions require a system for people, properties, and relationships. This chapter builds that system from small descriptions. You will learn patterns that let a few familiar words express many new meanings.

Bring a real photograph that everyone involved is comfortable discussing, or draw a fictional family. Do not upload private family pictures to use the exercises. Label people with the names and titles your own family prefers. The relationships below are common, but address conventions vary. A family may call several older relatives **Khala** or **Chacha** affectionately without using the strictly genealogical relationship. Learn both what the title usually means and whom it actually identifies in your household.

Begin with **Yeh meri behen hai**, “This is my sister.” The sentence has a person being introduced, a relationship description, and a linking form. Now compare **Yeh mera bhai hai**, “This is my brother.” The difference between **mera** and **meri** is not the speaker's gender. It follows the thing or person described as “my.” This one observation unlocks much of the chapter.

## Grammatical gender is a word's behavior

Urdu/Hindi nouns belong to masculine or feminine grammatical classes. A noun is a word naming a person, thing, place, or idea. For people, grammatical gender often corresponds to the person's gender in traditional usage, but for objects it is a grammatical property. **Ghar**, “house,” is masculine; **kursi**, “chair,” is feminine. A chair is not being described as biologically female. The classification predicts which other word forms go with it.

Learn each new noun inside a useful phrase: **mera ghar**, “my house”; **meri kursi**, “my chair.” This gives you the noun and one visible clue to its class. If you memorize only **kursi = chair**, you will later have to retrieve the meaning and guess the agreement. A phrase reduces that guesswork. Add an example that matters to you: **Meri kursi yahaan hai**, “My chair is here.”

Many masculine nouns end in **-a**, such as **ladka**, “boy,” and **kamra**, “room.” Many feminine nouns end in **-i**, such as **ladki**, “girl,” and **kursi**. These are useful tendencies, not universal laws. **Paani**, “water,” is masculine; **chai**, “tea,” is feminine. **Ammi**, “mother,” is feminine; **Abba**, “father,” is masculine. Meaning and learned usage override a spelling shortcut.

When you do not know a noun's gender, keep talking. A wrong ending is usually less disruptive than abandoning the message. Ask later **Yeh lafz kaise istemaal hota hai?**, “How is this word used?” or ask a partner to choose between **mera** and **meri** in a complete phrase. Record one corrected example. Do not turn every family conversation into a grammar interrogation.

## Agreement is a matching signal

An adjective describes a noun. **Achchha**, “good,” **bada**, “big/older,” and **chhota**, “small/younger,” change form in familiar patterns. Start with **achchha kamra**, “a good room,” and **achchhi chai**, “good tea.” The adjective changes because the noun's grammatical class changes. This matching is called agreement.

| Situation | Masculine singular | Feminine singular or plural | Masculine plural |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| good | achchha | achchhi | achchhe |
| big/older | bada | badi | bade |
| small/younger | chhota | chhoti | chhote |
| new | naya | nayi | naye |
| old | purana | purani | purane |

The table gives ordinary direct forms, used before adding the location and relationship markers studied next chapter. It is not the entire declension system. Work with one contrast first: **Mera bhai chhota hai**, “My brother is younger,” and **Meri behen chhoti hai**, “My sister is younger.” Both the possessive and adjective follow the noun. The linking form remains **hai** because each sentence describes one person with ordinary singular agreement.

Not every adjective changes. **Saaf**, “clean,” **khush**, “happy,” **pareshaan**, “worried,” **khoobsurat**, “beautiful,” and **laal**, “red,” remain visibly the same in these examples: **saaf kamra**, **saaf kursi**; **khush bhai**, **khush behen**. Do not invent a feminine ending for a word just because a different adjective has one. Learn which adjectives change, then reuse their forms.

An adjective can appear before its noun or as the main description after it: **naya ghar**, “a new house,” and **Ghar naya hai**, “The house is new.” The ending still follows the noun. English does not usually change “new” in either position; Urdu/Hindi does. Say the two versions in sequence so that word order and agreement become connected rather than separate memorized facts.

## Plurals: hear the whole phrase

Number means whether a phrase refers to one or more than one. A useful first family is **ladka → ladke**, “boy → boys,” and **kamra → kamre**, “room → rooms.” Pair the plural noun with **hain**: **Yeh kamre bade hain**, “These rooms are big.” The adjective becomes **bade**, too. That gives three signals of the plural relationship: noun, adjective, and linking form.

For many feminine nouns ending in **-i**, a direct plural uses **-iyaan**: **ladki → ladkiyaan**, **kursi → kursiyaan**. For many other feminine nouns, learn the whole plural: **behen → behnen**, “sister → sisters”; **kitaab → kitaaben**, “book → books.” The final vowel in these plural endings is nasalized. Roman spellings vary; focus on recognizing and producing the family of sounds.

Some masculine nouns do not change visibly in the direct plural: **ghar**, “house/houses,” **bhai**, “brother/brothers,” **dost**, “friend/friends.” Other words tell you the number: **Mere do bhai hain**, “I have two brothers,” literally “My two brothers are.” **Yeh mere dost hain** may introduce several friends or respectfully introduce one male friend. Context and respect can share the same grammatical form.

Avoid making every noun plural with English **-s**. English words inside family speech may take different patterns, including English plurals; that does not make the English ending a rule for Urdu vocabulary. Practice **do kamre**, **teen kursiyaan**, **do behnen**, **teen bhai** as chunks. Add **do**, “two,” and **teen**, “three,” to make the meaning unambiguous.

## Possession: follow what belongs

English “my” stays the same in “my brother,” “my sister,” and “my brothers.” The Urdu forms change: **mera bhai**, **meri behen**, **mere bhai**. Use this decision sequence: identify the possessed noun; identify its gender and number in the current phrase; choose the matching possessive. The owner's gender is not the decision point.

| Owner | With masculine singular | With feminine | With masculine plural |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| my | mera | meri | mere |
| our | hamaara | hamaari | hamaare |
| your, familiar | tumhaara | tumhaari | tumhaare |
| your, respectful | aap ka | aap ki | aap ke |
| his/her/that person's | us ka | us ki | us ke |

Say **Aap ka naam kya hai?**, “What is your name?” **Naam** is masculine singular, so use **ka**. Say **Aap ki behen kahaan hai?**, “Where is your sister?” **Behen** is feminine, so use **ki**. For respectful reference to the sister herself, many speakers use **Aap ki behen kahaan hain?** Both respect and the particular family convention matter; learn the respectful version when talking about an older sister.

With a named owner, place the owner first: **Samira ki kitaab**, “Samira's book”; **Aamir ka phone**, “Aamir's phone”; **Ammi ke dost**, “Mother's friends.” **Ka/ki/ke** relate the owner to the possessed item. A female owner's masculine noun still takes **ka**: **Samira ka ghar**. A male owner's feminine noun still takes **ki**: **Aamir ki kursi**. State the noun's class aloud if the owner's gender keeps distracting you.

Build “My younger sister's name is Aisha” in layers. Start with **behen**, “sister.” Add **meri chhoti**, making **meri chhoti behen**, “my younger sister.” Now that entire phrase is the owner of **naam**. Add **ka naam**: **meri chhoti behen ka naam**. Finish **Meri chhoti behen ka naam Aisha hai**. The **ka** matches **naam**, not **behen**, because the immediate relationship is “sister's name.”

## A family map with useful distinctions

| Relationship | Common term | Practical note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| mother | Ammi | Maa and other family choices also occur. |
| father | Abba | Abu, Papa, and other choices vary by family. |
| elder brother | bade bhai / Bhai jaan | Address may differ from description. |
| younger brother | chhota bhai | Chhota may mean younger, not physically smaller. |
| elder sister | badi behen / Aapa | Learn your household's preferred title. |
| younger sister | chhoti behen | Use the person's name if that is normal at home. |
| mother's mother | Nani | Maternal side. |
| mother's father | Nana | Maternal side. |
| father's mother | Dadi | Paternal side. |
| father's father | Dada | Paternal side. |
| mother's sister | Khala | Often a familiar community address too. |
| mother's brother | Mamu / Mama | Family preference matters. |
| father's sister | Phuppi | Roman spelling includes phuphi and phuppo variants. |
| father's younger brother | Chacha | Some families use it more broadly. |
| father's older brother | Taya | Titles such as Bade Abba may be preferred. |

Do not assume every English “aunt” is **Khala**. Urdu family vocabulary often preserves the side of the family that English leaves unspecified. When telling a story, this precision helps listeners place the person. When you genuinely do not know the exact relationship, a name or the English word “auntie” may be more accurate than a guessed kinship term.

Respectful reference often uses plural grammar: **Nani kaisi hain?**, “How is Grandmother?” **Abba ghar par hain**, “Father is at home.” For a male elder, an adjective can also take a respectful plural form: **Dada khush hain** uses an unchanging adjective, while **Dada taiyaar hain** is similarly easy. Save more variable polite descriptions for examples you have heard in your family.

## Questions that open a photograph

**Kaun** means “who”; **kya** means “what”; **kahaan** means “where”; **kaisa/kaisi/kaise** asks “what kind/how.” Ask **Yeh kaun hain?** when respectfully asking about someone in a photograph. Answer **Yeh mere Nana hain**. Here **mere** and **hain** show respectful reference to one grandfather. In **Yeh mera chhota bhai hai**, ordinary singular agreement suits a younger brother.

**Kis ka/kis ki/kis ke** asks whose. **Yeh kis ki kitaab hai?** means “Whose book is this?” The **ki** matches the feminine noun **kitaab**. Answer **Yeh meri kitaab hai**. To ask about a phone, change the matching forms: **Yeh kis ka phone hai? Yeh mera phone hai**. The question word **kis** does not tell you the owner's gender.

Question words usually remain near the information they replace. **Aap ki behen kahaan hain?** corresponds to **Meri behen ghar par hain**. You are replacing the location phrase with **kahaan** rather than moving everything into English order. This is a useful method for inventing questions: make a statement, identify the unknown part, and replace that part with a suitable question word.

### Check reference before counting endings

Compare **Yeh mere Nana hain** with **Yeh mere do dost hain**. Both contain **mere** and **hain**, but the first introduces one respected grandfather while the second introduces two friends. The numeral **do** and the relationship noun supply information that the endings alone cannot. Avoid a mechanical rule that every **hain** must become English “they are.” Decide whom the sentence refers to before choosing the English pronoun.

Now imagine a photograph with two older men. **Yeh mere Nana hain** could identify one man being pointed out. **Yeh dono mere dost hain**, “Both of these people are my friends,” explicitly includes both. **Dono** means both. A gesture may make the reference clear in person, but a phone call may need a name: **Imran mere dost hain**, “Imran is my friend,” respectfully. Clear reference is part of grammar in use; an accurate ending cannot repair an unidentified person.

## Negation, contrast, and gentle correction

Use **nahin** to deny a description or relationship: **Yeh meri behen nahin hai**, “This is not my sister.” Follow with the correction: **Yeh meri dost hai**, “This is my female friend.” **Dost** can refer to a male or female friend; agreement and context can clarify gender. A respectful reference may instead use **hain**. Do not treat a single translation as the only possible social choice.

**Lekin**, “but,” joins a contrast: **Ghar chhota hai, lekin achchha hai**, “The house is small, but it is nice.” **Aur**, “and,” adds information: **Kamra bada aur saaf hai**, “The room is big and clean.” You can communicate a great deal with accurate small clauses joined clearly. Longer is not automatically better.

For gentle correction, begin **Nahin, yeh ... hain/hai**, using a calm tone. In some Hyderabadi family conversations you may hear **nai** as a reduced negative. Recognize it, but retain **nahin** as the course spelling. Do not substitute **nakko** into every identity correction. **Yeh meri behen nakko hai** is not our taught way to say “This is not my sister.” Local vocabulary has grammatical limits.

## Three dialogues with different purposes

### Looking at a family photograph

| Roman Urdu/Hindi | English |
| --- | --- |
| Yeh kaun hain? | Who is this, respectfully? |
| Yeh meri Nani hain. | This is my maternal grandmother. |
| Aur yeh? | And this person? |
| Yeh mere Nana hain. | This is my maternal grandfather. |
| Aap ki Nani kaisi hain? | How is your grandmother? |
| Woh theek hain. | She is well. |
| Aap ka parivaar bada hai? | Is your family large? |
| Haan, hamaara khaandaan bada hai. | Yes, our extended family is large. |

**Parivaar** is widely encountered in Hindi; **khaandaan** is common in Urdu and can emphasize the extended family or lineage. **Ghar waale** is another conversational expression for one's household/family, explained further later. Learn recognition across choices, then prefer the form people around you actually use. No single word is required to authenticate a family identity.

### Returning a misplaced object

| Roman Urdu/Hindi | English |
| --- | --- |
| Yeh kis ka phone hai? | Whose phone is this? |
| Yeh mera phone nahin hai. | This is not my phone. |
| Kya yeh Aamir ka phone hai? | Is this Aamir's phone? |
| Hau, yeh us ka phone hai. | Yes, this is his phone. |
| Aur yeh kitaab? | And this book? |
| Yeh meri kitaab hai. Shukriya. | This is my book. Thank you. |

The local **hau** changes the conversational flavor without replacing the entire grammar. **Us ka** refers to Aamir because the prior turn established him. Outside that context, it could mean “his,” “her,” or “that person's.” If ambiguity matters, repeat the name.

### Introducing siblings

| Roman Urdu/Hindi | English |
| --- | --- |
| Mere do bhai hain. | I have two brothers. |
| Kya aap ki behen bhi hai? | Do you also have a sister? |
| Haan, meri ek chhoti behen hai. | Yes, I have one younger sister. |
| Us ka naam kya hai? | What is her name? |
| Us ka naam Aisha hai. | Her name is Aisha. |
| Woh kaisi hai? | What is she like?/How is she? |
| Woh khush hai, lekin abhi thaki hui hai. | She is happy, but right now she is tired. |

**Thaki hui** is a useful feminine description, “tired.” The masculine equivalent is **thaka hua**. Learn it as a phrase for now; the course later explains participles. **Woh kaisi hai?** can ask about personality or current well-being depending on the conversation. An answer and a follow-up question resolve that ambiguity more naturally than a dictionary definition.

## Practice laboratory: change one relationship at a time

Take **Yeh mera naya kamra hai**, “This is my new room.” First replace **kamra** with **kursi**. Because **kursi** is feminine, change both matching words: **Yeh meri nayi kursi hai**. Next make the chair plural: **Yeh meri nayi kursiyaan hain**. The feminine adjective and possessive do not gain a new visible plural ending here, but the noun and linking form do. Next return to masculine plural: **Yeh mere naye kamre hain**.

This exercise is more useful when you explain the change. “I used **meri** because **kursi** is feminine” is a portable reason. “I copied the answer” is not. When several changes feel overwhelming, keep the noun fixed and change only the owner: **meri kursi**, **aap ki kursi**, **Samira ki kursi**. Then hold the owner fixed and vary the noun. Separate difficulties before combining them.

For listening, ask a partner to read four descriptions with one changed word each. Identify whether the change concerns owner, size, number, or relationship. A learner who hears the endings can often understand before producing them fluently. Listening is not merely waiting for your turn to speak; it is learning what information a speaker signals with small sounds.

## Written exercises and answers

Translate these into Roman Urdu/Hindi: 1. This is my sister. 2. My house is small. 3. Your chair is new, respectful. 4. These are Samira's books. 5. Who is this, respectfully? 6. My younger brother's name is Imran. 7. This is not my phone. 8. Our rooms are clean.

Answers: 1. **Yeh meri behen hai**; respectful **hain** is possible for an older sister. 2. **Mera ghar chhota hai**; **ghar** is masculine. 3. **Aap ki kursi nayi hai**; both matching forms follow **kursi**. 4. **Yeh Samira ki kitaaben hain**; **ki** stays feminine with plural books. 5. **Yeh kaun hain?** 6. **Mere chhote bhai ka naam Imran hai**. The phrase **mere chhote bhai** is the form used before **ka**; chapter 3 explains this oblique environment. 7. **Yeh mera phone nahin hai**. 8. **Hamaare kamre saaf hain**; **saaf** does not change visibly.

Translate into English: 9. **Meri do behnen hain**. 10. **Yeh mere Abba hain**. 11. **Us ki kitaab purani hai**. 12. **Yeh kis ki kursi hai?** 13. **Woh meri dost hai**. 14. **Ghar bada hai, lekin kamra chhota hai**.

Answers: 9. “I have two sisters.” 10. “This is my father,” with respectful grammar. 11. “His/her book is old”; the possessor's gender is unspecified. 12. “Whose chair is this?” 13. “She is my friend”; **woh** can also mean “that person” with context. 14. “The house is big, but the room is small.” Notice that interpreting natural English sometimes requires more than replacing words in their original order.

For a correction task, repair **Mera behen achchha hai**. The ordinary singular answer is **Meri behen achchhi hai**. Two words change because **behen** is feminine. Repair **Samira ki ghar bada hai** as **Samira ka ghar bada hai**. The owner's identity does not determine **ka**. Repair **Yeh kursiyaan nayi hai** as **Yeh kursiyaan nayi hain**. Number affects the linking form.

## A family mission and a 70-hour plan

Create a spoken family map with six people. For each, give a name or preferred title, the relationship, one description, and one respectful question about them. Ask a relative to correct relationships first and grammar second. If a title differs from the table, record their preference without arguing. You are learning your family as well as a language.

| Activity | Hours | Evidence of active work |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Gender and adjective instruction | 12 | Build and explain contrasting phrases with familiar objects. |
| Possessive and plural practice | 12 | Reconstruct descriptions after a delay, then compare with answers. |
| Kinship and personalized vocabulary | 10 | Make a family map; confirm address preferences with consent. |
| Dialogue, shadowing, and dictation | 10 | Alternate unseen listening with recorded spoken responses. |
| Family conversation and photo descriptions | 14 | Short unprepared exchanges using approved photos or drawings. |
| Companion exercises and error repair | 8 | Sort errors into meaning, agreement, number, and register. |
| Review and exit recording | 4 | Repeat the mission with a new photo or family arrangement. |
| **Total** | **70** | Cumulative course time: 130 hours. |

At the exit, describe six people and five belongings without reading a script. Ask whose an item is, distinguish both sides of the family for four relationships, and correct one mistaken identification politely. A partner should understand the intended relationships. Agreement may still require self-correction. Before continuing, revisit any error that changes who owns an object or which relative is meant; those errors matter more immediately than a slightly unnatural adjective.
