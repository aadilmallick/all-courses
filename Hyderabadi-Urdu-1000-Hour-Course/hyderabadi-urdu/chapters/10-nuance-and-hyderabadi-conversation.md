# 10. Nuance and Hyderabadi conversation

**Time: 90 hours. Course total after this chapter: 815 hours. Target: flexible B2-oriented conversation.**

Your aunt says **Chai pi lijiye**. You know *chai*, tea, and *peena*, to drink. Why has *lena*, to take, appeared? Later someone says **Main bhool gaya**, I forgot. Nobody physically went anywhere. These everyday combinations are a major part of natural conversation. Their second verbs organise how the action feels: achieved, completed, undertaken for someone, or beyond the speaker's control.

This chapter teaches those meanings alongside emphasis and responsibility. It also makes an important distinction: common conversational grammar is shared widely, while particular Hyderabadi forms vary across households and situations. You will learn to notice what your family says without treating one family's speech as the only authentic version.

## A second verb changes the viewpoint

Begin with **Main kaam karta hoon**, I work/do work, for a male speaker. Now consider **Main yeh kaam kar leta hoon**, I'll do this task or I can get this done, depending on context. The first verb appears as the bare stem *kar*. The second verb, *lena*, carries the changing ending: *leta*, *leti*, *liya*, *loonga*. Its literal meaning “take” is no longer the main event.

Build the past sentence in stages. The action is **chai banaana**, making tea. The actor is *Nida*. A plain completed report is **Nida ne chai banaai**, Nida made tea. To present the tea-making as something she got done for herself or her side of the situation, say **Nida ne chai bana li**. *Chai* is feminine, and the final perfective *li* reflects that. Do not say *banaai li*: the first verb is a stem in this construction.

The self-benefit meaning is a tendency, not a rule that every sentence must literally benefit its grammatical subject. **Pehle sun lijiye** means please hear me out first. The respectful request invites the listener to complete the listening before deciding. **Main dekh leta hoon** means I'll take a look; **Aap baith jaaiye** means please sit down. English translation changes with the activity and situation.

Do not add a second verb to every action. **Main roz chai peeta hoon** is the clear way to state a habit. A compound may be natural in a particular exchange, but random addition creates strange emphasis. Learn whole combinations through meaning and context: *dekh lena*, *sun lena*, *likh lena*, *kar dena*, *bata dena*, *bhool jaana*, *so jaana*. Then vary person and time.

## Lena and dena in small acts of cooperation

Compare **Yeh number likhiye**, write this number, with **Yeh number likh lijiye**, note this number down. The second often suggests keeping it for your own use. Compare **Darwaaza band kijiye**, close the door, with **Darwaaza band kar dijiye**, please close the door for me/us. *Dena* can present an action as directed outward or as a completed service. It does not guarantee kindness: context and tone still determine how a demand sounds.

**Main aapko pata bhej deta hoon** means I'll send you the address, male speaker. A female speaker says *bhej deti hoon*. Here a present-looking compound often makes an immediate offer. **Maine aapko pata bhej diya** means I sent you the address. The presence of *ne* in this completed transitive sentence remains important. Changing the second verb changes viewpoint, not every other rule of the sentence.

For a feminine object, **Maine tasveer bhej di** means I sent the picture. For a masculine object, **Maine paighaam bhej diya** means I sent the message. When the object has *ko*, the shared-standard pattern normally uses the default masculine singular: **Maine unko dekh liya**, I saw them. You are reusing the agreement logic from chapter five with a longer verb phrase.

Now consider requests as relationships. **Zara bata dijiye** is a courteous request for information. **Bata do** is familiar. **Bata** is intimate or abrupt. A local word does not cancel the difference. With an older relative, retain **aap** and a respectful form unless that person explicitly invites another pattern. In your household notebook, record both the verb and who said it to whom.

## Jaana: completion, change, and lexical combinations

**Bachcha so gaya** means the child fell asleep. **Main thak gaya hoon**, for a male speaker, means I have become tired; a female speaker says **Main thak gayi hoon**. **Woh bhool gaye** can mean he respectfully forgot or they forgot. These combinations present a resulting state or completed transition. They are learned expressions, not commands to translate *gaya* as “went.”

The familiar compound **aa jaana** can mean to come over or arrive, depending on context. **Kal aa jaaiye** is please come tomorrow. **Mujhe samajh aa gaya** means I understood; here *samajh aana* is the main expression and *gaya* presents the understanding as achieved. **Mujhe baat samajh aa gayi** has feminine *baat*, the matter, so the final form changes. Reading only the English pronoun “I” will lead you to the wrong agreement.

Compare three stages: **Main samajh raha hoon**, I am understanding/following, male speaker; **Mujhe samajh aa raha hai**, it is becoming clear to me; **Mujhe samajh aa gaya**, I got it. A listener might use any of them naturally, but the viewpoint differs. Practice with an actual explanation: ask a cousin to explain a recipe or route, then choose the phrase that describes your understanding accurately.

In negative sentences, do not mechanically preserve every affirmative compound. **Maine nahin dekha**, I did not see it, is a useful neutral default. Compounds can occur in negatives when the meaning warrants them, but the choice is idiomatic. Learn **Main bhool nahin gaya**, I have not forgotten, as a meaningful denial rather than inventing a ban on all compounds with *nahin*.

## Beginning, continuing, and already finishing

Aspect describes the internal shape of an action: beginning, continuing, finishing, or happening repeatedly. You already know progressive **kar raha hai**. To say something begins, use an oblique infinitive followed by *lagna*: **Bachcha rone laga**, the child began to cry. *Rona* becomes *rone*. A girl **rone lagi**; children **rone lage**. The final verb agrees with the person beginning the activity.

**Main Urdu samajhne laga hoon** means I have begun to understand Urdu, male speaker; in this basic pattern, the feminine counterpart of *laga* is *lagi*. A female speaker says **Main Urdu samajhne lagi hoon**. Compare **Mujhe Urdu aasaan lagti hai**, Urdu seems easy to me. The word *lagna* has several constructions. Identify its neighbours: infinitive *samajhne* marks beginning, while adjective *aasaan* marks a judgement.

For continuity, **Woh bolta raha** means he kept talking. **Woh bolti rahi** means she kept talking. The habitual participle carries the repeated/continuing activity, and *rehna* carries the persistence. **Aap koshish karte rahiye** means keep trying, respectfully. Contrast **Aap koshish kar rahe hain**, you are trying, with **Aap koshish karte rahiye**, keep trying. One describes a current action; the other encourages its continuation.

**Main khaana kha chuka hoon** means I have already eaten, male speaker; **kha chuki hoon** for a female speaker. *Chukna* presents the action as already completed. In this pattern the construction is intransitive: **Main**, not **maine**, even though *khaana* alone can be transitive. For a neutral negative, say **Maine abhi tak khaana nahin khaaya**, I have not eaten yet. Avoid building a negative by copying the affirmative *chukna* mechanically.

## Describing an event without naming its agent

You may know that a meeting was cancelled without knowing who cancelled it. A passive sentence puts the affected thing in the foreground. Start with **Kisi ne meeting radd ki**, someone cancelled the meeting. To focus on the meeting, say **Meeting radd ki gayi**, the meeting was cancelled. *Meeting* is treated as feminine in this usage, so *ki gayi* is feminine.

A productive pattern is perfective participle plus *jaana*: **Yeh khaana yahaan banaaya jaata hai**, this food is made here. First choose the affected thing, masculine *khaana*. Use *banaaya*, made, then habitual *jaata hai*, is regularly done. For feminine *chai*, **Yeh chai yahaan banaai jaati hai**, this tea is made here. Every agreement choice belongs to the foregrounded thing in this simple construction.

Change time carefully: **Darwaaza khola gaya**, the door was opened; **Darwaaza khola ja raha hai**, the door is being opened; **Darwaaza khola jaayega**, the door will be opened. The opening is not literally travelling. Do not confuse this passive use with **Woh chala gaya**, he left, where *jaana* is part of an active motion expression.

Sometimes a separate intransitive verb is more natural: **Darwaaza khul gaya**, the door opened. Compare **Darwaaza khola gaya**, the door was opened. The first reports a change without necessarily presenting an unnamed agent. The second presents an action done to the door. In everyday speech, choose the version that conveys what matters instead of insisting every English passive needs a word-for-word passive translation.

## Getting someone else to do something

A causative says that someone causes or arranges an action. Compare **Main kapre dhota hoon**, I wash clothes, male speaker, with **Main kapre dhulwaata hoon**, I have clothes washed. In the second case, another person does the washing. The sentence does not identify that person until you add them: **Main dhobi se kapre dhulwaata hoon**, I have the laundry person wash the clothes.

Learn useful verb families as vocabulary, not as a universal spelling trick:

| Event/state | Direct action | Arrange/cause |
| --- | --- | --- |
| banna: to become/be made | banaana: to make | banwaana: to have made |
| khulna: to open | kholna: to open something | khulwaana: to have opened |
| padhna: to read/study | padhaana: to teach | padhwaana: to have someone read/study |
| seekhna: to learn | sikhaana: to teach | sikhwaana: to arrange teaching |

Meanings and available forms differ by verb. **Main darzi se kurta silwa raha hoon** means I am having a tailor make a kurta, male speaker. A female speaker uses *rahi hoon*. **Maine darzi se kurta silwaaya** is I had a kurta made by the tailor. *Ne* marks the arranger in the completed transitive pattern; *kurta* controls masculine agreement.

To describe letting someone act, use the oblique infinitive plus *dena*: **Bachchon ko khelne dijiye**, let the children play. This is not the same structure as the bare-stem compound **kar dijiye**. The *-ne* matters. **Mujhe bolne dijiye** means let me speak; **Mujhe bol dijiye** means tell me. Compare these aloud until the small difference has a clear practical meaning.

## Emphasis follows what you mean to highlight

Particles are short words that guide attention. **Main bhi aaunga**, male speaker, means I will come too. **Main aaj bhi aaunga** means I will come today too. **Main aaj hi aaunga** means I will come today itself, not another day. Move *bhi* or *hi* and the focus changes. A correct string of words with the wrong focus can answer the wrong question.

**Aap hi bataaiye** means you tell me, or you yourself tell me, depending on tone. **Yahi** combines *yeh* and focus: this very one. **Wahi** points to that same one. **Yahaan hi** means right here. In some Hyderabadi households, **yahaan ich** or **idhar ich** provides local emphasis comparable in many contexts to shared *hi*. Do not assume *ich* can be attached to any word without learning the household pattern.

**To** can mark contrast: **Chai to achchhi hai, lekin bahut garam hai**, the tea is good, but it is very hot. It can also introduce a consequence, as in *agar ... to*. The surrounding sentence tells you its job. **Na** at the end may seek agreement or soften a request: **Aap kal aa rahe hain na?**, you're coming tomorrow, aren't you? A sharp tone can make the same particle impatient.

Listen for emphasis in a short exchange. Question: **Kaun aayega?** Who will come? Answer: **Main hi aaunga**, I myself will come. Question: **Kab aaoge?** When will you come? Answer: **Aaj hi aaunga**, I will come today itself. The verb is unchanged; the focus answers a different question. Practise this with one set of people, places, and times before adding new vocabulary.

## Local forms as social knowledge

**Hau** is an affirmative in many Hyderabadi conversations. **Nakko** can decline an offer or express “don't” in a suitable construction. **Kaiku** asks why. **Mereku** and **tereku** may correspond to shared *mujhe/mujhko* and *tumhe/tujhe*, but the second-person form also carries familiarity and household-specific social meaning. These are useful recognition targets; there is no need to force all of them into every sentence.

For example, **Mereku maaloom nahin** means I do not know in a local style. Shared **Mujhe maaloom nahin** is an alternative. **Nakko, shukriya** can decline more food; shared **Nahin, shukriya** works more widely. **Fikr nakko karo** may be a familiar local “don't worry”; respectful shared **Fikr mat kijiye** is safer with an elder until you know the family's norms.

Recognise reductions without making them your spelling system. A relative may say a fast form resembling *aara* or *jaara* where a more separated model has *aa raha* or *jaa raha*. These spellings only approximate speech. First identify meaning and person. Ask **Aap isko dheere keh sakte hain?**, can you say that slowly? Write the family's actual phrase alongside the expanded form, rather than deciding that every speaker must reduce it identically.

Hyderabadi speech also includes variation in agreement and case marking. The course teaches shared-standard patterns explicitly so that you can understand wider Urdu/Hindi, then asks you to observe the family's patterns. Do not “correct” a native relative by quoting the textbook. Equally, do not conclude that one overheard sentence removes all distinctions involving *ne*, gender, or respect. A local pattern needs several real examples and a speaker's explanation.

## Dialogue: getting ready for guests

**Ammi:** Pehle paani bhar lo, phir mez saaf kar dena. — First fill the water, then clean the table.

**Nida:** Hau. Maine chai ke liye doodh bhi le liya hai. — Yes. I have bought milk for the tea too.

**Ammi:** Achchha. Darwaaza khula rehne do. — Good. Let the door stay open.

**Nida:** Theek hai. Bachche baahar khelne lage hain. — All right. The children have started playing outside.

**Ammi:** Unko thodi der khelne do. Mehmaan abhi nahin aaye. — Let them play for a little while. The guests have not arrived yet.

The actions form a practical sequence. *Bhar lo* is familiar preparation for the household; *kar dena* sets a later task; *le liya* presents milk-buying as achieved; *khelne do* permits an activity. Retell the exchange in respectful forms to an older relative helping you, and notice which endings must change.

## Dialogue: arranging a repair

**Aamir:** Pankha phir band ho gaya. — The fan has stopped again.

**Farah:** Kya aapne kisi ko dikhwaaya? — Did you have someone look at it?

**Aamir:** Hau, kal mistri ko bulaaya tha. Unhone kaha ki ek hissa badalna padega. — Yes, I called the repair person yesterday. They said one part would have to be changed.

**Farah:** To woh hissa kab badla jaayega? — So when will that part be replaced?

**Aamir:** Kal. Main waqt ki tasdeeq kar leta hoon. — Tomorrow. I'll confirm the time.

*Band ho gaya* reports a resulting state. *Dikhwaaya* means arranged showing/inspection, not merely “saw.” *Badla jaayega* foregrounds the part that will be changed. Rehearse this exchange with a phone, a tap, and a bag zip, learning any new repair terms your family uses.

## Dialogue: a missed meaning

**Khala:** Yeh cheez idhar ich rakh do. — Put this thing right here.

**Learner:** Maaf kijiye, “idhar ich” ka matlab “yahaan hi” hai? — Excuse me, does “idhar ich” mean “right here”?

**Khala:** Hau, yahaan hi. — Yes, right here.

**Learner:** Achchha, samajh aa gaya. Main yahin rakh deta hoon. — Oh, I understand. I'll put it here.

**Khala:** Aur yeh dabba bachchon ko kholne dena. — And let the children open this box.

**Learner:** Zaroor. Main khud nahin kholunga. — Certainly. I won't open it myself. Male speaker.

The learner asks about meaning without making the local phrase into a joke. The response provides a shared equivalent. This is the model for growing your own household dictionary: a real phrase, a real meaning, a social context, and permission to ask again.

## Translation and diagnosis workshop

Translate the following, then explain which person or noun controls the final ending.

1. Please note the address down. Respectful.
2. I sent the picture. Female or male speaker; feminine object.
3. The children started laughing.
4. Keep speaking slowly. Respectful.
5. I have already eaten. Give both speaker genders.
6. The tea is made here.
7. The door opened.
8. The door was opened.
9. I am having a tailor make a kurta. Female speaker.
10. Let me speak.
11. I will come today itself. Male speaker.
12. I will come too. Female speaker.

**Answers.** (1) **Pata likh lijiye.** *Lijiye* makes the request respectful. (2) **Maine tasveer bhej di.** The feminine object, not the speaker, controls *di*. (3) **Bachche hansne lage.** The plural group controls *lage*. (4) **Aahista bolte rahiye.** This encourages continuing, rather than merely describing current speech. (5) **Main khaana kha chuka/chuki hoon.** Use *main*, not *maine*, in this construction. (6) **Chai yahaan banaai jaati hai.** Feminine *chai* controls the passive forms. (7) **Darwaaza khul gaya.** An intransitive change. (8) **Darwaaza khola gaya.** A passive action. (9) **Main darzi se kurta silwa rahi hoon.** The speaker controls progressive *rahi*. (10) **Mujhe bolne dijiye.** The oblique infinitive marks the permitted action. (11) **Main aaj hi aaunga.** *Hi* focuses today. (12) **Main bhi aaungi.** *Bhi* adds the speaker to others who are coming.

Repair **Maine kha chuka hoon**, **Mujhe bol dijiye** when you mean “let me speak,” and **Chai banaaya jaata hai**. The repairs are **Main kha chuka hoon**, **Mujhe bolne dijiye**, and **Chai banaai jaati hai**. Finally, explain why *banaai li* does not match the intended compound pattern: the first verb should be a stem, so use *bana li*. Explain the repair aloud in English before producing another Urdu sentence.

## Listening and speaking laboratory

Collect twelve short consented household examples containing a second verb. Identify the basic action, then the contribution of the second verb. Do not force an English one-word label if the meaning depends on the situation. If you cannot identify it, write the whole exchange and ask the speaker what difference the shorter version would make. This comparison is more reliable than guessing from an isolated word.

Record a three-minute account of preparing for a visit. Include one task you did for yourself, one task you did for someone else, something that began, something that continued, and something already completed. Then describe one arrangement made through another person. Re-record only after checking whether the listener understood who did what; adding more complex verbs is not progress if responsibility becomes unclear.

For a listening dictation, have a partner choose from *khul gaya*, *khola gaya*, *khol diya*, *kholne diya*, and *khulwaaya*. Write the Roman phrase and its English meaning. Then put it into a complete sentence. The task tests meaningful contrasts, not whether your Roman spelling matches one rigid convention. In the companion, use synthetic audio as rehearsal and compare it against household recordings when available.

Do not count the same recording session twice because it appears under listening and speaking. Allocate its actual minutes to the activity you are doing. Spend some vocabulary review time on older simple words; they are the words that carry most of the complex grammar you are learning.

## Exit performance

Explain a sequence of practical events to someone who did not witness it. The listener should be able to identify what happened by itself, what you did, what you arranged through someone else, and what someone was allowed to do. Then ask the listener to change one detail and respond naturally. Use emphasis to correct one misunderstanding without raising your voice.

On a second occasion, interpret three authentic local phrases with a family member. Give shared equivalents and describe when you would use the local forms. Readiness means you can ask sensible questions about unfamiliar usage, preserve respect, and avoid treating a machine's voice as the authority on your family's speech. Chapter eleven puts these skills into longer calls, visits, complaints, and collaborative conversations.
