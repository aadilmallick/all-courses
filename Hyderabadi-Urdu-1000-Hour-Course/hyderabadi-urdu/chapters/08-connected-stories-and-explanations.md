# 08 · Connecting ideas without losing the listener

**90 hours · B1 development · Prerequisites:** present and past events, **ne/ko/se**, future and subjunctive, and the possession/reference patterns in Chapter 7. Your task is to tell a connected story, explain a decision, identify the person or thing you mean, and report what someone said while keeping clear who did what.

## Two clear sentences are the starting point

**Mere paas ek kitaab hai. Aapko vo kitaab chahiye.** “I have a book. You need that book.” These are easy to understand, but repeating the noun becomes awkward in a longer conversation. A connected version is **Jo kitaab mere paas hai, aapko vo chahiye**, “The book that I have is the one you need.” The first part identifies a book; the second says something about that same book. The repeated relationship is what makes the sentence work.

A **clause** is a group organized around a verb, often expressing an event or state. A complete sentence can contain one clause or several. A **relative clause** helps identify something: the book that I have, the person who called, the place where we met. In Urdu/Hindi, a common pattern begins with a **j** word and resumes with a matching demonstrative such as **vo**, **vahan**, or **tab**. This pair is called **relative–correlative**. Think “the one which…, that one…” as a learning bridge.

Longer is not automatically better. If you lose track of the people or verb endings, return to two short sentences. The goal is to make the listener’s work easier. Good conversational B1/B2 development includes choosing when to connect and when to pause, repeat a name, or ask whether the listener is following.

## Jo…vo: the one who, the thing that

**Jo kal aaya tha, vo mera bhai hai** means “The person who came yesterday is my brother.” **Jo chai mez par hai, vo aapki hai** means “The tea that is on the table is yours.” The **jo** clause introduces the shared person or thing; **vo** resumes it in the main statement. The relative clause can include the noun, as in **jo chai**, which often makes the reference clearer to a learner.

Build “The food Ammi made was very good” in stages. First: **Ammi ne khaana banaya**, Ammi made food. Second: **Khaana bahut achha tha**, the food was very good. Identify the common element, **khaana**. Put it in a relative clause: **Jo khaana Ammi ne banaya tha**. Resume it: **vo bahut achha tha**. The result is **Jo khaana Ammi ne banaya tha, vo bahut achha tha**. The first **tha** places the making before the evaluation; in many contexts **banaya** alone is sufficient.

Notice that **Ammi ne** still follows the Chapter 5 pattern. A relative clause does not cancel the grammar inside it. **Jo kitaab maine padhi, vo achhi thi** means “The book I read was good.” **Padhi** follows feminine **kitaab**. **Jo aadmi aaya, vo mere chacha hain** means “The man who came is my uncle,” with respectful plural **hain** in the identification. Family speech may also use respectful agreement in the arrival clause; keep the intended respect consistent.

Everyday speakers sometimes omit the second **vo** when the reference remains clear. Learn the full pair first, then recognize shorter versions. Do not insert a separate English-style “that” after every noun: the course pattern is a whole construction, not a word-for-word replacement of one English relative pronoun.

## Jis, jisko, jisse, and jiska

**Jo** changes form when its role needs a postposition. The oblique singular base is **jis**; plural is **jin**. Combine it with the relationship required by the clause: **jisko**, whom/to whom; **jisse**, from whom/with whom; **jiska/jiski/jiske**, whose. These are not decorative variants. They show how the identified person or thing participates in the first clause.

**Jis aadmi ko maine dekha, vo yahin rehta hai** means “The man I saw lives right here.” The person is the marked direct object of **dekha**, so use **jis aadmi ko**. The main clause’s **vo** is the subject of **rehta hai**, so it is unmarked. The relative and correlative need not have the same case; they perform different roles in their own clauses.

**Jis se maine baat ki, vo aapki behen theen?** asks “Was the person I spoke with your sister?” The first clause needs **se** because one talks **kisi se**, with someone. The second clause identifies that person. More explicit **Jis aurat se maine baat ki…** means “The woman with whom I spoke…” If the identity is sensitive or unclear, use the person’s name once you know it rather than describing them indefinitely.

**Jiski kitaab yahan hai, usko phone kijiye** means “Call the person whose book is here.” **Jiski** uses **ki** because the possessed noun **kitaab** is feminine. In the second clause the person called is an object with **ko**, giving **usko**. You have now combined two earlier ideas: possessive agreement follows the possession, and postpositions follow each clause’s role. Work them out one at a time.

## Jahan…vahan and jab…tab

**Jahan** means where in a relative connection, and **vahan** resumes that location. **Jahan hum rehte the, vahan ek bada darakht tha** means “Where we used to live, there was a big tree.” A more compact English translation is “There was a big tree where we used to live.” The two languages can organize the same information differently without changing the relationship.

**Jahan aap baithna chahein, vahan baithiye** means “Sit wherever you would like.” The place is not identified in advance, so **chahein** is subjunctive. **Jahan hum kal baithe the, vahan chalte hain** means “Let us go where we sat yesterday,” with an actual past place. The relative word itself does not force one tense or mood; the event’s meaning determines the verb.

**Jab** links a time or occasion; **tab** resumes it. **Jab main ghar aaya/aayi, tab sab so rahe the** means “When I came home, everyone was sleeping.” The arrival is one completed event; sleeping supplies the ongoing background. This is the connected version of the two-sentence exercise from Chapter 5. **Jab main chhota tha/chhoti thi, tab hum yahan rehte the** means “When I was little, we lived here.”

For repeated events, use repeated/habitual forms: **Jab bhi baarish hoti hai, hum ghar mein rehte hain**, “Whenever it rains, we stay at home.” **Bhi** adds the “ever” sense in **jab bhi**. For a future instruction, **Jab aap pahunchein, toh phone kijiye**, “When you arrive, please call,” uses a prospective subjunctive. **Toh** often replaces **tab** in natural conditional/instructional connections. Recognize the relationship rather than insisting every pair has only one acceptable second word.

## Jitna…utna: matching amounts and degrees

**Jitna** and **utna** match an amount or degree: **Jitna waqt mile, utna abhyaas kijiye**, “Practice as much as the time you have allows.” In Urdu-focused family conversation, **practice kijiye** or **mashq kijiye** may be more natural than **abhyaas**. The grammar works with any appropriate vocabulary. Choose the word your relatives actually understand; avoiding native script does not require avoiding ordinary borrowings.

With a feminine noun, use **jitni/utni**: **Jitni chai chahiye, utni lijiye**, “Take as much tea as you want.” With plural countable nouns, use **jitne/utne** where appropriate: **Jitne log aaye, utni kursiyan nahin theen**, “There were not as many chairs as people who came.” In that sentence **jitne log** is masculine plural, while **utni kursiyan** is feminine plural. Each member follows its own noun; they do not need to look identical.

For everyday use, start with **Jitna ho sake**, “as much as possible”; **Jaldi se jaldi**, “as soon as possible”; and **Jitna chahiye, utna le lijiye**, “Take as much as you need.” The full pattern helps you recognize the relationship even when a family speaker leaves a noun unstated.

## Ki: what someone said, thought, or knew

**Ki** introduces a content clause: the message someone said, the thought they had, or the fact they knew. **Ammi ne kaha ki khaana tayyar hai** means “Ammi said that the food is ready.” The first clause tells you the source and speech event; the second gives the content. The **ki** here is not the possessive **ki** in **Ammi ki kitaab**. The spelling is the same, but the surrounding structure reveals the job.

Build “I think you are right.” Begin with **Mujhe lagta hai**, “It seems to me/I think.” Add **ki**. Then add the thought **aap theek keh rahe/rahi hain**, “you are speaking correctly/you are right.” The result is **Mujhe lagta hai ki aap theek keh rahe/rahi hain**. In casual conversation **ki** is sometimes omitted: **Mujhe lagta hai aap theek keh rahe/rahi hain**. The full form is useful when learning because it marks the boundary between the opinion frame and its content.

Do not confuse a relative clause with a content clause. **Jo baat aapne kahi, vo sahi hai** means “The thing you said is correct”: it identifies a particular statement and evaluates it. **Aapne kaha ki main sahi hoon** means “You said that I am right”: it reports the content. One begins with a relationship to a noun; the other introduces a message.

## Report speech without automatically copying English tense rules

English often changes “I am tired” to “She said she was tired.” Urdu/Hindi reporting is more flexible, and the intended time and reference must be clear. You can give direct speech: **Sara ne kaha, “Main thak gayi hoon.”** “Sara said, ‘I am tired.’” Or report the content with a changed pronoun: **Sara ne kaha ki vo thak gayi hai/thi**, “Sara said she is/was tired,” according to whether you present the state as current or past.

Do not switch **main** to **vo** and every verb to past mechanically. Ask three questions: Who originally spoke? Who does each pronoun refer to now? Is the reported situation still current, or are you describing a past state? **Ali ne kaha ki vo kal aayega** commonly reports that Ali said he would come tomorrow, but **kal** can be ambiguous when the reporting day has changed. Use **agle din**, “the next day,” or the named day when needed.

For instructions, **Ammi ne kaha ki darwaza band kar do** can report “Ammi said to close the door,” with the instruction retained. A more explicit alternative is **Ammi ne darwaza band karne ko kaha**, “Ammi asked/told [someone] to close the door.” The infinitive changes to **karne** before **ko**. Include the recipient if it matters: **Ammi ne mujhe darwaza band karne ko kaha**. That prevents a group from wondering who is responsible.

When reporting a question, retain the question relationship: **Unhone poochha ki aap kab aayenge**, “They asked when you will come.” **Poochhna** is to ask a question; **kehna** is to say/tell. **Maine unse poochha** means “I asked them.” The person asked can be marked with **se**. Use the verb and its relationships as a chunk instead of translating English “ask” with one postposition in every situation.

## Kar and karke: one person, a sequence of actions

**Khaana kha kar main nikla/nikli** means “After eating, I left.” **Haath dho kar baithiye** means “Wash your hands and sit down,” respectfully. The connecting form is usually the verb stem plus **kar** or **karke**. The main verb carries the tense and agreement: **nikla/nikli**, left; **baithiye**, please sit. The earlier action is understood from the connection.

Work through “I called Ammi and then went to sleep.” First write **Maine Ammi ko phone kiya. Phir main so gaya/gayi.** To connect the actions, use **Ammi ko phone karke main so gaya/gayi**. The **phone karke** phrase does not itself carry the **ne** perfective pattern; the final **so gaya/gayi** is intransitive and follows the subject. Do not keep **maine** from the first standalone sentence automatically after changing the structure.

The understood actor of a **kar/karke** phrase is normally the same as the main clause’s actor. If you mean “After Ammi cooked, I ate,” avoid a phrase that sounds as though you did both actions. Say **Ammi ne khaana banaya. Uske baad maine khaya**, or **Jab Ammi ne khaana bana liya, tab maine khaya**. Clear separate clauses are preferable to an elegant-looking connection with the wrong actor.

Some combinations describe manner or reason as well as sequence: **Muskura kar unhone jawab diya**, “They answered with a smile”; **Aapse baat karke achha laga**, “It was nice talking to you.” The second contains an experience evaluation with an implicit experiencer, not a simple pair of actions by the same person. Learn such common expressions as complete constructions while using the same-actor guideline for ordinary action sequences.

## Before, after, while, since, and until

Use an oblique infinitive before postpositions: **jaane se pehle**, before going; **aane ke baad**, after coming; **khaane ke liye**, for eating/to eat. **Main nikalne se pehle phone karunga/karungi** means “I will call before leaving.” **Ghar aane ke baad main aaraam karta/karti hoon** means “After coming home I rest.” The **ne** ending in **aane** is part of the oblique infinitive; it is not the separate perfective subject marker **ne**.

**Aate waqt** means while coming/on the way here; **jaate waqt**, while going/on the way away; **khaate waqt**, while eating. These use a participial form plus **waqt**, time. **Aate waqt doodh le aaiye**, “Please bring milk on your way.” Keep the direction clear: **aana** is coming toward the relevant place, **jaana** going away. A remote caller may frame direction around the family home rather than their own physical location, so listen to the shared point of view.

**Se** can mark a starting point: **subah se**, since morning; **Hyderabad se**, from Hyderabad. **Tak** marks an endpoint: **shaam tak**, until/by evening; **ghar tak**, as far as home. **Main subah se yahan hoon** means “I have been here since morning.” **Main shaam tak yahan hoon** means “I am here until evening.” **Main shaam tak kaam khatam karunga/karungi** means “I will finish the work by evening.” The same endpoint marker can translate as “until” with a continuing state and “by” with a completion.

## Contrast, concession, and keeping a turn

**Lekin** and **magar** mean but. **Phir bhi** means even so/still. **Halanki** introduces although: **Halanki baarish ho rahi thi, phir bhi hum gaye**, “Although it was raining, we went anyway.” You need not use both parts every time. **Baarish ho rahi thi, lekin hum phir bhi gaye** is also clear. First say the actual contrast; do not collect connectors merely to sound advanced.

**Ek taraf…doosri taraf…** means on one hand…on the other hand. **Ek taraf kaam zaroori hai; doosri taraf ghar walon ke liye waqt bhi chahiye**, “On one hand work matters; on the other, time for family is also needed.” This gives you a structure for a balanced opinion. Chapter 9 expands discussion and reasons. Here practice following the connection without losing the original point.

Useful turn signals include **Pehle main yeh bata doon**, “First let me explain this”; **Mera matlab yeh hai ki…**, “What I mean is…”; **Ek misaal deta/deti hoon**, “Let me give an example”; and **Toh baat yeh hai…**, “So the point is…”. Use a pause after the frame. A connector helps only if the next clause actually fulfills the expectation it creates.

## Three connected conversations

### Finding the right relative

**Learner:** Jo kal aaye the, vo aapke chacha hain? — Is the person who came yesterday your paternal uncle?

**Cousin:** Nahin, vo mere mamu hain. Chacha vo hain jo darwaze ke paas baithe hain. — No, that is my maternal uncle. My paternal uncle is the one sitting near the door.

**Learner:** Achha. Jinki kitaab mere paas hai, vohi? — Oh. The same person whose book I have?

**Cousin:** Hau, vohi. Unko kitaab kal chahiye. — Yes, that very person. He needs the book tomorrow.

**Learner:** Main ghar jaane se pehle de doonga/doongi. — I will give it back before going home.

The dialogue solves an identification problem. **Jinki** uses a respectful/plural relative base and feminine possession **kitaab**. **Vohi** combines **vo** with emphasis **hi**, “that very one.” Avoid using a kinship label before you know which side of the family the person belongs to.

### Retelling a small delay

**Relative:** Tum der se kaiku aaye/aayi? — Why did you arrive late? Local familiar question.

**Learner:** Jab main nikla/nikli, tab baarish ho rahi thi. Jis raaste se aaya/aayi, vahan traffic tha. — When I left, it was raining. There was traffic on the route I took.

**Relative:** Phone kar dete/deti. — You could have called.

**Learner:** Ji, mujhe pehle phone karna chahiye tha. Pahunch kar message bheja. — Yes, I should have called earlier. After arriving, I sent a message.

**Relative:** Theek hai, ab baitho. — All right, sit now.

The explanation supplies sequence and place, then acknowledges a missed action. A long story should not hide the practical point. **Kaiku** can be replaced with shared **kyun** without changing the explanation’s grammar.

### Reporting a plan accurately

**Learner:** Ammi ne kaha ki vo jumme ko aayengi. — Ammi said she will come on Friday.

**Sibling:** Subah ya shaam? — Morning or evening?

**Learner:** Unhone kaha ki shaam tak pahunch jayengi. Aane se pehle phone karengi. — She said she will arrive by evening. She will call before coming.

**Sibling:** Toh main khaana bana loon? — Then shall I make the food?

**Learner:** Hau, lekin pehle unse poochh lete hain ki unko kya chahiye. — Yes, but let us first ask her what she would like.

Notice that **shaam tak** means by evening because the event is arrival. The report names the day instead of relying on **kal**. The final question avoids inventing a food preference on someone else’s behalf.

## Exercises and answers

### A. Connect two ideas

1. **Mere paas chai hai. Aapko vo chai chahiye.**
2. **Main ghar aaya/aayi. Sab so rahe the.**
3. **Hum yahan rehte the. Yahan ek dukaan thi.**
4. **Maine ek aadmi se baat ki. Vo mere chacha hain.**
5. **Main haath dhoonga/dhoongi. Phir khaana khaunga/khaungi.**
6. **Ammi ne kaha: “Khaana tayyar hai.”** Report the message.

**Possible answers:** 1. **Jo chai mere paas hai, aapko vo chahiye.** 2. **Jab main ghar aaya/aayi, tab sab so rahe the.** 3. **Jahan hum rehte the, vahan ek dukaan thi.** 4. **Jis aadmi se maine baat ki, vo mere chacha hain.** 5. **Main haath dho kar khaana khaunga/khaungi.** 6. **Ammi ne kaha ki khaana tayyar hai.** Each connection preserves the relationship; alternative orders may be natural if the reference stays clear.

### B. Choose the relationship

Fill the blank: **___ aap pahunchein, phone kijiye** for “When you arrive”; **Jis aadmi ___ maine dekha…**; **Aane ___ baad**; **Subah ___ ab ___**, “from morning until now”; **Mujhe lagta hai ___ vo ghar par hain**; and **___ kitaab yahan hai, usko phone kijiye**, “Call the person whose book is here.”

**Answers:** **Jab**; **ko**, because the identified person is the direct object seen; **ke**, in **aane ke baad**; **se…tak**; **ki**, the content-clause marker; **jiski**, because **kitaab** is feminine. For the first, **agar** would change “when” into “if.” The difference can matter in a plan.

### C. Repair ambiguous reporting

On Monday Ali said **Main kal aaunga**, “I will come tomorrow.” On Wednesday you report his message. Why is **Ali ne kaha ki vo kal aayega** risky? Because **kal** may now be heard as Thursday rather than the Tuesday Ali meant. Say **Ali ne kaha tha ki vo mangal ko aayega**, or **…agle din aayega**, with enough context to identify the reference day. Named days prevent a grammar exercise from becoming a real scheduling mistake.

### D. Translation practice

Translate: “The book I read was good”; “Call before leaving”; “I have been here since morning”; “I will finish by evening”; “They asked when you will come”; “Although it was raining, we went”; “Take as much tea as you want”; and “After calling Ammi, I went to sleep.”

**Possible answers:** **Jo kitaab maine padhi, vo achhi thi.** **Nikalne se pehle phone kijiye.** **Main subah se yahan hoon.** **Main shaam tak kaam khatam karunga/karungi.** **Unhone poochha ki aap kab aayenge/aayengi.** **Halanki baarish ho rahi thi, phir bhi hum gaye.** **Jitni chai chahiye, utni lijiye.** **Ammi ko phone karke main so gaya/gayi.** In the last sentence **main**, not **maine**, belongs with the final intransitive sleep expression.

## Listening, speaking, and the 90-hour plan

Listen to a two-minute family story with its transcript hidden. First draw five boxes for events and use arrows for sequence; no artistic drawing is needed. On a second pass mark background, reason, contrast, and reported information with short English labels. Then type four Roman sentences you heard and translate them. Finally compare the transcript. A connector you missed may explain why your event order was wrong. A different Roman spelling does not automatically mean you misheard the spoken relationship.

For speaking, choose an everyday story with five events, one earlier background fact, one person to identify, one quoted message, and one consequence. Tell it in three minutes. A partner interrupts with **Kaun?**, **Kab?**, and **Kisne kaha?** Your job is to clarify the reference and return to the story. Repeat with fewer connectors if the first version became tangled. Clear pauses and a well-placed name can be more effective than one very long sentence.

For the project, ask permission to collect one ordinary family story. Record who said each piece of information, what is certain, and what is remembered approximately. Retell it to another willing relative without exposing private details. Ask whether the sequence and references were clear. Do not claim exact words if you paraphrased. Reporting responsibly is both a language skill and a way of honoring a family’s stories.

At exit, relay a practical message with all critical details intact; identify an unfamiliar person or object using a relative clause; tell a three-minute story with clear event order and background; and explain one reason and one contrast. A partner should recover the same sequence and participants without seeing your notes. This is evidence of course progress rather than a formal CEFR award. The next stage uses these connections to sustain opinions, respond to disagreement, and discuss more abstract family and community topics.
