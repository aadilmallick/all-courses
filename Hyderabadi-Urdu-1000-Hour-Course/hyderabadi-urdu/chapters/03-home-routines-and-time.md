# 03. Home, routines, and what is happening now

**80 hours · Prerequisites: chapters 1-2 · Goal: locate people and things, describe ordinary routines, and distinguish a habit from an action in progress.**

## Two messages that English also keeps apart

Your aunt asks whether you drink tea. You answer “I drink tea,” describing a habit. A minute later someone asks why your hands are occupied. You answer “I am drinking tea,” describing the current activity. Both involve tea and drinking; the difference is how you view the action. Urdu/Hindi makes that contrast with two visibly different constructions: **Main chai peeta hoon** and **Main chai pee raha hoon** for a male speaker. A female speaker says **Main chai peeti hoon** and **Main chai pee rahi hoon**.

Before we build these forms, we need to place the activity somewhere and sometime. Families talk about who is at home, what is in the kitchen, what time someone gets up, and whether somebody is coming now. This chapter therefore connects location phrases, a few changes to nouns, time expressions, and present-tense verbs. Each new part helps you describe a day your listener can actually picture.

Keep a simple drawing of a home nearby. It can be fictional. Label **ghar**, “home/house”; **kamra**, “room”; **rasoi**, “kitchen,” or **kitchen**, which many families use; **darwaaza**, “door”; **mez**, “table”; **kursi**, “chair”; and **baathroom**, “bathroom.” Use the words your relatives recognize. A borrowed English word is not a learning failure when it is normal in the conversation.

## Location words come after their nouns

English uses “in the room” and “on the table.” Urdu/Hindi uses **kamre mein** and **mez par**. **Mein** and **par** follow the noun; for that reason they are called postpositions. You have already used **ghar par**, “at home.” The new idea is to make the phrase yourself instead of treating each location as a memorized exception.

| Marker or phrase | Central use | Example | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| mein | inside/in | kamre mein | in the room |
| par | on/at | mez par | on the table |
| se | from/with/by | ghar se | from home |
| tak | until/as far as | ghar tak | up to the house |
| ke paas | near; with for possession | darwaaze ke paas | near the door |
| ke saamne | in front of | ghar ke saamne | in front of the house |
| ke peeche | behind | ghar ke peeche | behind the house |
| ke andar | inside | kamre ke andar | inside the room |

Do not assign one English word to **se** and use it everywhere. It can mark a source, an instrument, a comparison, or another relationship. Begin with **ghar se**, “from home,” and **phone se**, “by phone/with a phone.” Context determines the intended relationship. Later chapters develop the remaining uses as you need them.

Build “The phone is on the table” from meaning. Identify the thing: **phone**. Identify the location: **mez par**. Add the linking form: **Phone mez par hai**. Change the location to “inside the room”: **Phone kamre mein hai**. Change the thing to plural books: **Kitaaben kamre mein hain**. Location stays a phrase; number still controls **hai/hain**.

## Why kamra becomes kamre

In chapter 2, **kamra** was the direct singular form. Before a postposition, many nouns and their matching words use another form, called the oblique. The term means “the form used in this grammatical environment”; it does not mean that the object is physically tilted. For a common masculine **-a** noun, singular **-a** becomes **-e**: **kamra → kamre mein**, **darwaaza → darwaaze par**, **ladka → ladke ko**.

The adjective and possessive change with the phrase: **mera naya kamra**, “my new room,” but **mere naye kamre mein**, “in my new room.” Build this in layers. Start **kamra**; add **mein**, requiring **kamre**; add the matching **naya**, requiring **naye**; add **mera**, requiring **mere**. Then place it in a sentence: **Phone mere naye kamre mein hai**. The phone is in my new room.

The form **kamre** can also be a direct plural. Compare **Yeh kamre bade hain**, “These rooms are big,” with **Phone kamre mein hai**, “The phone is in the room.” The following marker and the rest of the sentence identify the grammatical job. A form can serve more than one function; do not force each spelling to have only one English label.

Many singular nouns do not visibly change before a postposition: **ghar mein**, **kursi par**, **kitaab mein**, **bhai se**. They are still in the relevant grammatical environment. “Oblique” names the pattern even when no sound changes. In the plural, common forms include **kamron mein**, “in the rooms”; **kursiyon par**, “on the chairs”; **kitaabon mein**, “in the books”; **bhaiyon se**, “from/with brothers.” Learn a few useful plural phrases now; expand systematically with practice.

## Pronouns also have location and relationship forms

**Yeh** becomes **is** before a marker for a nearby singular referent: **is kamre mein**, “in this room,” or **is mein**, “in this/inside it.” **Woh** becomes **us** for a more distant or established singular referent: **us ghar mein**, “in that house”; **us se**, “from/with that person.” For plural reference, **in** and **un** occur: **in kamron mein**, “in these rooms”; **un logon se**, “with/from those people.”

This also explains familiar possessives: **us ka phone** combines an oblique pronoun with **ka**. With **aap**, use **aap ko**, **aap se**, **aap ke paas**. With **main**, forms include **mujhe** or **mujh ko**, “to me,” **mujh se**, “from/with me,” and **mere paas**, “with me/in my possession.” These are high-value forms, so learn complete phrases instead of mechanically adding markers to **main**.

In some Hyderabadi households, **mereku** functions in places where shared Urdu/Hindi uses **mujhe**, and **tereku** where a familiar form such as **tumhein/tujhe** might occur. These forms are local and relationship-sensitive. **Tereku** is not a respectful substitute for **aap ko**. Recognize it in cousins' speech; use **aap ko** with elders unless the household clearly guides you otherwise. The course keeps **mereku** as a Roman spelling; relatives may write **merku**, **mere ko**, or another version.

## Ko: recipient, experiencer, and selected object

**Ko** often marks the person who receives something: **Ammi ko chai**, “tea for/to Mother.” A complete action will come shortly: **Main Ammi ko chai deta hoon**, “I give Mother tea,” for a male speaker. It can also mark an experiencer, the person who feels or needs something: **Mujhe bhook lagi hai**, “I am hungry”; **mujhe** contains the “to me” relationship. Learn this hunger phrase whole for now.

**Ko** also marks many human or specific objects: **Main Aamir ko jaanta hoon**, “I know Aamir,” for a male speaker. Do not add **ko** after every English object. **Main chai peeta hoon** is the normal beginner model for “I drink tea,” without **chai ko**. The choice depends on animacy, specificity, and the verb. At this stage, learn **person ko** in tested examples, and keep ordinary food/drink objects unmarked unless a model calls for something else.

Physical possession often uses **ke paas**: **Mere paas phone hai**, “I have a phone.” Literally the phone is “near/with me,” but natural English says “I have.” **Mere do bhai hain** describes a family relationship and normally does not need **mere paas**. Brothers are not possessions sitting with you. Match the construction to the kind of relationship you mean.

## Verbs name actions; the infinitive is a dictionary form

A verb describes an action or state. Dictionary entries often end in **-na**: **karna**, “to do”; **peena**, “to drink”; **khana**, “to eat”; **jaana**, “to go”; **aana**, “to come”; **rehna**, “to live/stay”; **bolna**, “to speak”; **samajhna**, “to understand”; **seekhna**, “to learn”; **sona**, “to sleep”; **uthna**, “to get up.” The **-na** form is called an infinitive. It does not by itself mean “I do” or “she does.”

For the regular patterns here, remove **-na** to find a working stem: **kar**, **pee**, **kha**, **jaa**, **aa**, **reh**, **bol**, **samajh**, **seekh**, **so**, **uth**. The spelling of long vowels varies in everyday messages; our expanded stems make the construction easier to see. Add endings according to the meaning you need. Be careful with **khana**: it can be the infinitive “to eat” or the masculine noun “food.” Sentence structure tells you which.

## Habits and characteristic activities

For a regular habit, combine the stem with **-ta**, **-ti**, or **-te**, then a linking form. A male speaker says **Main ghar par kaam karta hoon**, “I work at home.” A female speaker says **Main ghar par kaam karti hoon**. **Kaam karna** is literally “work do,” an ordinary noun-plus-verb expression. Keep **kaam** before **karna**; do not search for an English-style single word for every activity.

| Subject | Example | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| main, male | Main chai peeta hoon. | I drink tea. |
| main, female | Main chai peeti hoon. | I drink tea. |
| tum, male | Tum chai peete ho. | You drink tea, familiar. |
| tum, female | Tum chai peeti ho. | You drink tea, familiar. |
| aap, male | Aap chai peete hain. | You drink tea, respectful. |
| aap, female | Aap chai peeti hain. | You drink tea, respectful. |
| woh, male singular | Woh chai peeta hai. | He drinks tea. |
| woh, female singular | Woh chai peeti hai. | She drinks tea. |

For a male or mixed group **hum**, use **Hum chai peete hain**; for an all-female group, **Hum chai peeti hain** is the basic pattern. Honorific reference uses the respectful forms. Gender agreement is not something the English pronoun “I” reveals, so English-to-Urdu exercises should accept an explicitly appropriate male or female answer rather than treating one as universally correct.

Add frequency words: **roz**, “every day”; **aksar**, “often”; **kabhi kabhi**, “sometimes”; **hamesha**, “always”; **aam taur par**, “usually.” **Main roz Urdu seekhta hoon** means “I learn/study Urdu every day,” for a male speaker. **Main kabhi kabhi chai peeti hoon** means “I sometimes drink tea,” for a female speaker. Place the frequency expression before the activity phrase for a clear beginner model.

## What is happening now

For an action unfolding now, use the stem plus **raha/rahi/rahe** and the linking form: **Main chai pee raha hoon**, male; **Main chai pee rahi hoon**, female. **Woh khana kha raha hai** means “He is eating food.” **Woh khana kha rahi hai** means “She is eating food.” The two appearances of **khana/kha** do different jobs: food is the object; eating is the action.

For respectful male address, **Aap kya kar rahe hain?**, “What are you doing?” For respectful female address, **Aap kya kar rahi hain?** For familiar male address, **Tum kya kar rahe ho?** For familiar female address, **Tum kya kar rahi ho?** Compare carefully: the ongoing element reflects gender/number, while the final linking form also reflects the pronoun and level of address.

**Abhi** means “now/right now” in these examples. **Ammi abhi chai bana rahi hain**, “Mother is making tea now.” **Banaana** means “to make”; the working stem is **banaa**. **Abba abhi aaraam kar rahe hain**, “Father is resting now.” **Aaraam karna** is another noun-plus-do expression. The respectful endings treat one parent politely; the English translation stays singular.

Do not put **-ta** and **raha** together for our ordinary present progressive: **Main chai peeta raha hoon** is not the intended beginner “I am drinking tea.” Choose the view first. A repeated habit uses **peeta/peeti hoon**; an action in progress uses **pee raha/rahi hoon**. Other combinations exist for other meanings, but adding more pieces is not automatically more accurate.

## Negation and questions with actions

Use **nahin** before the main action sequence: **Main chai nahin peeta hoon**, “I do not drink tea,” male, or **Main chai nahin peeti hoon**, female. Many speakers omit the final linking form in a negative habitual: **Main chai nahin peeta**. Both are recognizable. Keep the fuller model while learning so that person and agreement remain visible.

For an ongoing negative, say **Main abhi kaam nahin kar raha hoon**, male, or **... kar rahi hoon**, female: “I am not working right now.” A yes/no question can start with **kya**: **Kya aap roz chai peete hain?** A content question uses the relevant unknown: **Aap kahaan rehte hain?**, “Where do you live?” addressed respectfully to a male, or **Aap kahaan rehti hain?** to a female.

Hyderabadi **kaiku** asks “why” in many households: **Aap kaiku hans rahe hain?**, “Why are you laughing?” to a male respectfully. Shared **kyun** works across a wider range of settings: **Aap kyun hans rahe hain?** Questions about motives can sound challenging with abrupt intonation. Begin warmly, and do not use a local word as permission to interrogate an elder.

## Time, numbers, and the shape of a day

Start numbers with **ek, do, teen, chaar, paanch, chhe, saat, aath, nau, das** for one through ten. Add **gyaarah**, eleven; **baarah**, twelve; **bees**, twenty; **tees**, thirty; **chaalees**, forty; **pachaas**, fifty; **sau**, hundred. Hindi-Urdu numbers are not completely predictable by combining tens and ones. Learn your daily times and practical quantities first, then expand with the vocabulary bank.

For a time, use **baje**: **Main saat baje uthta hoon**, “I get up at seven,” male. **Main raat ko das baje sota hoon**, “I sleep/go to bed at ten at night.” Add **subah**, “morning”; **dopahar**, “afternoon/noon period”; **shaam**, “evening”; **raat**, “night,” to disambiguate. **Subah saat baje** is seven in the morning; **shaam saat baje** is seven in the evening.

**Saadhe saat** means half past seven; **sawa saat**, quarter past seven; **paune aath**, quarter to eight. These expressions are useful listening targets but need not delay a conversation: **saat baje tees minute**, seven thirty, is transparent. **Kal** can mean yesterday or tomorrow. Present habits, future forms, past forms, or extra context resolve it. If uncertain, ask whether the person means the coming day or the previous day rather than pretending the ambiguity is yours alone.

## Three conversations across an ordinary day

### Finding someone

| Roman Urdu/Hindi | English |
| --- | --- |
| Ammi kahaan hain? | Where is Mother? |
| Woh kitchen mein hain. | She is in the kitchen. |
| Woh kya kar rahi hain? | What is she doing? |
| Woh chai bana rahi hain. | She is making tea. |
| Aur Abba? | And Father? |
| Woh kamre mein aaraam kar rahe hain. | He is resting in the room. |

The person remains understood across turns. Repeating **woh** in every sentence is useful for learning; in natural conversation it can be omitted when reference is clear. Keep it when two people could be confused. Listening requires tracking the conversation, not only recognizing isolated words.

### Comparing routines

| Roman Urdu/Hindi | English |
| --- | --- |
| Aap subah kitne baje uthte hain? | What time do you get up in the morning? |
| Main chhe baje uthta hoon. Aur aap? | I get up at six. And you? |
| Main saat baje uthti hoon. | I get up at seven. |
| Kya aap roz chai peeti hain? | Do you drink tea every day? |
| Nahin, main aksar coffee peeti hoon. | No, I often drink coffee. |
| Main kabhi kabhi coffee peeta hoon. | I sometimes drink coffee. |

This exchange has a male and female speaker. The switches in **uthte/uthta/uthti** and **peeti/peeta** follow the person addressed or speaking, not the drink. Coffee and tea do not control agreement in these present-tense subject-agreement models.

### A familiar local exchange

| Roman Urdu/Hindi | English |
| --- | --- |
| Tum abhi kya kar rahe ho? | What are you doing now, familiar male? |
| Main Urdu seekh raha hoon. | I am learning Urdu. |
| Kaiku? | Why? |
| Mere ghar waale Urdu bolte hain. | My family members speak Urdu. |
| Achchha. Mereku bhi practice chahiye. | Good. I need practice too, local familiar wording. |
| Hau, hum saath practice karte hain. | Yes, we practice together. |

**Chahiye**, “is needed,” is treated fully next chapter. The local line is a model of recognition, not a requirement to replace **mujhe**. Shared **Mujhe bhi practice chahiye** expresses the same need. **Saath**, “together,” makes the learning goal social and concrete.

## Exercises and explanatory key

Translate: 1. The book is on the table. 2. I live in Hyderabad, male speaker. 3. I am learning Urdu, female speaker. 4. Mother is resting now. 5. The phone is in my new room. 6. I have a phone. 7. Why are you laughing, respectful male? 8. I get up at seven every day, female speaker.

Answers: 1. **Kitaab mez par hai**. 2. **Main Hyderabad mein rehta hoon**. 3. **Main Urdu seekh rahi hoon**. 4. **Ammi abhi aaraam kar rahi hain**. 5. **Phone mere naye kamre mein hai**; the location marker creates the oblique phrase. 6. **Mere paas phone hai**. 7. **Aap kyun hans rahe hain?**; **kaiku** is a local alternative when appropriate. 8. **Main roz saat baje uthti hoon**. Equivalent orderings are possible when the meaning stays clear.

Explain the contrasts: 9. **Main chai peeti hoon / Main chai pee rahi hoon**. 10. **Meri kitaab / meri kitaab mein**. 11. **Yeh kamre hain / woh kamre mein hai**. 12. **Main do bhai hoon / mere do bhai hain**.

Answers: 9. Habit versus current activity, with a female subject in both. 10. “My book / in my book”; the feminine singular phrase has no visible change here. 11. “These are rooms / he or she is in the room”; **kamre** serves different grammatical jobs. 12. The first incorrectly says “I am two brothers”; the second means “I have two brothers.” Replacing English “have” requires choosing the relationship, not inserting a single universal verb.

For dictation, have a partner contrast **roz**, **abhi**, **mein**, **par**, **raha**, and **rahi** in whole sentences. Type both the Roman sentence and the English meaning. Score the location and time view before spelling. For speaking, describe a fictional household picture for ninety seconds, then answer three unscripted questions about what people are doing.
